# Multivariate Gaussian 

A Gaussian process is built entirely out of multivariate Gaussians, so that is where we start. The multivariate Gaussian extends the familiar bell curve from a single number to a vector $\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^d$ of random variables that vary together. It is fully described by two things: a mean vector $\boldsymbol\mu$, which says where the distribution is centred, and a covariance matrix $\Sigma$, which says how much each component spreads and how strongly each pair of components moves together. Its density is

$$
\large p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\,\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right).
$$

We can take a sample from multi-variate gaussian and partition the vector, mean and covariance into two. 
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x_a\\\mathbf x_b\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\!\left(
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu_a\\\boldsymbol\mu_b\end{pmatrix},\;
\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{aa}&\Sigma_{ab}\\\Sigma_{ba}&\Sigma_{bb}\end{pmatrix}\right).
$$

### Marginalisation

To get $p(\mathbf x_a)$ you simply read off the relevant blocks — integrating out $\mathbf x_b$ does nothing more than drop its rows and columns:

$$\mathbf x_a \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_a,\; \Sigma_{aa}).$$


### Conditioning

Given an **observed** value of $\mathbf x_b$, the conditional $p(\mathbf x_a\mid \mathbf x_b)$ is Gaussian with

$$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\mu_{a\mid b} = \boldsymbol\mu_a + \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,(\mathbf x_b - \boldsymbol\mu_b)\;}$$

$$\boxed{\;\Sigma_{a\mid b} = \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ba}\;}$$

The derivation is in [Appendix A.1](#a1-derivation-of-the-conditional-gaussian).

## Appendix

### A.1 Derivation of the conditional Gaussian

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

$$
\bold{x} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_a\\\mathbf x_b\end{bmatrix} 
\hspace{3em}
\bold{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf \mu_a\\\mathbf \mu_b\end{bmatrix} 
\hspace{3em}
\bold{y} = \bold{x}-\bold{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf y_a\\\mathbf y_b\end{bmatrix}  = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x_a-\mu_a\\\mathbf x_b-\mu_b\end{bmatrix}  
$$

**Step 1: Only the exponent matters.** By the definition of conditional probability,

$$
p(\mathbf x_a \mid \mathbf x_b) = \frac{p(\mathbf x_a, \mathbf x_b)}{p(\mathbf x_b)}.
$$

$\mathbf x_b$ is observed, so it is a fixed number. The denominator $p(\mathbf x_b)$ and the normalising constant of the joint do not depend on $\mathbf x_a$, so as a function of $\mathbf x_a$

$$
p(\mathbf x_a \mid \mathbf x_b) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf y^\top \Sigma^{-1}\, \mathbf y\right).
$$

All we have to do is work out what this exponent looks like as a function of $\mathbf x_a$.

**Step 2: Partition the inverse covariance.** Write $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$ (the *precision matrix*) and split it the same way as $\Sigma$:

$$
\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{aa}&\Lambda_{ab}\\\Lambda_{ba}&\Lambda_{bb}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\Sigma$ is symmetric, so $\Lambda$ is symmetric too, which means $\Lambda_{aa}^\top = \Lambda_{aa}$, $\Lambda_{bb}^\top = \Lambda_{bb}$ and $\Lambda_{ba} = \Lambda_{ab}^\top$. Note that $\Lambda_{aa}$ is **not** $\Sigma_{aa}^{-1}$; we will find what it is in Step 5.

**Step 3: Expand the quadratic form.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf y^\top \Lambda\, \mathbf y
&= \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf y_a^\top & \mathbf y_b^\top\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{aa}&\Lambda_{ab}\\\Lambda_{ba}&\Lambda_{bb}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf y_a\\\mathbf y_b\end{bmatrix} \\[4pt]
&= \mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf y_a
 + \mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{ab}\, \mathbf y_b
 + \mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{ba}\, \mathbf y_a
 + \mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{bb}\, \mathbf y_b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two middle terms are scalars, and a scalar equals its own transpose: $\mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{ba}\, \mathbf y_a = (\mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{ba}\, \mathbf y_a)^\top = \mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{ab}\, \mathbf y_b$. So they are equal and

$$
\mathbf y^\top \Lambda\, \mathbf y
= \underbrace{\mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf y_a}_{\text{quadratic in } \mathbf y_a}
+ \underbrace{2\,\mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{ab}\, \mathbf y_b}_{\text{linear in } \mathbf y_a}
+ \underbrace{\mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{bb}\, \mathbf y_b}_{\text{constant}} .
$$

**Step 4: Complete the square in $\mathbf y_a$.** For any vector $\mathbf m$,

$$
(\mathbf y_a - \mathbf m)^\top \Lambda_{aa} (\mathbf y_a - \mathbf m)
= \mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf y_a - 2\,\mathbf y_a^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf m + \mathbf m^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf m .
$$

The quadratic term already matches Step 3. To make the linear terms match we need $-\Lambda_{aa}\,\mathbf m = \Lambda_{ab}\,\mathbf y_b$, i.e.

$$
\mathbf m = -\Lambda_{aa}^{-1} \Lambda_{ab}\, \mathbf y_b .
$$

With this choice of $\mathbf m$,

$$
\mathbf y^\top \Lambda\, \mathbf y
= (\mathbf y_a - \mathbf m)^\top \Lambda_{aa} (\mathbf y_a - \mathbf m)
+ \underbrace{\mathbf y_b^\top \Lambda_{bb}\, \mathbf y_b - \mathbf m^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \mathbf m}_{\text{does not depend on } \mathbf x_a} .
$$

The last two terms only involve $\mathbf x_b$, so they become a constant factor that is absorbed into the proportionality. Substituting $\mathbf y_a = \mathbf x_a - \boldsymbol\mu_a$:

$$
p(\mathbf x_a \mid \mathbf x_b) \;\propto\;
\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big(\mathbf x_a - (\boldsymbol\mu_a + \mathbf m)\big)^\top \Lambda_{aa}\, \big(\mathbf x_a - (\boldsymbol\mu_a + \mathbf m)\big)\right).
$$

Comparing with the Gaussian density at the top of the page, this is exactly the exponent of a Gaussian in $\mathbf x_a$ with mean $\boldsymbol\mu_a + \mathbf m$ and inverse covariance $\Lambda_{aa}$. Since a density must integrate to one, the normalising constant is forced, and

$$
\boldsymbol\mu_{a\mid b} = \boldsymbol\mu_a - \Lambda_{aa}^{-1} \Lambda_{ab}\,(\mathbf x_b - \boldsymbol\mu_b),
\hspace{3em}
\Sigma_{a\mid b} = \Lambda_{aa}^{-1} .
$$

**Step 5: Express $\Lambda$ in terms of $\Sigma$.** The result is correct but written with blocks of $\Lambda$. To get rid of them we need the blocks of $\Sigma^{-1}$, and the Schur complement identity (derived in [Appendix A.2](#a2-schur-complement-and-the-inverse-of-a-block-matrix)) gives them directly. Define the *Schur complement* of $\Sigma_{bb}$ in $\Sigma$:

$$
S = \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ba}.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{aa}&\Lambda_{ab}\\\Lambda_{ba}&\Lambda_{bb}\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{aa}&\Sigma_{ab}\\\Sigma_{ba}&\Sigma_{bb}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
= \begin{bmatrix}
S^{-1} & -S^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1} \\[4pt]
-\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ba}\,S^{-1} & \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} + \Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ba}\,S^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

We only need the top row. Reading off the two blocks:

$$
\Lambda_{aa} = S^{-1},
\hspace{3em}
\Lambda_{ab} = -S^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}.
$$

**Step 6: Substitute back.** The covariance from Step 4 is

$$
\Sigma_{a\mid b} = \Lambda_{aa}^{-1} = S = \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ba}.
$$

For the mean we need $-\Lambda_{aa}^{-1}\Lambda_{ab}$, where the $S$ and $S^{-1}$ cancel:

$$
-\Lambda_{aa}^{-1}\Lambda_{ab} = -S\,\big(-S^{-1}\,\Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\big) = \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1},
$$

so

$$
\boldsymbol\mu_{a\mid b} = \boldsymbol\mu_a + \Sigma_{ab}\,\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\,(\mathbf x_b - \boldsymbol\mu_b).
$$

These are the two boxed results from the [Conditioning](#conditioning) section. Notice that the conditional mean is linear in the observed $\mathbf x_b$, while the conditional covariance does not depend on the value of $\mathbf x_b$ at all.

</details>

### A.2 Schur complement and the inverse of a block matrix

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Take a square matrix split into four blocks, with $A$ and $D$ square and $D$ invertible:

$$
M = \begin{bmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The *Schur complement* of $D$ in $M$ is

$$
S = A - B\,D^{-1}C .
$$

The claim is that, whenever $S$ is invertible,

$$
\boxed{\;
M^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}
S^{-1} & -S^{-1}B\,D^{-1} \\[4pt]
-D^{-1}C\,S^{-1} & D^{-1} + D^{-1}C\,S^{-1}B\,D^{-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\;}
$$

The idea is block Gaussian elimination: knock out the off-diagonal blocks $B$ and $C$ so that $M$ becomes block diagonal, which is trivial to invert.

**Step 1: Eliminate $B$.** Subtract $B\,D^{-1}$ times the bottom block row from the top block row. As a matrix product this is a multiplication on the left:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}I & -B\,D^{-1}\\0&I\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}A - B\,D^{-1}C & B - B\,D^{-1}D\\C&D\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}S&0\\C&D\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This is where the Schur complement comes from: it is what is left of $A$ after $B$ has been eliminated.

**Step 2: Eliminate $C$.** Subtract the right block column times $D^{-1}C$ from the left block column, which is a multiplication on the right:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}S&0\\C&D\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\-D^{-1}C&I\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}S & 0\\C - D\,D^{-1}C&D\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}S&0\\0&D\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 3: Combine.** Name the two elimination matrices

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix}I & -B\,D^{-1}\\0&I\end{bmatrix},
\hspace{3em}
R = \begin{bmatrix}I&0\\-D^{-1}C&I\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Steps 1 and 2 together say

$$
L\,M\,R = \begin{bmatrix}S&0\\0&D\end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 4: Invert both sides.** $L$ and $R$ are triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal, so they are always invertible. Using $(L\,M\,R)^{-1} = R^{-1}M^{-1}L^{-1}$ and the fact that a block-diagonal matrix is inverted block by block:

$$
R^{-1}M^{-1}L^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&0\\0&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
M^{-1} = R \begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&0\\0&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix} L .
$$

**Step 5: Multiply out.** First the left two factors:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\-D^{-1}C&I\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&0\\0&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&0\\-D^{-1}C\,S^{-1}&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix},
$$

then multiply by $L$:

$$
M^{-1} =
\begin{bmatrix}S^{-1}&0\\-D^{-1}C\,S^{-1}&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}I & -B\,D^{-1}\\0&I\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
S^{-1} & -S^{-1}B\,D^{-1} \\[4pt]
-D^{-1}C\,S^{-1} & D^{-1} + D^{-1}C\,S^{-1}B\,D^{-1}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

which is the claimed identity.

**Applying it to $\Sigma$.** Set $A = \Sigma_{aa}$, $B = \Sigma_{ab}$, $C = \Sigma_{ba}$ and $D = \Sigma_{bb}$ to get the form used in [A.1](#a1-derivation-of-the-conditional-gaussian). Both invertibility assumptions hold for a covariance matrix: $\Sigma$ is positive definite, so its diagonal block $\Sigma_{bb}$ is positive definite and therefore invertible. Taking determinants of $L\,M\,R$ (with $|L| = |R| = 1$) gives $|\Sigma| = |S|\,|\Sigma_{bb}|$, and since $|\Sigma| \neq 0$ we get $|S| \neq 0$, so $S$ is invertible too.

</details>
