## Background

Suppose a model fails on one input in a thousand. To estimate that failure rate with ordinary
**Monte Carlo sampling**---approximating an expectation with an average of random evaluations---we
draw typical inputs and count failures. Almost every expensive evaluation tells us only that
nothing failed. The inputs that determine the answer are precisely the ones we rarely see.

**Importance sampling** changes where we look. We draw more often from a convenient
**proposal distribution** that visits the important region, then attach a weight that corrects
for having changed the distribution. It matters in rare-event simulation, Bayesian inference,
off-policy evaluation, and latent-variable models. The method can reduce variance by orders of
magnitude, but a bad proposal can also produce an estimate dominated by one enormous weight.

This post follows the treatment in Chapter 14, "Sampling," of Christopher M. Bishop and Hugh
Bishop's *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts*. We will derive the identity, work through a
Gaussian tail with real numbers, then cover proposal design, self-normalized estimates, and the
diagnostics that reveal failure.

## The change of distribution

Let $p(x)$ be a **target distribution** and let $f(x)$ be a quantity whose expectation we want:

$$
I = \mathbb E_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)p(x)\,dx.
$$

Direct Monte Carlo draws $x_1,\ldots,x_N \sim p$ and uses

$$
\widehat I_{\text{direct}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N f(x_n).
$$

Now introduce a proposal density $q(x)$. Multiply and divide the integrand by $q(x)$:

$$
I
= \int f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}q(x)\,dx
= \mathbb E_q\!\left[f(X)w(X)\right],
\qquad
w(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.
$$

The ratio $w(x)$ is the **importance weight**. It is an accounting correction. If the proposal
draws $x$ ten times as often as the target would, then $w(x)=1/10$, so that sample contributes
one tenth as much. This gives the importance-sampling estimator

$$
\widehat I_{\text{IS}}
= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N f(x_n)w(x_n),
\qquad x_n\sim q.
$$

Its expectation is still $I$, so the estimator is unbiased when the weights are exact. The
essential condition is **support coverage**: wherever $f(x)p(x)$ is non-zero, $q(x)$ must also
be non-zero. A weight cannot repair a region that the proposal never samples.

## A worked rare-event estimate

Let $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and estimate

$$
I = \Pr(Z>3)
= \mathbb E_p[\mathbf 1\{Z>3\}],
$$

where $\mathbf 1\{\cdot\}$ is an **indicator function**: one when its condition is true and zero
otherwise. The exact value, used here to check the estimators, is

$$
I = 1-\Phi(3) = 0.001349898 \approx 0.135\%.
$$

Direct sampling therefore sees one useful observation every $1/I=740.8$ draws on average. For
one direct draw, the indicator is Bernoulli, so its variance is

$$
\operatorname{var}_p[\mathbf 1\{Z>3\}]
= I(1-I)
= 0.00134808.
$$

Choose the proposal $q=\mathcal N(3,1)$, shifting the same bell curve into the tail. For equal
variances, its weight simplifies to

$$
w(x)
= \frac{\mathcal N(x;0,1)}{\mathcal N(x;3,1)}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{3^2}{2}-3x\right).
$$

Take four illustrative proposal draws that all exceed the threshold. Their contributions are
their weights because the indicator equals one:

| proposal draw $x_n$ | indicator | weight $w(x_n)$ | contribution |
| ------------------: | --------: | --------------: | -----------: |
|                 3.0 |         1 |      0.01110900 |   0.01110900 |
|                 3.5 |         1 |      0.00247875 |   0.00247875 |
|                 4.0 |         1 |      0.00055308 |   0.00055308 |
|                 4.5 |         1 |      0.00012341 |   0.00012341 |

Check one entry: at $x=3.5$,
$w(3.5)=\exp(4.5-10.5)=e^{-6}=0.00247875$. The sample is common under the shifted
proposal but rare under the target, so it counts only a little. These four deliberately chosen
values illustrate the arithmetic; they are not presented as a random estimate.

### Comparing variance exactly

For this Gaussian example, we can compare the estimators without running a simulation. If
$q=\mathcal N(\mu,1)$, the second moment of one weighted contribution is

$$
\mathbb E_q\!\left[\mathbf 1\{X>3\}w(X)^2\right]
= e^{\mu^2}\left[1-\Phi(3+\mu)\right].
$$

Subtracting $I^2$ gives the per-draw variance. The same factor $1/N$ would divide every row for
an average of $N$ independent draws, so it cancels in the comparison.

| proposal mean $\mu$ | proposal draws above 3 | per-draw variance | reduction vs. direct |
| -------------------: | ----------------------: | ----------------: | -------------------: |
|                  0.0 |                  0.135% |      0.001348076 |                 1.0× |
|                  1.0 |                  2.275% |      0.000084269 |                16.0× |
|                  2.0 |                 15.866% |      0.000013828 |                97.5× |
|                  3.0 |                 50.000% |      0.000006172 |               218.4× |
|                  4.0 |                 84.134% |      0.000009550 |               141.2× |

Moving from $\mu=3$ to $\mu=4$ creates more event samples, yet variance gets worse. Hit rate is
not the objective. The proposal must cover the parts of $f(x)p(x)$ that matter *and* keep the
weights well behaved.

## How to choose the proposal

The variance of an importance estimate is controlled by the spread of $f(X)p(X)/q(X)$ under
$q$. If $f$ is non-negative, the zero-variance proposal is

$$
q^*(x)=\frac{f(x)p(x)}{I}.
$$

Under $q^*$, every contribution is exactly $I$. This is a guide, not an algorithm: its
normalizing constant is the unknown answer, and sampling from it may be as hard as the original
problem. Still, it tells us what a useful proposal should resemble: the magnitude of the
integrand $f(x)p(x)$, not merely the target $p(x)$.

For the tail example, $q^*$ is the standard normal conditioned on $x>3$. The shifted normal is
easy to sample and roughly covers that region, which explains its lower variance. In a
multimodal problem, one shifted bell curve may miss an entire mode. A **mixture proposal**---a
weighted combination of several distributions---can cover multiple important regions while a
broad component protects the tails.

Three rules are useful in practice:

- Give $q$ support everywhere that $|f|p$ has support.
- Put proposal mass where $|f(x)|p(x)$ is large, not only where $p(x)$ is large.
- Prefer slightly heavier tails than the target; a thin-tailed proposal can create rare,
  explosive weights.

## When the target is not normalized

Bayesian inference often gives an unnormalized density $\widetilde p(x)$ with unknown constant
$Z$:

$$
p(x)=\frac{\widetilde p(x)}{Z}.
$$

We cannot compute $p(x)/q(x)$, but $Z$ cancels if we normalize the raw weights
$\widetilde w_n=\widetilde p(x_n)/q(x_n)$ across the sample:

$$
\widehat I_{\text{SNIS}}
= \frac{\sum_{n=1}^N \widetilde w_n f(x_n)}
       {\sum_{n=1}^N \widetilde w_n}.
$$

This is **self-normalized importance sampling** (SNIS). Unlike the ordinary estimator, the ratio
is generally biased at finite $N$, though it is consistent under standard conditions: it
converges to the correct expectation as $N$ grows. Its practical advantage is that only the
target density up to proportionality is needed.<Sidenote>The normalized weights should be
computed in log space with a log-sum-exp operation. Direct exponentiation can underflow or
overflow long before the mathematics fails.</Sidenote>

## Diagnosing failure

An estimate may look stable because nearly all weights are tiny while one decisive sample has
not appeared yet. A standard warning light is the **effective sample size** (ESS):

$$
\operatorname{ESS}
= \frac{\left(\sum_n \widetilde w_n\right)^2}
       {\sum_n \widetilde w_n^2}
= \frac{1}{\sum_n \bar w_n^2},
$$

where $\bar w_n=\widetilde w_n/\sum_j\widetilde w_j$. Equal weights give $\operatorname{ESS}=N$;
one dominant weight gives an ESS near one. ESS is a heuristic, not a guarantee, so report it
with repeated-run variability, the maximum normalized weight, and a plot of the weight
distribution.

The central failure modes are:

- **Missing support:** $q(x)=0$ where $f(x)p(x)\ne0$. The estimator cannot recover the omitted
  contribution.
- **Weight degeneracy:** a few samples carry almost all the mass, producing high or even
  infinite variance.
- **A proposal tuned to the wrong quantity:** matching $p$ is insufficient when $f$ concentrates
  elsewhere.
- **Adaptive reuse without care:** learning $q$ from the same draws used for the final estimate
  can change its statistical properties. Separate adaptation and estimation, or use a method
  whose adaptive estimator has been justified.

## Where it appears in deep learning

The identity is simple, but it reappears in several forms. **Importance-weighted autoencoders**
(IWAEs) use multiple importance-weighted latent samples to optimize a tighter evidence lower
bound than the basic variational autoencoder objective. Large classifiers use candidate
sampling: sampled softmax evaluates a subset of classes and corrects for the sampling law.
Off-policy reinforcement learning similarly reweights trajectories or actions collected under
one policy to estimate expectations under another.

These applications share the same trade-off. A proposal that tracks the target or the important
part of the integrand can save computation. A mismatch produces variable weights, low effective
sample size, and unstable gradients.

## Summary

- Importance sampling draws from $q$ and multiplies by $p/q$, changing where computation is
  spent without changing the expectation.
- The proposal must cover the support of $f p$; no finite weight can correct a region that was
  never sampled.
- Good proposals resemble $|f|p$. In the Gaussian-tail example, shifting the proposal reduced
  variance by about $218\times$.
- Self-normalization removes an unknown target normalizer, at the cost of finite-sample bias.
- Inspect weight concentration and effective sample size. More event hits do not by themselves
  imply a better estimate.
