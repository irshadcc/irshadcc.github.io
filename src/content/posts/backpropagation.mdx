## Background

Training a neural network means nudging every weight a little in the direction that lowers the
error. To know that direction we need the **gradient**: the derivative of the error with respect
to every one of the network's $W$ weights and biases. A small image classifier has tens of
thousands of them; a language model has billions. The question this post answers is how to compute
all of those derivatives at once, cheaply.

The answer is **backpropagation**: a forward sweep computes every unit's activation, then a
backward sweep passes "error signals" from the outputs towards the inputs, and each weight's
derivative falls out as a product of two numbers already sitting at its ends. The whole gradient
costs about as much as two or three forward passes. The obvious alternative, nudging each weight
and re-running the network, costs $W$ times more. That gap is why deep learning is practical.

This post follows chapter 8 of Bishop and Bishop's *Deep Learning: Foundations and
Concepts*,<Sidenote>C. M. Bishop and H. Bishop, *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts*, Springer, 2024, chapter 8, pp. 233–252. Equation numbers such as (8.13) refer to the book.</Sidenote>
equation by equation. We derive backpropagation for a general network, run it by hand on a tiny
one, compare it with finite differences, reuse it for the **Jacobian** (derivatives of outputs with
respect to inputs) and the **Hessian** (second derivatives), and finally see it as one case of
**automatic differentiation**, in both its forward and reverse modes. The
[matrix calculus post](/blog/matrix-calculus/) covers the same ideas in matrix form, and
[Inside JAX](/blog/jax-internals/) shows how a real library implements them.

A word on the name. "Backpropagation" is sometimes used for a whole training procedure, or even
for feed-forward networks themselves. Here, as in the book, it means only the procedure that
*evaluates derivatives*. What you then do with the gradient (gradient descent, Adam) is a separate
step.

## Gradients of a feed-forward network

### One error term at a time

Most error functions used in practice, including every one derived by maximum likelihood on
independent data points, are a sum of one term per data point:

$$
E(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} E_n(\mathbf{w}). \tag{8.1}
$$

So it is enough to compute $\nabla E_n$ for a single data point $n$. Stochastic gradient descent
uses it directly; for a batch or mini-batch we add the per-point gradients:

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial w_{ji}} = \sum_n \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}}. \tag{8.14}
$$

From here on we fix one data point and drop the subscript $n$ from the network's variables.

### A single-layer network

Start with the simplest case, a linear model whose outputs are weighted sums of the inputs, with a
**sum-of-squares error**:

$$
y_k = \sum_i w_{ki} x_i, \qquad E_n = \frac{1}{2}\sum_k (y_{nk} - t_{nk})^2. \tag{8.2, 8.3}
$$

The weight $w_{ji}$ only affects output $y_j$, so

$$
\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}} = (y_{nj} - t_{nj})\, x_{ni}. \tag{8.4}
$$

Read this as a **local** computation on the link $w_{ji}$: an **error signal** $y_{nj} - t_{nj}$ at
the link's output end, times the variable $x_{ni}$ at its input end. For example, with inputs
$\mathbf{x} = (2, -1)$, an output $y_1 = 0.3$ and a target $t_1 = 1$, the error signal is $-0.7$, so
$\partial E_n/\partial w_{11} = -0.7 \times 2 = -1.4$ and $\partial E_n/\partial w_{12} = -0.7
\times (-1) = 0.7$. Gradient descent raises $w_{11}$ and lowers $w_{12}$, both of which raise $y_1$
towards its target. The same form also appears for a logistic sigmoid output with cross-entropy
error, and for softmax with multiclass cross-entropy, because those pairs are matched (each is the
**canonical link** for its error function).<Sidenote>Bishop and Bishop, sections 5.4.3 and 5.4.6. For each matched pair, $\partial E_n / \partial a_k = y_k - t_k$, where $a_k$ is the output unit's input before its activation function.</Sidenote>
The whole of backpropagation is the extension of (8.4) to networks with hidden layers.

### A general network

In a general feed-forward network, each unit $j$ forms a weighted sum of its inputs, the
**pre-activation**, and passes it through a nonlinear **activation function** $h$:

$$
a_j = \sum_i w_{ji} z_i, \qquad z_j = h(a_j). \tag{8.5, 8.6}
$$

Here $z_i$ is either an input or another unit's activation. A bias is handled by adding a unit whose
activation is fixed at $+1$; its weight is then the bias, so biases need no separate treatment.
Running (8.5) and (8.6) for every unit in order, from inputs to outputs, is **forward
propagation**.

Now take the derivative of $E_n$ with respect to one weight $w_{ji}$. The weight affects the error
only through $a_j$, so the chain rule gives two factors:

$$
\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}} = \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a_j}\,\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial w_{ji}}. \tag{8.7}
$$

The first factor gets a name. The **error** of unit $j$ is

$$
\delta_j \equiv \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a_j}, \tag{8.8}
$$

and the second factor follows from (8.5), since $a_j$ is linear in $w_{ji}$:

$$
\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial w_{ji}} = z_i. \tag{8.9}
$$

Substituting both into (8.7):

$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}} = \delta_j\, z_i.} \tag{8.10}
$$

This is (8.4) again: the $\delta$ at the weight's output end times the $z$ at its input end (with
$z = 1$ for a bias). So once we know $\delta_j$ for every hidden and output unit, every derivative
is one multiplication.

For output units with the canonical link, the error is already known:

$$
\delta_k = y_k - t_k. \tag{8.11}
$$

For a hidden unit $j$, use the chain rule again. Unit $j$ influences the error only through the
pre-activations $a_k$ of the units $k$ it sends connections to:

$$
\delta_j \equiv \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a_j} = \sum_k \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a_k}\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial a_j}. \tag{8.12}
$$

The first factor is $\delta_k$. For the second, $a_k = \sum_j w_{kj} h(a_j)$, so $\partial a_k /
\partial a_j = w_{kj} h'(a_j)$. Together they give the **backpropagation formula**:

$$
\boxed{\delta_j = h'(a_j)\sum_k w_{kj}\,\delta_k.} \tag{8.13}
$$

A unit's error is the weighted sum of the errors of the units it feeds, scaled by the slope of its
own activation function. Step through the figure: the forward step shows (8.5) and (8.6), the
backward step shows the same weights carrying $\delta$s the other way.

Look at the indices. In the forward sum (8.5) the sum runs over the *second* index of $w_{ji}$
(the units feeding $j$). In (8.13) it runs over the *first* index of $w_{kj}$ (the units $j$
feeds). In a layered network with weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, forward propagation multiplies by
$\mathbf{W}$ and backpropagation multiplies by $\mathbf{W}^\top$.<Sidenote>This is the book's Exercise 8.2. With $\mathbf{a}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{z}^{(l-1)}$, (8.13) becomes $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l-1)} = h'(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}) \odot \mathbf{W}^{(l)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$, and (8.10) becomes the outer product $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}} E_n = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\mathbf{z}^{(l-1)\top}$.</Sidenote>
Because the output $\delta$s are known from (8.11), applying (8.13) from the top of the network
down gives every hidden $\delta$, for any feed-forward topology. If different units use different
activation functions, each just uses its own $h'$.

### The algorithm

The book collects the steps as Algorithm 8.1:

1. **Forward propagation.** For every hidden and output unit in order, compute $a_j$ by (8.5) and
   $z_j$ by (8.6). Keep them.
2. **Error evaluation.** For every output unit, compute $\delta_k = \partial E_n / \partial a_k$
   (by (8.11) for a canonical link).
3. **Backward propagation.** For every hidden unit in *reverse* order, compute $\delta_j$ by
   (8.13), and every derivative $\partial E_n / \partial w_{ji} = \delta_j z_i$ by (8.10).

For a network with one hidden layer, in NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np

def backprop(W1, W2, x, t):
    # Forward propagation (8.18)-(8.20); x0 = z0 = 1 carry the biases.
    x = np.concatenate([[1.0], x])
    a = W1 @ x
    z = np.concatenate([[1.0], np.tanh(a)])
    y = W2 @ z
    # Error evaluation (8.21).
    d_out = y - t
    # Backward propagation (8.22): drop the bias column, z0 has no delta.
    d_hid = (1 - z[1:] ** 2) * (W2[:, 1:].T @ d_out)
    # Derivatives (8.23): delta at the output end times activation at the input end.
    return np.outer(d_hid, x), np.outer(d_out, z)
```

Note the `W2[:, 1:].T`: the transpose is the "first index" of (8.13).

## A worked example

The book's example is a two-layer network: $D$ inputs, $M$ hidden units with
$h(a) = \tanh(a)$, and $K$ linear outputs ($y_k = a_k$), trained with sum-of-squares error. Tanh
has a convenient derivative, expressed through its own output:

$$
h(a) = \tanh(a), \qquad h'(a) = 1 - h(a)^2. \tag{8.15, 8.16}
$$

So the backward pass needs only the stored $z_j$, not $a_j$. With $x_0 = z_0 = 1$ for the biases,
forward propagation is

$$
a_j = \sum_{i=0}^{D} w^{(1)}_{ji} x_i, \qquad z_j = \tanh(a_j), \qquad y_k = \sum_{j=0}^{M} w^{(2)}_{kj} z_j, \tag{8.18–8.20}
$$

the error for one data point is

$$
E_n = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(y_k - t_k)^2, \tag{8.17}
$$

and the backward pass is

$$
\delta_k = y_k - t_k, \qquad \delta_j = (1 - z_j^2)\sum_{k=1}^{K} w^{(2)}_{kj}\delta_k, \tag{8.21, 8.22}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w^{(1)}_{ji}} = \delta_j x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w^{(2)}_{kj}} = \delta_k z_j. \tag{8.23}
$$

Here it is with $D = 2$, $M = 3$, $K = 2$, one input $\mathbf{x} = (0.8, -0.5)$ and target
$\mathbf{t} = (0.5, -0.5)$. The weights are listed in the first step. Step through the forward
pass, the output errors, the backward pass (first for one hidden unit, then for all three) and the
two gradient matrices. Blue marks the forward direction and red the backward one.

## Why backpropagation is efficient

### The cost of a forward pass

Let $W$ be the total number of weights and biases. One forward pass costs $O(W)$ operations: each
term of the sum (8.5) is one multiplication and one addition, and except in very sparse networks
there are far more weights than units, so the activation functions are a small overhead.

The backward pass has the same shape. The sums in (8.13) touch each weight once more, and (8.10)
is one more multiplication per weight. So backpropagation gets all $W$ derivatives in $O(W)$, a
small constant times the cost of the forward pass.

### Numerical differentiation

The alternative needs no derivation at all: perturb one weight, re-run the network, and divide.
The **forward difference** is

$$
\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}} = \frac{E_n(w_{ji} + \epsilon) - E_n(w_{ji})}{\epsilon} + O(\epsilon), \tag{8.24}
$$

and the **central difference**, which perturbs both ways, is

$$
\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial w_{ji}} = \frac{E_n(w_{ji} + \epsilon) - E_n(w_{ji} - \epsilon)}{2\epsilon} + O(\epsilon^2). \tag{8.25}
$$

Why the different error orders? Expand in a Taylor series, writing $E'$, $E''$, $E'''$ for
derivatives along $w_{ji}$:

$$
E(w \pm \epsilon) = E \pm \epsilon E' + \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^2 E'' \pm \tfrac{1}{6}\epsilon^3 E''' + O(\epsilon^4).
$$

The forward difference keeps the $\tfrac12 \epsilon E''$ term, an $O(\epsilon)$ error. In the
central difference the even terms cancel when the two expansions are subtracted, leaving
$E' + \tfrac16 \epsilon^2 E''' + O(\epsilon^4)$.<Sidenote>This is the book's Exercise 8.3.</Sidenote>

Shrinking $\epsilon$ does not help forever. A double has a relative precision of about
$u = 1.1 \times 10^{-16}$, so the difference in the numerator carries a rounding error of roughly
$u|E|$, which the division by $\epsilon$ blows up into $u|E|/\epsilon$. The total error is about
$\epsilon|E''| + u|E|/\epsilon$ for forward differences, smallest near $\epsilon \approx \sqrt{u}
\approx 10^{-8}$, and $\epsilon^2|E'''| + u|E|/\epsilon$ for central differences, smallest near
$\epsilon \approx u^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$. The chart reproduces the book's Figure 8.2 on our
network, for the derivative with respect to $w^{(1)}_{11}$. On the right, truncation error
dominates and the lines have slope 1 and 2. On the left, rounding dominates and the lines turn
noisy and rise.

## The Jacobian matrix

Backpropagation computes more than the gradient of an error. The **Jacobian** of a network is the
matrix of derivatives of its outputs with respect to its inputs,

$$
J_{ki} \equiv \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i}, \tag{8.26}
$$

each taken with the other inputs held fixed.

### Why Jacobians matter

Jacobians are the glue in systems built from several differentiable modules. In the figure below,
module 2 sits between the parameter $w$ of module 1 and the error. To train $w$ we need

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial w} = \sum_{k,j} \frac{\partial E}{\partial y_k}\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial z_j}\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial w}, \tag{8.27}
$$

and the middle factor is module 2's Jacobian. Module 2 can be anything differentiable, fixed or
learnable, linear or not: as long as it can multiply an error signal by its Jacobian, errors pass
through it. Click the boxes; the bottom row is the backward flow.

<BoxDiagram nodes={moduleNodes} edges={moduleEdges} selected="m2" title="Error signals through a modular system (after Figure 8.3)" />

The Jacobian also measures **sensitivity**. If the inputs carry small known errors $\Delta x_i$,
the outputs move by about

$$
\Delta y_k \simeq \sum_i \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i}\Delta x_i. \tag{8.28}
$$

This is a first-order approximation. A trained network is nonlinear, so the Jacobian changes with
the input and (8.28) holds only for small $\Delta x$. The chart moves $x_1$ of our example network
by $\Delta x_1$ and compares the true change in $y_2$ with $J_{21}\Delta x_1$ ($J_{21} =
0.8211$). Near zero they agree: at $\Delta x_1 = 0.25$ the estimate is off by 4%. At $\Delta x_1 = 1$ it is off by 22%, and at $2$ by 60%. (On the negative side the curves happen to cross again near $\Delta x_1 = -2$; that is a coincidence of this network's shape, not a sign that (8.28) holds there.)

### Computing the Jacobian by backpropagation

The derivation copies the one for the gradient. Input $i$ reaches the outputs only through the
pre-activations $a_j$ of the units it connects to, and $\partial a_j / \partial x_i = w_{ji}$ by
(8.5):

$$
J_{ki} = \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_j}\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x_i} = \sum_j w_{ji}\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_j}. \tag{8.29}
$$

The quantities $\partial y_k / \partial a_j$ play the role of $\delta_j$, and obey the same
recursion, where $l$ runs over the units that $j$ feeds:

$$
\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_j} = \sum_l \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_l}\frac{\partial a_l}{\partial a_j} = h'(a_j)\sum_l w_{lj}\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_l}. \tag{8.30}
$$

The recursion starts at the output units. For linear outputs, $y_k = a_k$, so

$$
\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_l} = \delta_{kl}, \qquad \delta_{kl} = \begin{cases} 1 & k = l \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \tag{8.31, 8.32}
$$

where $\delta_{kl}$ is the **Kronecker delta**, the entries of the identity matrix (not an error
$\delta$). Other output activations change only this starting point:

$$
\text{logistic sigmoid: } \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_l} = \delta_{kl}\,\sigma'(a_l), \qquad \text{softmax: } \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial a_l} = \delta_{kl}\,y_k - y_k y_l. \tag{8.33, 8.34}
$$

A sigmoid output depends only on its own pre-activation, so its starting matrix is diagonal. A
softmax couples all outputs, because they must sum to one. For example, softmax of
$\mathbf{a} = (1, 0, -1)$ gives $\mathbf{y} = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, and (8.34) gives a full
matrix whose first row is $(0.223, -0.163, -0.060)$. Check the first entry:
$y_1 - y_1^2 = 0.665 - 0.443 = 0.223$. The row sums to zero: raising $a_1$ takes probability from
the other outputs.<Sidenote>Values from `torch.softmax` and `torch.autograd.functional.jacobian`, which matches $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{y}) - \mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^\top$ to $3 \times 10^{-17}$. For the sigmoid at the same $\mathbf{a}$, the diagonal is $\sigma(1 - \sigma) = (0.197, 0.25, 0.197)$.</Sidenote>

So the procedure is: forward-propagate the input, then for each output $k$ (each row of
$\mathbf{J}$) seed the outputs with (8.31), (8.33) or (8.34), backpropagate with (8.30) to the
hidden units, and finish with (8.29). One backward pass per row. Step through it on our network:

<GraphSteps data={jacobianSteps} title="The Jacobian of the 2-3-2 network, one row per backward pass (8.29)–(8.31)" />

The last step checks the result with central differences, now perturbing inputs instead of
weights:

$$
\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i} = \frac{y_k(x_i + \epsilon) - y_k(x_i - \epsilon)}{2\epsilon} + O(\epsilon^2). \tag{8.35}
$$

That takes $2D$ forward passes, $O(DW)$ in total.<Sidenote>The Jacobian can also be built by propagating *forwards*, carrying $\partial a_j/\partial x_i$ for one input $i$ through the network; that is the book's Exercise 8.5, and it is exactly forward-mode automatic differentiation, below. Forward gives one column per pass, backward one row per pass.</Sidenote>

## The Hessian matrix

### Second derivatives and their cost

Backpropagation can also deliver second derivatives. Collect all $W$ weights and biases into one
vector $\mathbf{w}$; the **Hessian** is the $W \times W$ matrix

$$
H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_i\,\partial w_j}, \qquad \text{elements of } \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_{ji}\,\partial w_{lk}}. \tag{8.36, 8.37}
$$

It describes the curvature of the error surface (see [the Hessian post](/blog/hessian-matrix/)),
and it is used by second-order optimizers, by Bayesian treatments of neural networks, and to
decide how far each weight can be rounded when quantizing a large model.<Sidenote>Bishop and Bishop cite Bishop (2006) for optimization and Bayesian uses, MacKay (1992) for the Bayesian framework, and Shen et al. (2019) for Hessian-guided low-precision weights.</Sidenote>

The difficulty is size. The Hessian has $W^2$ entries, so even an efficient extension of
backpropagation, which exists, costs $O(W^2)$ per data point.<Sidenote>Bishop (1992). The book's Exercise 8.6 derives the exact Hessian of a two-layer network from the quantities $\delta_k$ and $M_{kk'} = \partial^2 E_n/\partial a_k \partial a_{k'}$.</Sidenote>
For a 7-billion-parameter model that is $4.9 \times 10^{19}$ entries, about 98 exabytes at two
bytes each. Inverting it costs $O(W^3)$. In practice we need either products with the Hessian
(below) or approximations.

The simplest approximation keeps only the diagonal. It needs $O(W)$ storage and its inverse is
trivial, but computing it exactly still takes $O(W^2)$ (Ricotti et al., 1988), or $O(W)$ with
further approximation (Becker and LeCun, 1989). Real Hessians usually have large off-diagonal
entries, so the diagonal approximation must be used with care.

### The outer product approximation

A better approximation comes from the structure of sum-of-squares error. Take one output and

$$
E = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - t_n)^2. \tag{8.38}
$$

Differentiate once, $\nabla E = \sum_n (y_n - t_n)\nabla y_n$, and again with the product rule:

$$
\mathbf{H} = \nabla\nabla E = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla y_n(\nabla y_n)^\top + \sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - t_n)\nabla\nabla y_n. \tag{8.39}
$$

The first sum needs only first derivatives. The second is weighted by the residuals $y_n - t_n$.
For a well-trained network the residuals are small, so the second sum is small. There is a
subtler argument too: the function that minimizes sum-of-squares error is the conditional mean of
the target, so the residual behaves like zero-mean noise, and if it is uncorrelated with
$\nabla\nabla y_n$ the second sum averages out over $n$.<Sidenote>The book's Exercise 8.9 makes this exact in the infinite-data limit: at the optimum, $\partial^2 E/\partial w_r \partial w_s = \int (\partial y/\partial w_r)(\partial y/\partial w_s)\,p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$.</Sidenote>
Dropping it gives the **Levenberg–Marquardt** or **outer product approximation**:

$$
\mathbf{H} \simeq \sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla a_n\,\nabla a_n^\top. \tag{8.40}
$$

The book writes $\nabla a_n$, the gradient of the output unit's pre-activation; with a linear
output $y_n = a_n$, so this is the $\nabla y_n$ of (8.39). Each $\nabla a_n$ costs one backward
pass, $O(W)$, and the outer products add up in $O(W^2)$. The approximation is also always
**positive semi-definite** (a sum of outer products), which the true Hessian need not be.

At the last checkpoint, identity (8.39) holds to {sci(hessianFinal.err39)}. Learning rate {hessianSetup.lr}, torch seed 0.</Sidenote>

Early on the true Hessian has a large negative eigenvalue ({first.minEig.toFixed(2)}): the error
surface curves downward in some direction, and a Newton step would go the wrong way. The outer
product approximation cannot see this; its eigenvalues are never negative (the values near
$10^{-16}$ are rounding). That is a feature for optimization, since it always gives a descent
direction, but it means (8.40) is only a faithful Hessian for a network that is already well
trained.

For a single logistic-sigmoid output with cross-entropy error, the same steps give a weighted
version. There $\partial E_n/\partial a_n = y_n - t_n$ again, so
$\nabla\nabla E = \sum_n y_n(1 - y_n)\nabla a_n\nabla a_n^\top + \sum_n (y_n - t_n)\nabla\nabla a_n$,
where $y_n(1-y_n) = \sigma'(a_n)$ comes from differentiating $y_n$ once more. Dropping the second
sum:

$$
\mathbf{H} \simeq \sum_{n=1}^{N} y_n(1 - y_n)\nabla a_n\nabla a_n^\top. \tag{8.41}
$$

Softmax outputs give the analogous matrix-weighted form. Because (8.40) adds one outer product per
data point, its *inverse* can also be built in one pass over the data, updating with the
Sherman–Morrison identity at each point.<Sidenote>Hassibi and Stork (1993); the book's Exercise 8.12. With $\mathbf{H}_{n+1} = \mathbf{H}_n + \mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^\top$ and $\mathbf{b} = \nabla a_{n+1}$: $\mathbf{H}_{n+1}^{-1} = \mathbf{H}_n^{-1} - \frac{\mathbf{H}_n^{-1}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{H}_n^{-1}}{1 + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{H}_n^{-1}\mathbf{b}}$, starting from $\mathbf{H}_0 = \alpha\mathbf{I}$ with small $\alpha$.</Sidenote>

Often we need neither the Hessian nor an approximation, only its product $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}$
with a vector. That costs $O(W)$, and the cleanest way to see why is automatic differentiation.

## Automatic differentiation

### Four ways to get a gradient

The book lists four ways to evaluate the derivatives of a network's error:

| approach | how | accuracy | cost | weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| By hand | derive backprop equations, code them | machine precision | $O(W)$ | slow to derive, error-prone, two codes to keep in sync |
| Numerical | finite differences (8.24), (8.25) | limited by $\epsilon$ and rounding | $O(W^2)$ | scales badly; useful for checking |
| Symbolic | computer algebra applies the rules of calculus | machine precision | can blow up | expression swell; needs closed form, no loops or branches |
| Automatic | augment the forward code with derivative variables | machine precision | $O(W)$ | needs the library's support (now standard) |

Hand-derived backpropagation was the norm for years. The cost is human: every architecture change
means re-deriving and re-coding the backward pass in step with the forward pass, which limits how
quickly new architectures can be tried. Numerical differentiation we have seen. That leaves
symbolic and automatic differentiation.

### Symbolic differentiation and expression swell

**Symbolic differentiation** has software apply the chain rule, product rule and so on to a
formula, producing a formula for the derivative. It is exact and avoids human error, but the
derivative of a formula can be much longer than the formula. Even the product rule duplicates:

$$
f(x) = u(x)v(x), \qquad f'(x) = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x). \tag{8.42, 8.43}
$$

Now $u$ and $v$ each appear twice. If they are products themselves, the duplication nests. This
is **expression swell**.

The book's example is a network with one input $x$, one hidden unit and one output,

$$
z = h(w_1 x + b_1), \qquad y = h(w_2 z + b_2), \tag{8.44, 8.45}
$$

where the activation $h$ is the **soft ReLU** (softplus), which the book writes as $\zeta$:

$$
\zeta(a) = \ln(1 + \exp(a)), \tag{8.46}
$$

so that

$$
y(x) = h\big(w_2\, h(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2\big). \tag{8.47}
$$

The symbolic derivative with respect to $w_1$ is

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial w_1} = \frac{w_2 x \exp\big(w_1 x + b_1 + b_2 + w_2 \ln[1 + e^{w_1 x + b_1}]\big)}{(1 + e^{w_1 x + b_1})\big(1 + \exp(b_2 + w_2\ln[1 + e^{w_1 x + b_1}])\big)}. \tag{8.48}
$$

The subexpression $w_1 x + b_1$ appears three times and $\ln[1 + e^{w_1x+b_1}]$ twice; a program
evaluating the formula recomputes them. Backpropagation would instead reuse the stored
pre-activations: since $\zeta'(a) = \sigma(a)$, the derivative is just
$\sigma(a_2)\, w_2\, \sigma(a_1)\, x$.<Sidenote>Checked with sympy 1.14: `diff` of (8.47) and the expression (8.48) agree numerically ($-0.147720$ at $x = 0.7$, $w_1 = 1.3$, $w_2 = -0.8$, $b_1 = 0.2$, $b_2 = 0.5$). sympy's own derivative has 29 operations against 10 for $y$. This is the book's Exercise 8.13.</Sidenote>

Swell grows with depth. The book's Exercise 8.14 uses the **logistic map**
$L_{n+1}(x) = 4L_n(x)(1 - L_n(x))$, $L_1(x) = x$, which is like a deep network with one unit per
layer. The chart counts the operations in the unsimplified formulas for $L_n$ and $L_n'$, and in
an evaluation trace (defined next) that computes them.

{swell[7].f} for the function, and both roughly double or triple per step because each step uses
$L_n$ twice. A program that computes $L_n$ step by step, carrying the derivative along, needs 6

The trace count is 3 operations for $v \leftarrow 4v(1-v)$ and 3 for $\dot v \leftarrow \dot v(4 - 8v)$ per step. The formula sizes depend on how the expression is written; the growth rate is the point.</Sidenote>
Symbolic differentiation has a second limitation: it needs a closed-form expression, so it cannot
handle loops, recursion, branches or function calls.

### The idea of automatic differentiation

**Automatic differentiation** (autodiff, or algorithmic differentiation) does not produce a formula
for the derivative. It takes the *code* that computes a function and produces code that computes
its derivatives, by augmenting each intermediate variable with a derivative variable. It is exact
to machine precision, like symbolic differentiation, but it reuses intermediate values instead of
duplicating them, and it handles any control flow, because it differentiates the operations that
actually ran. It comes in two forms, **forward mode** and **reverse mode**.<Sidenote>Baydin et al. (2018) survey autodiff for machine learning. The field developed mostly outside machine learning; Griewank and Walther (2008) is the standard reference.</Sidenote>

The book's running example has two inputs:

$$
f(x_1, x_2) = x_1 x_2 + \exp(x_1 x_2) - \sin(x_2). \tag{8.49}
$$

Written as code, it is a sequence of elementary operations, the **evaluation trace**. Each
intermediate result is a **primal variable** $v_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_1 &= x_1, & v_2 &= x_2, & v_3 &= v_1 v_2, & v_4 &= \sin(v_2),\\
v_5 &= \exp(v_3), & v_6 &= v_3 - v_4, & v_7 &= v_5 + v_6. &&
\end{aligned} \tag{8.50–8.56}
$$

Note that $x_1 x_2$ is computed once, as $v_3$, and used twice. The trace is a graph: $v_3$ has
**parents** $v_1$ and $v_2$ (written $\mathrm{pa}(3)$) and **children** $v_5$ and $v_6$
($\mathrm{ch}(3)$). The figures below draw it as in the book's Figures 8.4 and 8.5. All examples
use $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 2$.

### Forward mode

Forward mode pairs each primal $v_i$ with a **tangent variable** $\dot v_i$, the derivative of
$v_i$ with respect to one chosen input. For $\partial f/\partial x_1$, $\dot v_i = \partial v_i /
\partial x_1$. The chain rule computes each tangent from its parents' tangents:

$$
\dot v_i = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_1} = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{pa}(i)} \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_1}\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial v_j} = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{pa}(i)} \dot v_j\,\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial v_j}. \tag{8.57}
$$

Applied to each line of the trace, (8.57) gives the tangent trace:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot v_1 &= 1, & \dot v_2 &= 0, & \dot v_3 &= v_1 \dot v_2 + \dot v_1 v_2, & \dot v_4 &= \dot v_2\cos(v_2),\\
\dot v_5 &= \dot v_3 \exp(v_3), & \dot v_6 &= \dot v_3 - \dot v_4, & \dot v_7 &= \dot v_5 + \dot v_6. &&
\end{aligned} \tag{8.58–8.64}
$$

The software writes these from the primal code, one rule per elementary operation, and runs both
together: each step computes the pair $(v_i, \dot v_i)$, then both can be discarded once their
children are done. The figure also includes the book's second output (Figure 8.5),

$$
f_2(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 x_2 - \sin(x_2))\exp(x_1 x_2) = v_5 v_6, \tag{8.65}
$$

which costs one more line, $v_8 = v_5 v_6$.

The final tangent is $\dot v_7 = 16.778 = \partial f_1/\partial x_1$, which matches the closed
form $x_2 + x_2 e^{x_1 x_2} = 2 + 2e^2$.<Sidenote>The book says the pass ends at $\dot v_5$, "which is the required derivative"; it should be $\dot v_7$, since $v_7 = f$. This is the book's Exercise 8.17, worked in the figure.</Sidenote>
The same pass also gave $\partial f_2/\partial x_1 = 30.897$. But
$\partial f_1/\partial x_2$ needs a *second* pass, seeded with $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = (0, 1)$.

In general, for a function with $D$ inputs and $K$ outputs, the **Jacobian** is $K \times D$:

$$
\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_D} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_K}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_K}{\partial x_D} \end{bmatrix}. \tag{8.66}
$$

A forward pass seeded with $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{e}_i$ (the $i$th unit vector) produces column
$i$, so the full Jacobian takes $D$ passes. Here, with two passes:

$$
\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} 16.778 & 8.805 \\ 30.897 & 18.523 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Seeding with an arbitrary vector $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{r}$ gives the **Jacobian-vector
product** $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{r}$ in one pass, because the tangent equations are linear in the seed
and $\mathbf{r} = \sum_i r_i\mathbf{e}_i$:<Sidenote>This is the book's Exercise 8.18.</Sidenote>

$$
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{r} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_D} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_K}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_K}{\partial x_D} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ \vdots \\ r_D \end{bmatrix}. \tag{8.67}
$$

With $\mathbf{r} = (1, -1)$ a single pass gives $(\dot v_7, \dot v_8) =
(7.973, 12.373)$. Check the first entry from the matrix:
$16.778 - 8.805 = 7.973$.

Forward mode is efficient when there are few inputs and many outputs ($K \gg D$). Training is the
opposite: one output, the error, and millions or billions of inputs, the weights. Forward mode
would need one pass per weight, which is no better than finite differences.

### Reverse mode

Reverse mode turns the flow around. Each primal $v_i$ gets an **adjoint variable**, the derivative
of the (single) output with respect to it:

$$
\bar v_i = \frac{\partial f}{\partial v_i}. \tag{8.68}
$$

Now the chain rule runs over a node's *children*, the variables it was used to compute:

$$
\bar v_i = \frac{\partial f}{\partial v_i} = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{ch}(i)} \frac{\partial f}{\partial v_j}\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i} = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{ch}(i)} \bar v_j\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i}. \tag{8.69}
$$

This is (8.12) again, with "units" replaced by any elementary operation: reverse mode is the
generalization of backpropagation. For our trace it gives, starting at the output:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar v_7 &= 1, & \bar v_6 &= \bar v_7, & \bar v_5 &= \bar v_7, & \bar v_4 &= -\bar v_6,\\
\bar v_3 &= \bar v_5 v_5 + \bar v_6, & \bar v_2 &= \bar v_3 v_1 + \bar v_4\cos(v_2), & \bar v_1 &= \bar v_3 v_2. &&
\end{aligned} \tag{8.70–8.76}
$$

The term $\bar v_5 v_5$ in $\bar v_3$ uses $\partial v_5/\partial v_3 = \exp(v_3) = v_5$, a value
already stored. In the figure, each red edge label is one term of (8.69): the child's adjoint times
the local derivative.

<GraphSteps data={reverseModeSteps} title="Reverse-mode autodiff of (8.49): adjoints from the output back (8.69)–(8.76)" colGap={175} nodeWidth={118} />

One backward pass gave both $\partial f/\partial x_1 = \bar v_1 = 16.778$ and
$\partial f/\partial x_2 = \bar v_2 = 8.805$: the whole gradient, however many
inputs there are. For a network's error, the adjoints of the weights are exactly the derivatives
$\partial E/\partial w$. With several outputs, reverse mode needs one pass per output; each pass
gives one *row* of $\mathbf{J}$, a **vector-Jacobian product** $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{J}$ with
$\mathbf{u}$ the seed.

PyTorch exposes both modes directly. `torch.func.jvp` is forward mode and `torch.func.vjp` reverse
mode; on our two-output function they return column 1 and row 1 of $\mathbf{J}$, and the
Jacobian-vector product for $\mathbf{r} = (1, -1)$:

```python
import torch
from torch.func import jvp, vjp

def f(x):
    x1, x2 = x
    v3 = x1 * x2
    return torch.stack([v3 + torch.exp(v3) - torch.sin(x2),          # f1 (8.49)
                        (v3 - torch.sin(x2)) * torch.exp(v3)])       # f2 (8.65)

x = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0], dtype=torch.float64)
e1 = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0], dtype=torch.float64)

_, col = jvp(f, (x,), (e1,))      # forward mode: column 1 of J
_, pullback = vjp(f, x)
(row,) = pullback(e1)             # reverse mode: row 1 of J
_, Jr = jvp(f, (x,), (torch.tensor([1.0, -1.0], dtype=torch.float64),))
print(col, row, Jr, sep="\n")
# tensor([16.7781, 30.8966], dtype=torch.float64)
# tensor([16.7781,  8.8052], dtype=torch.float64)
# tensor([ 7.9729, 12.3734], dtype=torch.float64)
```

### Forward or reverse?

| | forward mode | reverse mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| carries | tangents $\dot v_i = \partial v_i/\partial x_j$ | adjoints $\bar v_i = \partial f/\partial v_i$ |
| chain rule over | parents, (8.57) | children, (8.69) |
| one pass gives | one column of $\mathbf{J}$, or $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{r}$ | one row of $\mathbf{J}$, or $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{J}$ |
| full $K \times D$ Jacobian | $D$ passes | $K$ passes |
| best when | few inputs, many outputs | few outputs, many inputs (training) |
| memory | discard values as you go | keep every primal for the backward pass |

The memory row matters in practice. Reverse mode cannot start until the forward pass has finished,
and it needs the stored primals ($v_1$, $v_2$, $v_5$ above; every layer's activations in a
network). Activation memory is often what limits batch size, which is why frameworks offer to
recompute some activations instead of storing them (see [AOTAutograd](/blog/aot-autograd/) for how
PyTorch decides). Forward mode computes each tangent alongside its primal and is simpler to
implement.

Both modes are cheap. A single pass of either costs at most about 6 times one evaluation of the
function, and typically 2 to 3 times (Griewank and Walther, 2008), consistent with the
multiplication count earlier.

### Hessian-vector products

Combining the two modes gives the Hessian-vector product promised earlier (Pearlmutter, 1994).
Reverse mode computes the gradient $\nabla f$ as a program. Running forward mode on *that* program,
seeded with $\dot{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{v}$, gives the directional derivative of the gradient,

$$
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\nabla f(\mathbf{w} + \alpha\mathbf{v})\Big|_{\alpha=0} = \nabla^2 f\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{v},
$$

at a small constant times the cost of the gradient: $O(W)$, even though $\mathbf{H}$ has $W^2$
entries. The book describes the mirror-image order (forward mode to get
$\mathbf{v}^\top\nabla f$, then reverse mode to differentiate it); both orders give
$\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}$.<Sidenote>The book's text starts "from a vector $\mathbf{b}$" but then seeds with $\mathbf{v}$; they are the same vector. Forming the full Hessian by autodiff is possible too, at $O(W^2)$.</Sidenote>
On the Hessian experiment's final network ($W = 10$) we compared three ways of computing
$\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}$ for a random $\mathbf{v}$: the full matrix times $\mathbf{v}$,
forward-over-reverse with `torch.func.jvp(torch.func.grad(E), (w,), (v,))`, and central
differences of the gradient, $(\nabla E(\mathbf{w} + \epsilon\mathbf{v}) - \nabla E(\mathbf{w} -
\epsilon\mathbf{v}))/2\epsilon$ with $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$. Forward-over-reverse matched the full
product to {sci(hessianFinal.errFwdRev)}, rounding level; central differences to
{sci(hessianFinal.errFd)}, the accuracy the finite-difference chart predicts.

## Summary

- Each weight's derivative is local: $\partial E_n/\partial w_{ji} = \delta_j z_i$, the error at
  the weight's output end times the activation at its input end (8.10).
- Errors flow backwards through the same weights, transposed:
  $\delta_j = h'(a_j)\sum_k w_{kj}\delta_k$ (8.13). One forward and one backward sweep give all $W$
  derivatives in $O(W)$, against $O(W^2)$ for finite differences. Central differences remain the
  tool for checking gradients.
- The same recursion with a one-hot seed gives the Jacobian, one row per backward pass. The
  Hessian has $W^2$ entries, so in practice we use the outer product approximation (8.40), good
  only once the residuals are small, or Hessian-vector products in $O(W)$.
- Automatic differentiation generalizes backpropagation to any program. Forward mode carries
  tangents and gives one Jacobian column per pass; reverse mode carries adjoints, gives one row
  per pass, and is what training uses, at the price of storing the forward pass.
