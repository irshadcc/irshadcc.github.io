## Background

A batch model collects a dataset, trains, and then serves a fixed set of parameters. That split is
awkward for a search-ad system. Impressions arrive continuously, a click changes what the system
knows, and yesterday's traffic need not look like today's. Rebuilding a model from all historical
data after every event would be far too expensive.

**Online learning** processes examples one at a time: predict with the current model, observe the
outcome, suffer a loss, and update the model. It matters wherever feedback is a stream---ads,
recommendations, spam filtering, pricing, and monitoring. It also gives us a way to reason about
changing or even adversarial data without assuming that examples are independent and identically
distributed (IID).

This post first defines the online protocol and loss, then builds **regret** as its measure of
performance. From there we derive Follow the Regularized Leader (FTRL), prove a basic regret
bound, and finally derive the sparse, per-coordinate FTRL-Proximal update in Google's
*Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches*. A five-impression example checks every state
update.

## The online-learning protocol

At round $t=1,2,\ldots,T$, a learner goes through four ordered steps:

1. observe a context $x_t$ and choose parameters $w_t$;
2. make a prediction, such as $p_t=\sigma(w_t^\top x_t)$;
3. observe the outcome $y_t$ and incur loss $\ell_t(w_t)$;
4. use that loss to construct $w_{t+1}$.

Here $\sigma(a)=1/(1+e^{-a})$ is the **sigmoid** function. The subscript matters: $w_t$ must be
chosen before $y_t$ is known. Otherwise the learner could simply copy the answer.

“Online” describes the order of information, not necessarily the deployment architecture. A
system may update after every example, after a small mini-batch, or by replaying a stored stream.
The mathematical distinction from ordinary empirical risk minimization is that each prediction is
evaluated using only the past.

### Loss turns a mistake into a number

A **loss function** says how costly a prediction is. For a binary label $y_t\in\{0,1\}$ and a
predicted probability $p_t$, logistic loss is

$$
\ell_t(p_t)
=-y_t\log p_t-(1-y_t)\log(1-p_t).
$$

It is zero only in the limit of a perfectly confident, correct prediction. Confident mistakes are
expensive: predicting $p=0.9$ when $y=0$ costs $-\log(0.1)=2.303$, while predicting $p=0.6$
costs $-\log(0.4)=0.916$.

For logistic regression, let $a_t=w^\top x_t$ and $p_t=\sigma(a_t)$. Differentiate in two
steps:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial p_t}
=-\frac{y_t}{p_t}+\frac{1-y_t}{1-p_t},
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial a_t}=p_t(1-p_t).
$$

Multiplying and simplifying gives

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial a_t}=p_t-y_t,
\qquad
g_t=\nabla_w\ell_t(w_t)=(p_t-y_t)x_t.
$$

This compact gradient is important in a sparse system. If only 20 coordinates of $x_t$ are
non-zero, only those 20 coordinates have non-zero gradients.

## Regret: compare decisions, not datasets

Cumulative loss alone lacks a reference point. A loss of 1,000 may be excellent on a difficult
stream and terrible on an easy one. **Regret** compares the learner with a fixed decision $u$
chosen with hindsight:

$$
\operatorname{Regret}_T(u)
=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t(w_t)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t(u).
$$

The usual **external regret** uses the best fixed comparator in a set $\mathcal W$:

$$
R_T
=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t(w_t)
-\min_{u\in\mathcal W}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t(u).
$$

“Fixed” is essential. The comparator may be selected after seeing the whole sequence, but it must
use the same $u$ on every round. Comparing with a clairvoyant action that changes after seeing
each $y_t$ would ask an online learner to beat an impossible opponent.

An algorithm is **no-regret** when $R_T/T\to0$. Its total excess loss can grow, but slower than
the number of rounds, so the excess per prediction vanishes.<Sidenote>A regret guarantee is not a
claim that the loss itself vanishes. Noise may force both the learner and the comparator to make
mistakes.</Sidenote>

### A five-round calculation

Suppose the labels are $[1,1,0,1,0]$ and an online learner predicts
$[0.50,0.75,0.75,0.50,0.60]$.

| round $t$ | label $y_t$ | prediction $p_t$ | log loss |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 1 | 0.50 | 0.6931 |
| 2 | 1 | 0.75 | 0.2877 |
| 3 | 0 | 0.75 | 1.3863 |
| 4 | 1 | 0.50 | 0.6931 |
| 5 | 0 | 0.60 | 0.9163 |
| **learner total** | | | **3.9766** |

Restrict the comparator to one constant probability $q$. Its cumulative loss is

$$
L(q)=-3\log q-2\log(1-q).
$$

Set its derivative $-3/q+2/(1-q)$ to zero. The best constant is $q^*=3/5=0.6$, the empirical
click rate. It loses

$$
L(0.6)=-3\log(0.6)-2\log(0.4)=3.3651.
$$

Therefore $R_5=3.9766-3.3651=0.6115$, or $0.1223$ per round. Regret can be negative on a finite
sequence: an adaptive learner can outperform every *fixed* comparator.

## From Follow the Leader to FTRL

The most direct idea is **Follow the Leader** (FTL): on each round, choose the parameter that
would have performed best on all previous rounds,

$$
w_{t+1}=\arg\min_w\sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_s(w).
$$

FTL can be unstable. When two decisions have nearly equal historical loss, one new observation
can move the minimizer a long way. The learner then chases the newest winner. **Regularization**
adds a preference for controlled solutions:

$$
w_{t+1}
=\arg\min_w\left[
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_s(w)+R(w)
\right].
$$

This is **Follow the Regularized Leader**. A strongly convex $R$ gives the objective curvature,
so a small change in accumulated loss causes a small change in the minimizer.

In large models, repeatedly minimizing every original loss is still costly. For a convex loss,
the tangent at $w_t$ is a global lower bound:

$$
\ell_t(w)\geq \ell_t(w_t)+g_t^\top(w-w_t),
\qquad g_t=\nabla\ell_t(w_t).
$$

Terms independent of $w$ do not affect the minimizer. Replacing past losses with their linear
parts gives the linearized FTRL update

$$
w_{t+1}=\arg\min_w\left[g_{1:t}^\top w+R(w)\right],
\qquad g_{1:t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_s.
$$

The entire past is compressed into the gradient sum.

### A basic regret bound, step by step

Take $R(w)=\lVert w\rVert_2^2/(2\eta)$. Differentiating the FTRL objective gives

$$
g_{1:t}+\frac{1}{\eta}w_{t+1}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w_{t+1}=-\eta g_{1:t}.
$$

Since $w_t=-\eta g_{1:t-1}$, we also have the familiar gradient update
$w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta g_t$. FTRL and online gradient descent coincide in this particular case.

Convexity gives $\ell_t(w_t)-\ell_t(u)\leq g_t^\top(w_t-u)$. Substitute
$g_t=(w_t-w_{t+1})/\eta$ and use

$$
2(a-b)^\top(a-c)
=\lVert a-c\rVert^2-\lVert b-c\rVert^2+\lVert a-b\rVert^2.
$$

Then each round satisfies

$$
g_t^\top(w_t-u)
=\frac{\lVert w_t-u\rVert^2-\lVert w_{t+1}-u\rVert^2}{2\eta}
+\frac{\eta}{2}\lVert g_t\rVert^2.
$$

Sum over $t$. The squared-distance terms telescope. With $w_1=0$, dropping the final
non-positive term gives

$$
\operatorname{Regret}_T(u)
\leq \frac{\lVert u\rVert^2}{2\eta}
+\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert g_t\rVert^2.
$$

If $\lVert u\rVert\leq D$ and $\lVert g_t\rVert\leq G$, choose
$\eta=D/(G\sqrt T)$. Both terms balance, producing

$$
\operatorname{Regret}_T(u)\leq DG\sqrt T,
\qquad
\frac{\operatorname{Regret}_T(u)}{T}\leq\frac{DG}{\sqrt T}.
$$

This is a no-regret guarantee. It holds for every sequence satisfying the gradient bound; no
probability model for the data was needed.

## FTRL-Proximal for click prediction

Google's paper considers logistic regression with billions of sparse examples and an enormous
feature vocabulary. Two properties matter at that scale:

- frequent features should earn smaller learning rates than rare features;
- unhelpful features should have weights exactly equal to zero, saving memory and prediction work.

FTRL-Proximal combines a per-coordinate quadratic stabilizer with $L_1$ and $L_2$
regularization. In the paper's notation, after observing gradient $g_t$, it chooses

$$
w_{t+1}=\arg\min_w\left[
g_{1:t}^\top w
+\frac12\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_i\sigma_{s,i}(w_i-w_{s,i})^2
+\lambda_1\lVert w\rVert_1
+\frac{\lambda_2}{2}\lVert w\rVert_2^2
\right].
$$

The word **proximal** refers to the terms centred at old iterates $w_s$: moving away from a past
solution has a quadratic cost. The curvature increment is chosen as

$$
\sigma_{t,i}
=\frac{\sqrt{n_{t,i}+g_{t,i}^2}-\sqrt{n_{t,i}}}{\alpha},
\qquad
n_{t,i}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}g_{s,i}^2.
$$

Thus the accumulated curvature telescopes:

$$
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s,i}
=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^2}}{\alpha}.
$$

The implementation adds $\beta/\alpha$ to this curvature. The effective coordinate learning
rate is therefore

$$
\eta_{t,i}
=\frac{\alpha}{\beta+\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^2}}.
$$

A coordinate with many large gradients accumulates a large denominator and takes cautious
steps. A rare coordinate retains a larger step. This is the per-coordinate adaptation familiar
from AdaGrad, embedded inside FTRL.

### Deriving the closed-form weight

Because every term is separable by coordinate, solve for one $w_i$. Expand the proximal sum:

$$
\frac12\sum_s\sigma_{s,i}(w_i-w_{s,i})^2
=\frac12\left(\sum_s\sigma_{s,i}\right)w_i^2
-\left(\sum_s\sigma_{s,i}w_{s,i}\right)w_i+C,
$$

where $C$ does not depend on $w_i$. Define

$$
z_{t,i}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sigma_{s,i}w_{s,i},
\qquad
A_{t,i}=\lambda_2+\frac{\beta+\sqrt{\sum_{s=1}^{t}g_{s,i}^2}}{\alpha}.
$$

The one-coordinate objective is now only

$$
\phi_i(w_i)=z_{t,i}w_i+\lambda_1|w_i|+\frac12A_{t,i}w_i^2+C.
$$

There are three cases.

1. If $w_i>0$, then $\partial|w_i|=1$. Setting the derivative to zero gives
   $w_i=-(z_{t,i}+\lambda_1)/A_{t,i}$, valid when $z_{t,i}<-\lambda_1$.
2. If $w_i<0$, then $\partial|w_i|=-1$. This gives
   $w_i=-(z_{t,i}-\lambda_1)/A_{t,i}$, valid when $z_{t,i}>\lambda_1$.
3. At $w_i=0$, the subgradient of $|w_i|$ is the interval $[-1,1]$. Zero is optimal when
   $0\in z_{t,i}+\lambda_1[-1,1]$, or $|z_{t,i}|\leq\lambda_1$.

Combining the cases yields the paper's lazy weight formula:

$$
w_{t+1,i}=
\begin{cases}
0, & |z_{t,i}|\leq\lambda_1,\\[4pt]
-\dfrac{z_{t,i}-\operatorname{sign}(z_{t,i})\lambda_1}
{(\beta+\sqrt{n_{t+1,i}})/\alpha+\lambda_2},
& |z_{t,i}|>\lambda_1.
\end{cases}
$$

The flat middle is why FTRL produces exact sparsity. Small, inconsistent gradients can cancel
inside $[-\lambda_1,\lambda_1]$ without allocating a non-zero weight. Once the evidence crosses
the threshold, the weight grows linearly after subtracting the threshold.

### Deriving the state updates

Recomputing the sums in $z_{t,i}$ would defeat online learning. Take the difference between two
successive accumulators:

$$
z_{t,i}-z_{t-1,i}=g_{t,i}-\sigma_{t,i}w_{t,i}.
$$

So each active coordinate needs only two stored values:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{t,i}
&=\frac{\sqrt{n_i+g_{t,i}^2}-\sqrt{n_i}}{\alpha},\\
z_i&\leftarrow z_i+g_{t,i}-\sigma_{t,i}w_{t,i},\\
n_i&\leftarrow n_i+g_{t,i}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

At prediction time, compute $w_i$ from $z_i$ and $n_i$ only for coordinates present in $x_t$.
This **lazy regularization** is crucial: the system does not sweep over billions of absent
features after every impression.

## A complete five-impression update

Use one feature with value $x_t=1$ on every round and labels $[1,0,1,1,0]$. Choose illustrative
hyperparameters $\alpha=1$, $\beta=1$, $\lambda_1=0.5$, and $\lambda_2=0.1$, starting from
$z=n=0$. These values are chosen to make the threshold visible; they are not Google's production
settings.

The gradient is $g_t=p_t-y_t$. Each row first derives $w_t$ from the old state, predicts, and
then updates $z$ and $n$.

| $t$ | $y_t$ | $w_t$ | $p_t$ | $g_t$ | $\sigma_t$ | new $z$ | new $n$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 1 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 | -0.500000 | 0.500000 | -0.500000 | 0.250000 |
| 2 | 0 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 | 0.500000 | 0.207107 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 |
| 3 | 1 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 | -0.500000 | 0.158919 | -0.500000 | 0.750000 |
| 4 | 1 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 | -0.500000 | 0.133975 | -1.000000 | 1.000000 |
| 5 | 0 | 0.238095 | 0.559244 | 0.559244 | 0.145755 | -0.475459 | 1.312754 |

Check round 1. The initial logit is zero, so $p_1=\sigma(0)=0.5$ and
$g_1=0.5-1=-0.5$. Then

$$
\sigma_1=\sqrt{0+(-0.5)^2}-\sqrt0=0.5,
\quad
z\leftarrow0-0.5-0.5(0)=-0.5,
\quad
n\leftarrow0.25.
$$

Because $|z|=\lambda_1$, the next weight remains exactly zero. The opposite gradient on round 2
returns $z$ to zero. This is the sparsity mechanism doing useful work: contradictory evidence
does not make the parameter jitter around zero.

After round 4, $z=-1$ crosses the threshold. The round-5 weight is

$$
w_5
=-\frac{-1-(-1)(0.5)}{(1+\sqrt1)/1+0.1}
=\frac{0.5}{2.1}
=0.238095.
$$

Check the prediction: $\sigma(0.238095)=0.559244$. The non-click gives
$g_5=0.559244$, pulling the accumulator back to $-0.475459$; because that is inside the dead
zone, the following weight becomes zero again.

## What the Google paper adds in practice

The derivation explains the optimizer, but the “trenches” paper is valuable because it connects
the optimizer to system constraints. Its deployed click-through-rate (CTR) models made billions
of predictions per day from extremely sparse inputs. FTRL-Proximal offered strong online accuracy
and sparsity, while per-coordinate rates handled features observed at very different frequencies.

The paper also reports engineering ideas around the algorithm: hashing feature identifiers,
probabilistic storage to save memory, training several models in one pass, evaluating performance
on slices, estimating prediction uncertainty, and calibrating probabilities. These are not part
of the FTRL derivation, but they are part of making an online learner useful. A small optimizer
improvement can be irrelevant if model state no longer fits in memory or serving cannot reproduce
the training features.

Three cautions keep the method in perspective:

- Low regret is relative to a comparator class. A linear model cannot regret its way into learning
  interactions that its features do not express.
- $L_1$ sparsity depends on feature scaling and $\lambda_1$. A zero weight means the accumulated
  evidence has not crossed the optimizer's threshold, not that the feature is universally useless.
- Online updates react quickly to drift, but they also react to corrupted labels and pipeline
  bugs. Monitoring, replay, and rollback remain necessary.

## Summary

- Online learning predicts first, observes the outcome second, and updates without retraining from
  scratch.
- Regret compares cumulative learner loss with the best fixed decision in hindsight; sublinear
  regret makes the average gap vanish.
- Quadratic FTRL recovers online gradient descent and gives an $O(\sqrt T)$ regret bound under
  bounded comparators and gradients.
- FTRL-Proximal combines adaptive coordinate-wise curvature, $L_1$ sparsity, and $L_2$ shrinkage.
- Expanding its one-coordinate objective produces a soft-thresholded closed form, while the
  $z_i,n_i$ state makes sparse updates lazy and cheap.
