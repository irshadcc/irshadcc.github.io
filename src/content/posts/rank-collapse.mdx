## Background

A hidden layer may have 1,024 neurons and still carry only a handful of independent signals.
Width counts coordinates; **rank** counts the independent directions in which a batch of
representations actually varies. When that number becomes much smaller than the width, the
representation has suffered **rank collapse**.

This matters because the lost directions can carry distinctions that a later layer needs. If
two inputs end up at (almost) the same hidden vector, every layer after it treats them (almost)
the same. Training slows, features become redundant, and some targets become impossible to fit.
The surprising part is that no single layer has to be broken for this to happen: a stack of
perfectly invertible layers can collapse a representation on its own.

This post first defines rank and two softer measures of it. It then follows twelve points
through a deep network whose only layer is a gentle, invertible mixing of two neurons, and
watches the collapse happen one layer at a time. It repeats the experiment on 64-wide random
networks to see which design choices (orthogonal weights, residual connections, batch
normalisation) prevent it, and ends with how to measure collapse in a real model and when low
rank is actually what you want.

## What collapses?

Put a batch of $n$ hidden vectors into a matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, one example per
row and one feature per column. Its **singular value decomposition** (SVD) writes it as

$$
H = U\Sigma V^\top, \qquad
\operatorname{rank}(H) = \#\{i : \sigma_i > 0\}.
$$

The columns of $V$ are directions in feature space, and the singular value $\sigma_i$ says how
much the batch spreads along the $i$-th one. If only one singular value is non-zero, every row
lies on a line. If two are, the rows lie in a plane.

Exact rank is brittle in floating-point arithmetic: a singular value of $10^{-12}$ is technically
non-zero but carries no usable signal. Two softer measures avoid an arbitrary cut-off. The
**stable rank** compares the total spread with the largest direction, and the **effective rank**
is the exponential of the entropy of the normalised singular values:

$$
\operatorname{srank}(H) = \frac{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}{\sigma_1^2},
\qquad
\operatorname{erank}(H) = \exp\!\left(-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\right),
\quad p_i = \frac{\sigma_i}{\sum_j \sigma_j}.
$$

Both equal $d$ when all $d$ singular values are equal, and fall to 1 as one direction comes to
dominate. Centre $H$ (subtract the mean row) before measuring if the question is how examples
differ from each other; otherwise a large shared offset shows up as one big direction.<Sidenote>The
rank of the weights and the rank of the activations are related but not the same. "Rank
collapse" can mean either, so a measurement should always name its matrix. This post measures
activations.</Sidenote>

### The obvious way to lose a direction

For a linear layer applied to a batch, $Z = XW^\top$, the rank can only go down:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(Z) \le \min\big(\operatorname{rank}(X),\operatorname{rank}(W)\big).
$$

If $W$ is singular it has a **null space**: directions $v$ with $Wv = 0$. Inputs $x$ and $x + v$
then produce the same output, and no later layer can tell which one arrived. For example,
$W = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ sends $(0.3, 0)$ and $(0.3, 5)$ to the same vector $(0.3, 0)$.

This is real but rare. A randomly initialised weight matrix is invertible with probability one,
and training seldom drives a square matrix exactly singular. The rest of the post is about the
more common route, in which every layer is invertible and the collapse comes from depth.

## Collapse with no singular layer

### A layer that mixes two neurons

Take a network with two neurons per layer and no activation function, so that each layer is
$h \leftarrow W h$ with the same weight matrix at every depth:

$$
W = \begin{bmatrix} 5/6 & 1/6 \\ 1/6 & 5/6 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each neuron keeps five sixths of its own value and takes one sixth of its neighbour's. Nothing
about this layer looks dangerous. Its determinant is $2/3$, so it is invertible; its condition
number is $1.5$; it never increases a vector's length. The only thing it does is treat two
directions differently:

$$
W u = u \;\text{ for }\; u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1),
\qquad
W v = \tfrac{2}{3}\, v \;\text{ for }\; v = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1).
$$

The **agreement** of the two neurons (their sum, along $u$) passes through unchanged. Their
**disagreement** (their difference, along $v$) shrinks by a third. Every row of $W$ is a
weighted average with positive weights, which is also what self-attention computes across
tokens; we come back to that below.<Sidenote>Using the same $W$ at every depth makes the network a power
iteration, the textbook algorithm for finding a matrix's dominant eigenvector, and power
iteration is designed to collapse everything onto one direction. Different random matrices per
layer collapse too, as the 64-wide experiment below shows; the directions just move around.</Sidenote>

The input batch is twelve points on the unit circle, $30°$ apart. Stacked as rows they form a
$12 \times 2$ matrix $X$ with $X^\top X = 6I$, so both singular values are $\sqrt6 \approx 2.449$:
the batch spreads equally in every direction, and its effective rank is exactly 2.

### Working through the numbers

Write an input as $x = a\,u + b\,v$, its agreement and disagreement parts. Since $u$ and $v$ are
eigenvectors of $W$, applying $L$ layers is easy:

$$
W^L x = a\,u + \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{L} b\,v.
$$

Check it on the $0°$ input $x = (1, 0)$, where $a = b = 1/\sqrt2$. After one layer,
$a u + \tfrac23 b v = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac23(\tfrac12, -\tfrac12) = (\tfrac56, \tfrac16)$,
which is the first column of $W$, as it should be. The $90°$ input $(0, 1)$ has the same $a$ and
the opposite $b$, so the gap between the pair is $\sqrt2\,(2/3)^L$.

The batch inherits the same factor. The circle spreads equally along $u$ and $v$, so
$\sigma_1 = \sqrt6$ at every depth and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt6\,(2/3)^L$. The table collects the
figure's numbers plus the stable rank: effective rank (erank), stable rank (srank), exact rank, the distance
between the $0°$ and $90°$ points (gap), and the number of distinct points after rounding each
coordinate to three decimals.

| 0 | 1 | 2.000 | 2.000 | 2 | 1.414 | 12 |
| 1 | 0.667 | 1.960 | 1.444 | 2 | 0.943 | 12 |
| 2 | 0.444 | 1.854 | 1.198 | 2 | 0.629 | 12 |
| 4 | 0.198 | 1.565 | 1.039 | 2 | 0.279 | 12 |
| 8 | 0.0390 | 1.174 | 1.002 | 2 | 0.0552 | 12 |
| 16 | 0.00152 | 1.011 | 1.000 | 2 | 0.00215 | 10 |
| 24 | 5.9e-5 | 1.001 | 1.000 | 2 | 8.4e-5 | 6 |
| 32 | 2.3e-6 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 2 | 3.3e-6 | 6 |

Two things stand out. First, a mild per-layer effect compounds: losing a third of the
disagreement per layer sounds harmless, but $(2/3)^{16}$ is $1/657$ and $(2/3)^{32}$ is
$1/431{,}440$. Second, the exact rank never moves from 2, while the effective and stable ranks
report the collapse from the first few layers. This is the reason to track a soft measure.

### Why the lost direction is gone in practice

In exact arithmetic nothing is lost: $W$ is invertible, so $W^{-L}$ would recover the input.
A real network cannot use that escape hatch.

- **Noise wins.** At depth 16 the disagreement part of every point is at most $0.0015$. Any
  perturbation of that size along $v$, from rounding in low precision, dropout or the input
  itself, is as large as the signal. To read $b$ back, a later layer would need a gain of
  $(3/2)^{16} \approx 657$ along $v$, which amplifies the noise by the same factor.
- **Gradients shrink by the same factor.** The layer's Jacobian is $W$, which is symmetric, so
  backpropagation scales the $v$ component of the gradient by $2/3$ per layer as well. A loss
  that depends on telling $0°$ from $90°$ sends back a gradient $657$ times weaker along $v$ at
  depth 16 than at depth 0, and the earlier layers barely learn the distinction.

So rank collapse is not about the matrix becoming singular. It is about the spread of the
batch becoming so uneven that the small directions are useless, for the forward pass and the
backward pass alike.

### Reproducing the table

```python
import numpy as np

theta = np.deg2rad(np.arange(12) * 30)
X = np.stack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)], axis=1)   # 12 x 2, one point per row
W = np.array([[5 / 6, 1 / 6], [1 / 6, 5 / 6]])

for L in (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32):
    H = X @ np.linalg.matrix_power(W, L).T
    s = np.linalg.svd(H - H.mean(axis=0), compute_uv=False)
    p = s / s.sum()
    erank = np.exp(-(p * np.log(p)).sum())
    srank = (s**2).sum() / s[0] ** 2
    gap = np.linalg.norm(H[0] - H[3])                  # 0 degrees vs 90 degrees
    distinct = len(np.unique(np.round(H, 3), axis=0))
    print(L, s[1] / s[0], erank, srank, np.linalg.matrix_rank(H), gap, distinct)
```

## The same effect at width 64

The two-neuron network makes the mechanism visible. To see it at a more realistic scale, push
a batch of 256 Gaussian inputs through 128 layers that are 64 neurons wide, with a fresh
random weight matrix in every layer, and measure the effective rank of the centred batch after
each one. Five variants differ in a single design choice:

- **Linear**: $h \leftarrow W h$, with entries of $W$ drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1/64)$ so that
  vector lengths are preserved on average.
- **ReLU**: $h \leftarrow \operatorname{ReLU}(W h)$, with He initialisation, $\mathcal{N}(0, 2/64)$.
- **Orthogonal**: $h \leftarrow Q h$, with $Q$ a random orthogonal matrix.
- **Residual**: $h \leftarrow h + W h / \sqrt{128}$, the linear layer added to an identity path.
- **Batch norm**: the linear layer followed by **batch normalisation**, which rescales every
  feature to zero mean and unit variance over the batch.

The input starts at 61.8 rather than 64 because 256 random samples do not spread exactly
evenly over 64 directions. From there:

- **Linear** layers lose a fifth of the effective rank in the very first layer and are down to
  4.4 directions out of 64 at depth 64. Each random matrix stretches some directions more than
  others, and as in the two-neuron case the differences multiply.<Sidenote>For products of
  random matrices the growth rate along each direction converges to a fixed number, a result
  of Furstenberg and Kesten (1960). Unequal rates mean the ratio between directions changes
  exponentially with depth, just like $(2/3)^L$ above.</Sidenote>
- **ReLU** collapses faster still, to 1.7 at depth 64. ReLU zeroes every negative
  coordinate, which pushes all activations into the same orthant and makes them more alike.
  This curve varies the most between random seeds: over five seeds its depth-64 value ranged
  from 1.7 to 7.0, while the linear one stayed between 3.6 and 4.8.
- **Orthogonal** layers keep all 61.8: the rank does not move at all.
- **Residual** layers keep 54.6 at depth 64, and fall only to 48.0 at depth 128.
- **Batch norm** does not stop the early drop, but it levels off at about 12 instead of falling
  towards 1.

The last three are the standard countermeasures. The section on prevention below explains why
each one works, and where it stops working.

These are untrained networks at initialisation, one seed each (seed 0, NumPy's
`default_rng`). They show the starting point that training has to work from, not what a
trained model ends up with.

### Why optimisation suffers

When many examples have nearly parallel hidden vectors, their gradients with respect to the next
weight matrix are nearly parallel too. The updates from different examples then point the same
way, and fitting different targets for different examples becomes hard. At effective rank 1 the
layer exposes essentially one feature to the rest of the network.

Small singular values also make the network ill-conditioned, as the two-neuron example showed:
the backward pass shrinks gradients along the collapsed directions by the same factor as the
forward pass. This links rank collapse to vanishing gradients, but the two are not the same.
Rank describes how varied a batch of representations is; gradient size describes how sensitive
the loss is.

## Related collapses, and when low rank helps

**Neural collapse** is a more specific, late-training phenomenon in supervised classification.
Within each class, last-layer features converge to their class mean, and the class means and
the classifier settle into a highly symmetric arrangement. Rank collapse is the broader loss of
independent directions in a weight or activation matrix. Neural collapse produces
low-dimensional features, but a low-rank hidden layer is not by itself evidence of neural
collapse.

In Transformers, **self-attention** collapses rank by the same mechanism as the mixing layer:
each output token is a weighted average of value vectors with positive weights. Dong et al.
prove that a stack of attention layers with no skip connections or feed-forward layers loses
rank doubly exponentially with depth, so that all tokens converge to the same vector. Skip
connections play the same role as the residual variant above.

Low rank is also not automatically bad. A model should throw away directions that carry noise
or irrelevant detail. In the mixing example, if the task only needs the sum of the two inputs
(the $u$ direction), the collapse loses nothing and the network has found the smallest sufficient
representation. Low-rank adapters and matrix factorisation rely on this idea to cut parameters.
The useful question is not "is the rank small?" but "did the model keep the directions the task
needs?"

## Diagnosing rank collapse

Rank collapse is invisible in the loss curve until it is severe. The way to see it is to
measure the spread of the activations directly, layer by layer, and then decide whether the
loss of directions actually hurts the task.

### Measure a rank profile

A **rank profile** is the effective rank of every layer's output for one fixed batch, plotted
against depth. Five rules make it trustworthy:

1. **Use many more rows than the width.** After centring, a batch of $n$ rows has rank at
   most $n - 1$, whatever the network does. Pure Gaussian noise of width 64 has effective rank
   28.6 with 32 rows, 50.9 with 64, 61.9 with 256 and 63.5 with 1,024, so a small batch looks
   collapsed before any layer has run. For a Transformer, stack every token of every sequence as
   a row.
2. **Fix the batch.** Use the same held-out examples at every measurement, so a change in the
   profile comes from the model and not from the data.
3. **Centre each column**, so a shared offset (such as the positive mean that ReLU adds) does
   not show up as one dominant direction.
4. **Compute in float64.** Small singular values are exactly what you are looking for, and
   low-precision SVD rounds them away.
5. **Measure at initialisation and during training.** The profile at step 0 shows what the
   architecture does on its own; later profiles show what training does.

PyTorch forward hooks collect the profile without changing the model:

```python
import torch
from torch import nn


def effective_rank(h):
    h = h.reshape(-1, h.shape[-1]).double()  # one row per example (or token)
    s = torch.linalg.svdvals(h - h.mean(dim=0))
    p = s / s.sum()
    p = p[p > 0]
    return torch.exp(-(p * p.log()).sum()).item()


def rank_profile(model, batch, layer_type):
    """Effective rank of the output of every `layer_type` module, in forward order."""
    ranks = []
    hooks = [
        m.register_forward_hook(lambda mod, inp, out: ranks.append(effective_rank(out)))
        for m in model.modules()
        if isinstance(m, layer_type)
    ]
    with torch.no_grad():
        model(batch)
    for h in hooks:
        h.remove()
    return ranks
```

To try it, build a 32-block, 64-wide ReLU network twice: once as plain blocks
$h \leftarrow \operatorname{ReLU}(Wh)$, and once with a residual connection
$h \leftarrow h + \operatorname{ReLU}(Wh)/\sqrt{32}$. Both use He initialisation and no bias, and
the batch is 1,024 Gaussian rows (effective rank 63.5):

```python
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, width, residual):
        super().__init__()
        self.linear = nn.Linear(width, width, bias=False)
        nn.init.normal_(self.linear.weight, std=(2 / width) ** 0.5)  # He initialisation
        self.residual = residual

    def forward(self, h):
        out = torch.relu(self.linear(h))
        return h + out / 32**0.5 if self.residual else out


torch.manual_seed(0)
batch = torch.randn(1024, 64)  # many more rows than the width
for residual in (False, True):
    model = nn.Sequential(*[Block(64, residual) for _ in range(32)])
    print(rank_profile(model, batch, Block))
```

With seed 0 it prints:

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Plain | 59.2 | 47.0 | 34.7 | 24.5 | 10.1 |

seed before you trust a single number.

### Read the profile

The shape of the curve tells you where to look.

- **A steady decline with depth** is the compounding of the earlier sections: no single layer
  is to blame, so the fix is architectural (residual connections, normalisation,
  initialisation).
- **A cliff at one layer** points at that layer. Check the singular values of its weight
  matrix (a near-zero one means a near-null space), and, after a ReLU, the fraction of **dead
  units**, which output zero for every example in the batch and so contribute no direction at all.
- **A profile that falls during training** may be healthy compression rather than collapse. The
  next check decides which.

### Decide whether it hurts

Low rank is a problem only if the discarded directions carried something the task needs, so
pair the profile with a measurement of the task:

- **A linear probe**: train a linear classifier on a layer's activations to predict the label. If
  the probe's accuracy drops at the same layer as the effective rank, that layer is discarding
  useful information.
- **Per-layer gradient norms**: collapsed directions also shrink the backward pass, as the
  two-neuron example showed. Early layers whose gradients are orders of magnitude smaller than
  late layers' are learning slowly.
- **The task metric itself**: if two variants with different rank profiles reach the same
  validation accuracy, the lower rank is compression, not damage.

## Preventing rank collapse

Every countermeasure attacks the cause found earlier. A deep network multiplies many layers
together, and the ratio between its strongest and weakest directions multiplies too. There are
two ways to stop that: keep every layer's singular values close together, so there is little
to multiply, or put the batch's spread back in order every few layers.

The two-neuron mixing layer is a good test, because the effect of each fix can be computed
exactly. Its plain version has $\sigma_2/\sigma_1 = 0.00152$ at depth 16.

| Countermeasure | What it changes | $\sigma_2/\sigma_1$ at depth 16 | Effective rank at depth 16 |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| None | — | 0.00152 | 1.011 |
| Orthogonal weights | every singular value is 1 | 1 | 2.000 |
| Residual, $\varepsilon = 0.1$ | layer is $I + \varepsilon W$ | 0.611 | 1.942 |
| Residual, $\varepsilon = 1$ | layer is $I + W$ | 0.0541 | 1.224 |
| Batch normalisation | each feature rescaled | 0.00152 | 1.011 |
| Whitening | covariance reset to $I$ | 1 | 2.000 |

### Orthogonal initialisation

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies $Q^\top Q = I$, so all its singular values equal 1. It
rotates (or reflects) the batch without stretching any direction, and $HQ^\top$ has exactly the
same singular values as $H$. A stack of orthogonal layers is therefore a stack of rotations:
nothing compounds, and the 64-wide orthogonal network above holds 61.8 at every depth. Saxe et
al. showed that deep linear networks initialised this way train at a speed that does not depend
on depth.

Two limits apply. Orthogonality holds only at initialisation; training moves the weights away
from it. And a nonlinearity breaks it: ReLU zeroes about half of every vector, which is not a
rotation. The 64-wide ReLU network with orthogonal weights (scaled by $\sqrt2$, as He
initialisation scales Gaussians) still falls to 7.7 at depth 64, against 1.7 with Gaussian
weights. That is better, but it does not prevent collapse.<Sidenote>Pennington, Schoenholz and
Ganguli show that ReLU networks cannot reach **dynamical isometry** (all singular values of the
input-output Jacobian near 1) even with orthogonal weights, while sigmoid-like networks
can.</Sidenote>

### Residual connections

A residual block computes $h \leftarrow h + \varepsilon f(h)$: the input passes through
unchanged and the layer only adds a correction. For a linear $f(h) = Ah$ the block is the
matrix $I + \varepsilon A$, whose singular values all lie between $1 - \varepsilon\lVert A\rVert$
and $1 + \varepsilon\lVert A\rVert$. With a small $\varepsilon$, every direction is stretched
by nearly the same amount, so there is little to compound.

In the mixing example, $I + \varepsilon W$ keeps $u$ with factor $1 + \varepsilon$ and $v$ with
factor $1 + \tfrac23\varepsilon$, so the ratio per layer is

$$
\frac{1 + \tfrac23\varepsilon}{1 + \varepsilon}
= 0.970 \;\text{ for } \varepsilon = 0.1,
\qquad 0.833 \;\text{ for } \varepsilon = 1,
$$

against $0.667$ with no skip connection. Raised to the 16th power, that is the difference
between $0.611$ and $0.00152$. The chart below shows the effective rank across depth.

The smaller the residual branch, the slower the collapse. This is why deep residual networks
scale the branch down, for example by $1/\sqrt{L}$ as in the 64-wide experiment, or by
initialising the last layer of each branch near zero. The same holds in Transformers: Dong et
al. show that skip connections are what stops a stack of attention layers from converging to
rank 1.

Residual connections slow collapse; they do not stop it. The 64-wide residual network still
falls from 61.8 to 48.0 over 128 layers.

### Normalisation, and where it stops

**Batch normalisation** (BN) subtracts each feature's mean over the batch and divides by its
standard deviation. In the random 64-wide network, collapse shows up partly as some features
shrinking relative to others, and BN puts every feature back at unit variance after every
layer. That is enough to put a floor under the effective rank: about 12 in the experiment above,
and Daneshmand et al. prove a lower bound on the rank for randomly initialised linear networks
with BN.

BN equalises the **variance** of each feature. It does nothing about the **correlation**
between features. In the mixing example the two neurons keep equal variance at every depth,
while their correlation climbs to $0.997$ by depth 8. BN therefore multiplies the whole batch
by one number, and the collapse is identical to the plain version, as the table shows.

**Whitening** goes one step further: it multiplies the centred batch by $C^{-1/2}$, where $C$ is
the batch's covariance matrix, so the new covariance is exactly $I$. All singular values become
equal and the effective rank returns to its maximum, here 2 at every depth. Two costs keep it
out of most networks. It needs an eigendecomposition of a $d \times d$ matrix at every step,
and dividing by the square root of a tiny eigenvalue multiplies whatever noise sits in that
direction. Whitening can restore a direction only while its signal is still above the noise.
At depth 32 of the mixing example, the $v$ component is $2.3 \times 10^{-6}$ of the $u$
component, far below the rounding error of bfloat16 (up to $2^{-8} \approx 0.4\%$ of a value), so in low
precision whitening would mostly amplify rounding error.

### Decorrelation losses

Instead of whitening inside the network, a loss can push the output towards a whitened
covariance. Self-supervised learning uses this. There, an encoder is trained to give two
augmented views of the same image similar embeddings, and the embeddings tend to end up in a
low-dimensional subspace of the embedding space. Jing et al. call this **dimensional collapse**
and show that it happens even in contrastive methods, which also push different images apart.

- **Barlow Twins** pushes the cross-correlation matrix between the embeddings of two views
  towards the identity: diagonal entries to 1, off-diagonal entries to 0.
- **VICReg** adds a variance term that keeps every feature's standard deviation above a
  threshold, and a covariance term that penalises the squared off-diagonal entries of the
  covariance matrix.

Both work for the reason whitening does. A covariance that is diagonal with equal entries is a
multiple of $I$, every singular value is equal, and the effective rank is maximal. A soft
penalty gets most of that effect without an eigendecomposition.

### Spectral and orthogonality penalties

To keep weights well conditioned during training, add a penalty to the loss:

$$
\lambda \,\lVert W^\top W - I \rVert_F^2 = \lambda \sum_i \left(\sigma_i^2 - 1\right)^2.
$$

Each term is zero only when $\sigma_i = 1$, so the gradient shrinks large singular values and
grows small ones, pulling the layer back towards orthogonal. For the mixing layer, with
singular values 1 and $2/3$, the penalty is $(1 - 4/9)^2 = 0.309$, all of it from the shrunk
direction.

**Spectral normalisation** divides $W$ by its largest singular value, so that
$\sigma_1 = 1$. It stops any one direction from growing, but it does not lift the small
singular values, so on its own it does not prevent collapse.

### Watch the weight decay

Weight decay (an $L_2$ penalty on the weights) pushes towards low rank in deep networks. For
two layers $W_2W_1$, the smallest possible value of
$\lVert W_1\rVert_F^2 + \lVert W_2\rVert_F^2$ over all factorisations of the same product is
$2\lVert W_2W_1\rVert_*$, twice the **nuclear norm**, which is the sum of the singular values.
Penalising a sum of singular values works like an $L_1$ penalty: it drives the small ones to
zero. Zangrando et al. prove that weight decay leads deep networks to low-rank weights.
If the diagnosis shows harmful collapse in a model trained with strong weight decay, lowering it
(or removing it from the affected layers) is a cheap experiment.

## Summary

- Rank collapse means a batch of representations varies along far fewer directions than the
  layer width; the effective and stable ranks measure it, exact rank does not.
- It does not need a singular layer. A mixing layer that keeps the agreement of two neurons and
  shrinks their disagreement by a third is invertible, yet after 16 layers the disagreement is
  657 times smaller and after 32 layers 431,440 times smaller.
- Small directions are lost in practice because noise of the same size swamps them and
  gradients along them shrink by the same factor.
- In 64-wide random networks, plain linear and ReLU stacks collapse to a few directions within
  64 layers; orthogonal weights preserve the full rank, residual connections slow the collapse,
  and batch normalisation puts a floor under it.
- Diagnose with a rank profile: the effective rank of every layer on a fixed batch with many
  more rows than the width, checked against a linear probe or the task metric.
- Prevent it by keeping each layer close to an isometry (orthogonal initialisation, small
  residual branches, orthogonality penalties) or by re-equalising the spread (batch
  normalisation for per-feature scale, whitening or decorrelation losses for correlation).
- Low rank is useful compression when the discarded directions are irrelevant to the task. It
  becomes collapse when it removes information the task needs or stalls optimisation.
