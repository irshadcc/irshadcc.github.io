## Background

Adding noise to a picture is easy: add a random number to every pixel. Do it a thousand times
and any picture becomes static that looks like every other static. Going the other way is the
hard part. Given static, which picture did it come from? A **diffusion model** is a neural
network trained to undo noise one small step at a time. Start it from pure static, let it run
for a few hundred steps, and a new picture appears.

This one idea is behind most image, video and audio generators released since 2021 (DALL·E 2,
Imagen, Stable Diffusion, Sora). It matters for two reasons. First, the training loss is
almost embarrassingly simple: add noise to a training example and ask the network to predict
the noise, with a mean squared error. Second, sampling is slow, because it takes many network
calls, and most of the engineering since the first papers has been about cutting that number.
Both facts fall out of the math, so it pays to see where they come from.

This post builds the model from scratch, in order:

1. **The forward process**: how noise is added, and the closed form that lets us jump to any
   step.
2. **The reverse process**: why removing a little noise is a Gaussian step, and what its mean
   is when the clean data are known.
3. **The training objective**: the evidence lower bound, and how it simplifies to "predict the
   noise".
4. **What the network learns**: the optimal denoiser, worked out exactly for a tiny dataset,
   and its link to the **score** $\nabla \log q$.
5. **Sampling**: the DDPM and DDIM samplers, and what happens with fewer steps.

Every figure runs on data small enough to solve exactly: a mixture of a few Gaussians in one
or two dimensions. For such data the ideal network has a closed form, so the figures use it in
place of a trained network, and what you see is the algorithm itself rather than the quality
of a fit.<Sidenote>Every number in this post was computed by a script, from the same
TypeScript module the figures run (`src/utils/diffusion/diffusion.ts`). The closed forms were
cross-checked by Monte Carlo in NumPy, and the optimal denoiser against a small network trained
with PyTorch 2.9.1 (see [A trained network](#a-trained-network-against-the-exact-answer)).</Sidenote>
The post leans on Langevin sampling from the [sampling post](/blog/sampling-methods/) for one
connection, but does not need it.

## The forward process

### One noising step

Write $\mathbf{x}_0$ for a data point (an image flattened to a vector, say). The **forward
process** builds a chain $\mathbf{x}_0 \to \mathbf{x}_1 \to \dots \to \mathbf{x}_T$ by
repeatedly shrinking the current point a little and adding a little Gaussian noise:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t,
\qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}).
$$

As a distribution, $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t;
\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t \mathbf{I})$. The numbers $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$
are the **noise schedule**: small variances, fixed in advance. Ho et al.'s DDPM uses $T = 1000$
steps with $\beta_t$ rising linearly from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$.<Sidenote>J. Ho, A. Jain and P.
Abbeel, "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models", NeurIPS 2020,
[arXiv:2006.11239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239). The model goes back to Sohl-Dickstein et
al., 2015, [arXiv:1503.03585](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585).</Sidenote> The forward process
has no parameters. Nothing in it is learned.

Why shrink by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ rather than just add noise? Because it keeps the variance in
check. If $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ has unit variance, then $\mathbf{x}_t$ has variance
$(1 - \beta_t) + \beta_t = 1$. Data scaled to unit variance stay at unit variance, and the
chain heads for $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ instead of blowing up. The strip below
shows the chain on 400 points from a ring of eight small clusters: the forward arrow (orange)
destroys the structure, and the job of the model is the reverse arrow (purple).

### Jumping to any step

Running 250 steps to get $\mathbf{x}_{250}$ would make training slow. Fortunately we do not
have to. Define $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and the running product

$$
\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s .
$$

Then

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\right),
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.
$$

**Why it holds.** Unroll two steps:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}\big) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-2} + \underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t}_{\text{sum of independent Gaussians}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

A sum of independent zero-mean Gaussians is a Gaussian whose variance is the sum of the
variances: $\alpha_t - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. So two
steps are one step with signal $\sqrt{\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}}$ and noise variance
$1 - \alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. Repeat down to $\mathbf{x}_0$ and the products become
$\bar\alpha_t$.

The number $\bar\alpha_t$ is the whole story of the schedule. The point $\mathbf{x}_t$ is a
blend of $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ parts signal and $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ parts noise, and the
**signal-to-noise ratio** is $\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$.

**Worked example.** Take a one-dimensional data point $x_0 = 1$, the linear schedule, and
$t = 250$. The script gives $\bar\alpha_{250} = 0.5241$, so $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{250}} = 0.7239$
and $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{250}} = 0.6899$. With the noise draw $\epsilon = 0.8$:

$$
x_{250} = 0.7239 \times 1 + 0.6899 \times 0.8 = 1.2758 .
$$

About half of the original variance is left ($\bar\alpha = 0.52$, SNR $= 1.10$). Running the
chain one step at a time from $x_0 = 1$ for 200,000 independent copies gives a mean of
$0.7253$ and a variance of $0.4784$ at $t = 250$, against the closed form's $0.7239$ and
$1 - 0.5241 = 0.4759$.

### Noise schedules

The schedule decides how fast information is destroyed. The table lists $\bar\alpha_t$ for
DDPM's linear schedule and for the **cosine schedule** of Nichol and Dhariwal, which sets
$\bar\alpha_t = f(t)/f(0)$ with $f(t) = \cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{t/T + s}{1+s}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)$
and $s = 0.008$.<Sidenote>A. Nichol and P. Dhariwal, "Improved Denoising Diffusion
Probabilistic Models", ICML 2021, [arXiv:2102.09672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672),
eq. 17. The $\beta_t$ it implies are clipped at $0.999$ so the last step stays
finite.</Sidenote>

## The reverse process

### Why removing a little noise is a Gaussian step

To generate data we want to run the chain backwards: draw $\mathbf{x}_T \sim
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, then $\mathbf{x}_{T-1}$, and so on down to
$\mathbf{x}_0$. That needs the **reverse conditional** $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$.
By Bayes' rule it is

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t) \propto q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})\, q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}),
$$

and the marginal $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ depends on the whole data distribution, so in general
this is intractable. The key fact that makes diffusion work: **when $\beta_t$ is small, the
reverse conditional is close to a Gaussian.** The likelihood term
$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ is a narrow Gaussian of width $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ in
$\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, and over such a short distance the smooth function $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$
looks like $\exp$ of a straight line. A Gaussian times $\exp$ of a linear function is a
Gaussian with a shifted mean.<Sidenote>Precisely: expand $\log q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})$ to first
order around $\mathbf{x}_t$. The product is then a Gaussian of variance about $\beta_t$ whose
mean is moved by about $\beta_t \nabla \log q(\mathbf{x}_t)$, which is where the score enters
later. The error is of order $\beta_t^2$, so it vanishes as steps get small. Feller (1949)
proved the continuous-time version; Sohl-Dickstein et al. cite it as the reason the reverse
process can share the forward process's functional form.</Sidenote>

So the model is a chain of Gaussians running backwards:

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t),\ \sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}\right),
\qquad p(\mathbf{x}_T) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}).
$$

A network computes the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ from $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $t$. The
variance $\sigma_t^2$ is fixed; DDPM tried $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ and the
$\tilde\beta_t$ defined below, and found both work about equally well.

### The reverse step when the clean point is known

The reverse conditional is intractable because it averages over every possible clean
$\mathbf{x}_0$. If we also condition on $\mathbf{x}_0$, it becomes exact and Gaussian. This
**forward posterior** $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ is the target the
model will be trained against.

By Bayes' rule and the Markov property,

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \propto
\underbrace{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})}_{\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t)}\
\underbrace{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}_{\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_0,\ 1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})} .
$$

Both factors are Gaussian in $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, so the product is too. Collect the terms of
the exponent that are quadratic in $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ to get the precision, and the linear ones
to get the mean:

$$
\frac{1}{\tilde\beta_t} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}
= \frac{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}
= \frac{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})},
$$

using $\alpha_t - \alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$. Then

$$
\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) &= \tilde\beta_t\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\mathbf{x}_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_0\right) \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The mean is a weighted blend of where we are, $\mathbf{x}_t$, and where we came from,
$\mathbf{x}_0$.<Sidenote>These are eqs. 6–7 of Ho et al.</Sidenote>

**Worked example.** Continue with $x_0 = 1$ and $x_{250} = 1.2758$. The script gives
$\beta_{250} = 0.005060$, $\bar\alpha_{249} = 0.5268$ and $\bar\alpha_{250} = 0.5241$, so

$$
\tilde\mu = \underbrace{0.007717}_{\text{weight on } x_0} \times 1 + \underbrace{0.991880}_{\text{weight on } x_t} \times 1.2758 = 1.2732,
\qquad
\tilde\beta = 0.005032 .
$$

One reverse step moves $x_t$ back toward $x_0$ by only $0.0026$, and its variance
$\tilde\beta = 0.005032$ is just below $\beta = 0.005060$. A Monte Carlo check agrees: of four
million forward pairs $(x_{249}, x_{250})$ started from $x_0 = 1$, the 6685 with $x_{250}$
within $0.002$ of $1.2758$ have $x_{249}$ with mean $1.2733$ and variance $0.005040$.

### Writing the mean in terms of the noise

During training we make $\mathbf{x}_t$ from $\mathbf{x}_0$ and a noise draw
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, so we can swap $\mathbf{x}_0$ for
$(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$. After
simplifying, the posterior mean depends on $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the noise only:

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right).
$$

In words: to step back, subtract a small multiple of the noise that is in $\mathbf{x}_t$,
then undo the shrink. This suggests the parametrisation DDPM uses. The network
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ predicts the noise, and the mean is built from
it:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right).
$$

Check on the example: $(1.2758 - \tfrac{0.005060}{0.6899} \times 0.8)/\sqrt{0.99494} = 1.2732$,
the same $\tilde\mu$ as before.

## The training objective

### The evidence lower bound

We want parameters $\theta$ that make the data likely under the model, that is, large
$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$. But $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0) = \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})\,
d\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ integrates over every path, which we cannot compute. The standard fix, as in
a variational autoencoder, is a lower bound. The forward process is a natural choice of
"encoder" $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$, and Jensen's inequality gives the
**evidence lower bound** (ELBO):

$$
\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0) \ \ge\ \mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}\right]
= \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log p(\mathbf{x}_T) + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})}\right].
$$

As written, every term involves a random $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$, so a Monte Carlo
estimate is noisy. The trick is to flip each forward factor around with Bayes' rule, conditioned
on $\mathbf{x}_0$ (allowed because the chain is Markov):

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_0)
= \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)\, q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} .
$$

Substitute this for $t \ge 2$. The ratios $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) / q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
\mid \mathbf{x}_0)$ telescope to $q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0) / q(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid
\mathbf{x}_0)$, and the negative ELBO splits into three kinds of term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{L_T:\ \text{prior}}
\ \underbrace{-\ \mathbb{E}_q \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}_{L_0:\ \text{reconstruction}} \\
&\quad + \sum_{t=2}^{T} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}:\ \text{denoising}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each term has a plain meaning.

| Term | What it measures | Depends on $\theta$? | Size here |
| --- | --- | :---: | ---: |
| $L_T$ | how far the fully noised data are from $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ | no | $2.0\times10^{-5}$ nats for $x_0 = 1$ |
| $L_{t-1}$ | how well one model step matches the exact posterior step | yes | trained |
| $L_0$ | how well the last step reconstructs the data | yes | trained (DDPM uses a discrete decoder for 8-bit pixels) |

The prior term is a constant: with $\bar\alpha_T = 4.04\times10^{-5}$, the KL between
$\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}x_0, 1-\bar\alpha_T)$ and $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is
$\tfrac12(\bar\alpha_T x_0^2 - \bar\alpha_T - \log(1 - \bar\alpha_T)) = 2.0\times 10^{-5}$ nats
for $x_0 = 1$. The work is in the denoising terms, and each of them compares two Gaussians,
the exact step from the previous section and the model's step, which has a closed form.

### From KL divergence to noise prediction

Two Gaussians with the same covariance $\sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}$ (taking the model's variance as
fixed) have KL divergence $\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}} -
\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta\|^2$ plus a constant. Both means are written in the noise form of the
last section, so their difference is just the difference of the two noises:

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\big(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\big),
$$

and therefore

$$
L_{t-1} = \underbrace{\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}_{\lambda_t}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Big[\big\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big] + C .
$$

The likelihood bound has turned into a regression: noise a data point, ask the network for
the noise, and score it with a squared error. This is **denoising score matching** in
disguise, as we will see shortly.<Sidenote>P. Vincent, "A Connection Between Score Matching
and Denoising Autoencoders", *Neural Computation* 23(7), 2011, showed that this squared error
on the noise is, up to scaling, score matching on the noised data.</Sidenote>

### The simple loss

The weights $\lambda_t$ vary a lot. With $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$,
$\lambda_t = \beta_t / (2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t))$:

| $t$ | 1 | 2 | 10 | 250 | 500 | 1000 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\lambda_t$ | 0.500 | 0.273 | 0.0737 | 0.0053 | 0.0055 | 0.0102 |

The bound puts almost all its weight on the first few steps, where the noise is tiny and
predicting it is hard but matters little for how a sample looks. Ho et al. dropped the weights
and sampled $t$ uniformly, which gives the **simple loss**:

$$
L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}\{1..T\},\ \mathbf{x}_0,\ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Big[\big\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big].
$$

It is no longer a bound on the likelihood, but it gave better samples (DDPM's best FID on
CIFAR-10 used it). Relative to the bound it down-weights the small-$t$ terms and spends the
network's effort on the noisier steps that shape the image.<Sidenote>Ho et al., eq. 14 and
Table 2. Nichol and Dhariwal later learned $\sigma_t^2$ with a hybrid of $L_{\text{simple}}$
and a small weight on the true bound, which improves likelihood without hurting
samples.</Sidenote>

### Training and sampling in code

The whole method fits in a few lines. Training picks a random step, noises a batch in one
jump with the closed form, and regresses the noise:

```python
def train_step(model, x0, alpha_bar, opt):
    t = torch.randint(1, T + 1, (x0.shape[0],))          # one random step per example
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    a = alpha_bar[t].view(-1, *[1] * (x0.dim() - 1))
    xt = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps            # jump straight to step t
    loss = ((model(xt, t) - eps) ** 2).mean()            # L_simple
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
```

Sampling (DDPM's ancestral sampler) runs the learned reverse chain from noise, one network call
per step:

```python
@torch.no_grad()
def sample(model, shape, beta, alpha_bar):
    x = torch.randn(shape)                                # x_T ~ N(0, I)
    for t in range(T, 0, -1):
        eps = model(x, torch.full((shape[0],), t))
        mean = (x - beta[t] / (1 - alpha_bar[t]).sqrt() * eps) / (1 - beta[t]).sqrt()
        z = torch.randn(shape) if t > 1 else 0            # no noise on the last step
        x = mean + beta[t].sqrt() * z                     # sigma_t^2 = beta_t
    return x
```

Training is cheap per step (one network call, any $t$), while sampling needs $T$ calls in
sequence. That asymmetry is the main practical weakness of diffusion models.

## What the network learns

### The optimal denoiser

A squared-error regression has a well-known minimiser: the conditional mean. For every
$\mathbf{x}_t$ and $t$, the best possible network outputs

$$
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^\star(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \mid \mathbf{x}_t],
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_t] = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^\star}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} .
$$

Two consequences follow. First, predicting noise and predicting the clean point are the same
task: either output gives the other. We call $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ the **denoised estimate**.
Second, the loss cannot reach zero. A given $\mathbf{x}_t$ could have come from many
$\mathbf{x}_0$, and the best network can only predict their average. The leftover loss is
$\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$; for the 1-D bump data,
averaged over $t$, it is about $0.226$.

### Worked example: two points

Let the data be two equally likely points, $x_0 = +1$ or $x_0 = -1$. Given $x_t$, the
posterior probability of each is proportional to its likelihood
$\mathcal{N}(x_t; \pm\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}, 1-\bar\alpha_t)$. The ratio is
$\exp\!\big(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\big)$, and the posterior mean
$P(+1) - P(-1)$ is a hyperbolic tangent:

$$
\hat x_0(x_t, t) = \tanh\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\right).
$$

Check one value: at $t = 250$ and $x_t = 0.3$, the argument is
$0.7239 \times 0.3 / 0.4759 = 0.4563$ and $\tanh(0.4563) = 0.4271$. The posterior gives
$P(+1) = 0.7136$ and $P(-1) = 0.2864$, whose difference is the same $0.4271$. The optimal noise
prediction is $(0.3 - 0.7239 \times 0.4271)/0.6899 = -0.0133$: almost all of $x_t$ is
explained as signal.

### The score

There is a second way to read the optimal network. The **score** of a distribution is the
gradient of its log density, $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log q(\mathbf{x})$: a vector field that
points toward higher density. Differentiate
$q(\mathbf{x}_t) = \int q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)\, q(\mathbf{x}_0)\, d\mathbf{x}_0$
and divide by $q(\mathbf{x}_t)$:

$$
\nabla \log q(\mathbf{x}_t)
= \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \,\big|\, \mathbf{x}_t\big]
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[-\frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} \,\middle|\, \mathbf{x}_t\right]
= -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^\star(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} .
$$

So the optimal noise prediction is the score of the noised data, scaled by
$-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. Rearranged, this is **Tweedie's formula**: $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0
\mid \mathbf{x}_t] = \big(\mathbf{x}_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\nabla \log
q(\mathbf{x}_t)\big)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$.<Sidenote>B. Efron, "Tweedie's Formula and Selection
Bias", *JASA* 106(496), 2011. Song et al. ("Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic
Differential Equations", ICLR 2021, [arXiv:2011.13456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456)) made
the link the basis of a continuous-time view: DDPM is a discretised stochastic differential
equation, and sampling solves its time reversal.</Sidenote> In the two-point example at
$t = 250$, $x_t = 0.3$, the score is $0.0133/0.6899 = 0.0193$.

This explains the reverse step. Plug $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^\star = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,
\nabla\log q$ into the DDPM mean: $\boldsymbol{\mu} = (\mathbf{x}_t + \beta_t \nabla \log
q(\mathbf{x}_t))/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$. Each step climbs the score by $\beta_t$, undoes the shrink
and adds noise of variance $\beta_t$: it is a step of Langevin dynamics, the sampler of the
[sampling post](/blog/sampling-methods/), on a target that changes slowly from
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ to the data.

### A trained network against the exact answer

Does a real network learn this? A small multilayer perceptron (input $(x_t, t/T)$, three hidden
layers of 128 SiLU units) was trained on the 1-D bump data with $L_{\text{simple}}$: 20,000 Adam
steps with batch 512 and a cosine-decayed learning rate from $10^{-3}$. Its final loss is
$0.2257$, against $0.2266$ for the exact denoiser (both Monte Carlo estimates, so the gap is
noise). Converting its noise output to $\hat x_0$:

| $t$ | $x_t$ | network $\hat x_0$ | exact $\hat x_0$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 50 | −1.0 | −1.060 | −1.075 |
| 50 | 0.0 | 0.161 | 0.236 |
| 50 | 1.0 | 1.010 | 1.011 |
| 250 | −1.0 | −1.038 | −1.050 |
| 250 | 0.0 | 0.218 | 0.205 |
| 250 | 1.0 | 0.984 | 0.987 |
| 500 | −1.0 | −0.246 | −0.254 |
| 500 | 0.0 | 0.126 | 0.127 |
| 500 | 1.0 | 0.486 | 0.467 |

The network matches the exact denoiser to about $0.02$ everywhere except at $t = 50$,
$x_t = 0$. That point lies between the two bumps at low noise, a place training examples
almost never land, so the network has little data there. This is a general pattern: a
diffusion model is accurate where the noised data are dense and guesses elsewhere.

## Sampling

### Ancestral sampling (DDPM)

With a denoiser in hand, sampling is the loop from the training section: start at
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and take $T$ Gaussian steps with mean
$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$. The figure runs it on the bump data with the exact denoiser. Press
play and watch the particles start as one blob on the right, then split into two groups in
the middle steps, which is where the denoiser curve turns from flat to steep.

### DDIM: the same network, fewer steps

A thousand network calls per sample is expensive. Song, Meng and Ermon noticed that the
training loss only ever uses the marginals $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$, never the
step-by-step chain. Many different chains have those same marginals, and some of them allow
large jumps. Their **DDIM** update goes from step $t$ to any earlier step $s < t$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{s} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{s}}\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0
+ \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{s} - \sigma^2}\ \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
+ \sigma\, \mathbf{z},
\qquad
\sigma = \eta\,\sqrt{\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{s}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{s}}},
$$

where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ and
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ is the denoised estimate built from it.<Sidenote>J. Song, C. Meng and S.
Ermon, "Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models", ICLR 2021,
[arXiv:2010.02502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02502), eqs. 12 and 16.</Sidenote> Read it as:
predict the clean point, then re-noise it to level $s$, reusing the predicted noise direction
for most of the noise and fresh noise $\mathbf{z}$ for the rest.

- **$\eta = 1$** with $s = t - 1$ gives $\sigma^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ and exactly the DDPM step with
  variance $\tilde\beta_t$ (the script checks the means agree to $10^{-14}$ at $t = 2$, $250$
  and $999$).
- **$\eta = 0$** removes the fresh noise. Sampling becomes a deterministic map from
  $\mathbf{x}_T$ to $\mathbf{x}_0$, a discretisation of an ordinary differential equation (the
  **probability-flow ODE** of Song et al.). The same starting noise always gives the same
  sample.

Both work with a network trained on $L_{\text{simple}}$; only the sampler changes. The 2-D
figure shows them on two half moons. The purple arrows are the score field at the current
step; the dashed lines join eight particles to their denoised estimate
$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$. Early on every estimate sits near the centre (the
data mean); as $t$ falls they spread to the moons and the particles follow.

### How many steps?

Cutting the steps makes each jump large, and large jumps break the assumption the reverse step
rests on. Each step replaces "all clean points that could have produced $\mathbf{x}_t$" by
their average $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$, plus Gaussian noise. That is accurate for small steps.
Over a large jump the true distribution of $\mathbf{x}_s$ given $\mathbf{x}_t$ is spread over
several modes, but the sampler puts one Gaussian around a blend of them. Samples land between
the modes and the spread shrinks. In the extreme of a single step from $t = 1000$, every sample
is $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0(\mathbf{x}_T)$, which is about the data mean: the blurry average.

DDIM is only slightly better than DDPM here, because the figures use the exact denoiser. With
a trained network, the deterministic sampler is reported to hold quality far better at 10 to
50 steps (DDIM's Table 1 on CIFAR-10 and CelebA), and later ODE solvers and distillation push
the count lower still.

## Practical considerations

**What the network predicts.** Predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\mathbf{x}_0$ or the
**velocity** $\mathbf{v} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,
\mathbf{x}_0$ are interchangeable at the optimum, since each converts to the others given
$\mathbf{x}_t$. They differ in how errors scale: an $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ error is amplified
by $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ when turned into $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$, which is
huge at high noise. The $\mathbf{v}$ target, from progressive distillation, stays well behaved
at both ends.<Sidenote>T. Salimans and J. Ho, "Progressive Distillation for Fast Sampling of
Diffusion Models", ICLR 2022, [arXiv:2202.00512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00512).</Sidenote>

**The network.** DDPM uses a U-Net that takes $\mathbf{x}_t$ and a sinusoidal embedding of $t$,
and outputs a tensor of the same shape. Diffusion transformers (DiT) replace it with a
transformer over image patches.<Sidenote>W. Peebles and S. Xie, "Scalable Diffusion Models with
Transformers", ICCV 2023, [arXiv:2212.09748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748).</Sidenote> One
network serves every $t$, which is why the time input matters: it tells the network which of
the curves in the two-point figure to be.

**Conditioning and guidance.** To generate from a prompt $c$, give $c$ to the network as an
extra input. **Classifier-free guidance** trains one network both with $c$ and with $c$
dropped (an empty prompt), then samples with an extrapolated noise prediction
$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = (1+w)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, c) -
w\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$. Because the noise prediction is a scaled
score, this sharpens the distribution toward samples that fit $c$, at the cost of
diversity.<Sidenote>J. Ho and T. Salimans, "Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance", 2022,
[arXiv:2207.12598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12598). With $w$ the guidance weight, the
implied score is that of $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid c)^{1+w} / q(\mathbf{x}_t)^{w}$.</Sidenote>

**Latent diffusion.** Running diffusion on a 512×512×3 image is costly. Latent diffusion first
compresses images with an autoencoder (Stable Diffusion v1 maps 512×512 images to 64×64×4
latents), runs the diffusion model in that latent space and decodes at the
end.<Sidenote>R. Rombach et al., "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models",
CVPR 2022, [arXiv:2112.10752](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752).</Sidenote>

**Memorisation.** The optimal denoiser for a finite training set treats that set as the data
distribution, so sampling with it would only reproduce training examples. Real networks
generalise because they cannot represent that exact function; with enough data, two networks
trained on disjoint halves of a dataset produce nearly the same samples.<Sidenote>Z. Kadkhodaie
et al., "Generalization in Diffusion Models Arises from Geometry-Adaptive Harmonic
Representations", ICLR 2024, [arXiv:2310.02557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02557). This
post's figures avoid the issue by using smooth mixtures, not a finite set of points.</Sidenote>

## Summary

- The forward process adds Gaussian noise in $T$ small steps, and has a closed form:
  $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.
  The schedule $\bar\alpha_t$ decides how fast the signal disappears.
- For small steps the reverse step is Gaussian too. Conditioned on $\mathbf{x}_0$ it is exact,
  with a mean that blends $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathbf{x}_0$, and that can be written using the
  noise alone.
- The evidence lower bound becomes a sum of Gaussian KL divergences, each a squared error on
  the noise. Dropping the weights gives $L_{\text{simple}}$: noise a sample, predict the noise.
- The best network predicts $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$, which is the
  denoised estimate $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_t]$ and, scaled, the score
  $\nabla \log q(\mathbf{x}_t)$. Sampling climbs that score while the noise level falls.
- DDPM samples with fresh noise every step; DDIM reuses the same network with large or
  deterministic jumps. Around 100 steps loses nothing on the toy data; a handful of steps
  collapses samples toward the mean.
