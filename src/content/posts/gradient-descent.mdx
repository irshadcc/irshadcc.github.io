## Background

A neural network can represent a useful function only after we choose its weights. Training turns
that choice into an optimization problem: find the parameter vector $\mathbf w$ that makes an error
$E(\mathbf w)$ small. Direct search wastes almost all its evaluations. A gradient says which local
direction raises the error fastest, so its negative gives a principled direction in which to move.

This post follows Chapter 7 of Bishop and Bishop's *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts*.
It develops the geometry of an error surface, batch and stochastic gradient descent (SGD),
mini-batches, initialization, momentum, schedules, RMSProp, Adam, and data, batch, and layer
normalization. A final section gives derivations for all fourteen exercises. The next post,
[Backpropagation](/blog/backpropagation/), explains how the gradients are computed; the third,
[Regularization](/blog/regularization/), explains why minimizing training error is not the whole goal.

## Error surfaces

The **weight space** is the space whose coordinates are all weights and biases. For a small
displacement $\delta\mathbf w$, the first-order Taylor expansion is

$$
E(\mathbf w+\delta\mathbf w)
=E(\mathbf w)+\delta\mathbf w^T\nabla E(\mathbf w)+O(\|\delta\mathbf w\|^2).
$$

Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$\delta\mathbf w^T\nabla E\geq-\|\delta\mathbf w\|\|\nabla E\|$, with equality when the step
points along $-\nabla E$. Thus the negative gradient is the steepest local descent direction for a
fixed Euclidean step length. A **stationary point** has $\nabla E=0$; it may be a minimum, maximum,
or saddle point.

### The local quadratic model

Near $\widehat{\mathbf w}$,

$$
E(\mathbf w)\simeq E(\widehat{\mathbf w})
+(\mathbf w-\widehat{\mathbf w})^T\mathbf b
+\frac12(\mathbf w-\widehat{\mathbf w})^T\mathbf H(\mathbf w-\widehat{\mathbf w}),
$$

where $\mathbf b=\nabla E(\widehat{\mathbf w})$ and $\mathbf H=\nabla^2E$ is the
**Hessian**. At a stationary point $\mathbf w^\star$, $\mathbf b=0$. Because a smooth Hessian is
symmetric, it has orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf u_i$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. Write
$\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\star=\sum_i\alpha_i\mathbf u_i$ to obtain

$$
E(\mathbf w)=E(\mathbf w^\star)+\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2.
$$

This equation is the geometry in one line. Positive eigenvalues bend upward; a negative one gives a
descent direction; a zero one is locally flat. Constant-error contours have semi-axis lengths
proportional to $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. A large condition number
$\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ therefore produces a long, narrow valley.

The surface below is the scaled-down quadratic $E(w_1,w_2)=4w_1^2+w_2^2/2$. Rotate it and compare
the two axes. A step of the same length changes the error eight times more strongly along $w_1$
than along $w_2$. This is why one learning rate can oscillate across a valley while making slow
progress along it.

### Why equivalent minima appear

Permuting $M$ hidden units leaves a layer's function unchanged if the corresponding incoming and
outgoing weights are permuted together. With a symmetric activation, flipping one hidden unit's
incoming signs and compensating in the next layer adds another symmetry. A two-layer network can
therefore have $M!2^M$ parameter vectors representing the same function. This is one reason that a
unique parameter optimum is neither expected nor required.

## Gradient descent optimization

Write a general iterative update as

$$
\mathbf w^{(\tau)}=\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}+\Delta\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}.
$$

This notation separates two questions. The iteration index $\tau$ tells us *when* a parameter
vector is used. The update $\Delta\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)}$ tells us *how* the next vector is chosen.
An optimizer is a rule for constructing this update from information available at the current and
possibly earlier iterations.

### Using gradient information

Why compute a gradient rather than only compare function values? Suppose the parameter vector has
$W$ components. A derivative-free search must probe directions to discover which way is downhill.
The gradient packages the local slopes in all $W$ coordinate directions into one vector. Its inner
product with a proposed step predicts the change in error:

$$
\Delta E \simeq \Delta\mathbf w^T\nabla E.
$$

If we constrain $\|\Delta\mathbf w\|=\epsilon$, write the angle between the update and gradient as
$\theta$. Then $\Delta E\simeq\epsilon\|\nabla E\|\cos\theta$. The smallest value occurs at
$\theta=\pi$, which proves that $-\nabla E$ is locally steepest. This is a local statement: a large
step can cross a valley, enter a region where the Taylor approximation is poor, and increase the
error.

The gradient is also coordinate-dependent. “Steepest” here means steepest under Euclidean distance
in the chosen parameterization. Rescaling one parameter changes that geometry. Data normalization,
RMSProp, Adam, and natural-gradient methods can all be viewed as changing the relative scale of
directions.

### Batch gradient descent

**Batch gradient descent** chooses
$\Delta\mathbf w=-\eta\nabla E$, where the learning rate $\eta>0$. For the quadratic model, each
eigen-coordinate evolves independently:

$$
\alpha_i^{(\tau)}=(1-\eta\lambda_i)\alpha_i^{(\tau-1)}.
$$

It converges without oscillatory growth exactly when
$|1-\eta\lambda_i|<1$ for every direction, hence $0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$. One global learning
rate is limited by the stiffest direction even when progress along flat directions is slow.

There are three regimes in one eigen-direction:

| Learning rate | Multiplier $1-\eta\lambda_i$ | Behaviour |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| $0<\eta<1/\lambda_i$ | between $0$ and $1$ | approaches without crossing |
| $1/\lambda_i<\eta<2/\lambda_i$ | between $-1$ and $0$ | crosses and oscillates with decreasing amplitude |
| $\eta>2/\lambda_i$ | magnitude greater than $1$ | oscillations grow and training diverges |

For example, let $E(w)=2w^2$, so $\nabla E=4w$ and $\lambda=4$. Starting at $w=2$ with
$\eta=0.2$ gives $w^{(1)}=2-0.2(8)=0.4$ and $w^{(2)}=0.08$. With $\eta=0.4$, the multiplier is
$-0.6$, so the iterates $2,-1.2,0.72,-0.432,\ldots$ cross the minimum. Both converge because
$\eta<2/4=0.5$. At $\eta=0.6$, the multiplier is $-1.4$ and the magnitude grows.

### SGD and mini-batches

For independent data, $E=\sum_{n=1}^N E_n$. **Stochastic gradient descent** uses one term,

$$
\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\nabla E_n(\mathbf w).
$$

If $n$ is sampled uniformly, $N\nabla E_n$ is an unbiased estimator of $\nabla E$. Its noise can
help leave shallow basins, but also makes the path jitter. A **mini-batch** $\mathcal B$ of size $B$
uses $B^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathcal B}\nabla E_n$. Independent gradient noise then has variance roughly
$1/B$, while matrix operations become much more efficient than $B$ separate evaluations.

To see the unbiasedness carefully, define the mean objective
$\bar E=N^{-1}\sum_nE_n$ and sample an index $I$ uniformly. Then

$$
\mathbb E_I[\nabla E_I]
=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1N\nabla E_n
=\nabla\bar E.
$$

For a batch sampled independently with replacement,
$\widehat{\mathbf g}=B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B\nabla E_{I_b}$ has the same expectation. If each sample
gradient has covariance $\Sigma$, independence gives
$\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf g})=\Sigma/B$. The standard deviation therefore falls as
$1/\sqrt B$, not $1/B$. Doubling a large batch gives diminishing noise reduction, which is one
reason the largest batch that fits in memory is not automatically best.

The chart separates the two rates. Variance falls as $1/B$, but the visible scatter of a gradient
estimate is its standard deviation, which falls only as $1/\sqrt B$. Moving from batch size 1 to 16
reduces standard deviation by four; achieving another factor of four requires batch size 256.

The convention matters. If the objective is a *sum*, its full gradient grows with $N$; if it is a
*mean*, it does not. Modern libraries normally average a mini-batch loss. Changing batch size while
summing changes the effective learning rate even if the optimizer's displayed $\eta$ is unchanged.

An **epoch** is one pass through the training set. Shuffling between epochs prevents a fixed order
from imprinting a repeated correlation on the updates. Duplicating every example doubles the cost
of a batch step but conveys no new information; SGD exposes this redundancy directly.

### Initialization

Equal weights give hidden units equal activations and equal gradients, so they remain identical.
Random initialization breaks this symmetry. Scale matters too. If a ReLU layer has fan-in $M$,
independent zero-mean weights of variance $\epsilon^2$, and incoming second moment $\lambda^2$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(a_i)=M\epsilon^2\mathbb E[\operatorname{ReLU}(a)^2]
=\frac{M}{2}\epsilon^2\lambda^2.
$$

Preserving variance gives $\epsilon=\sqrt{2/M}$, the He initialization rule. It prevents signals
from exploding or vanishing before learning begins.

## Convergence and adaptive methods

### Momentum

**Momentum** keeps a velocity-like running update:

$$
\Delta\mathbf w^{(\tau)}=-\eta\mathbf g^{(\tau)}+\mu\Delta\mathbf w^{(\tau-1)},
\qquad \mathbf w^{(\tau+1)}=\mathbf w^{(\tau)}+\Delta\mathbf w^{(\tau)}.
$$

When gradients keep pointing the same way, the geometric series makes the effective learning rate
$\eta/(1-\mu)$. Across a narrow valley, alternating components cancel. Momentum therefore
accelerates the flat direction without equally amplifying oscillation in the stiff direction.
Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient after a look-ahead step; to first order it reduces to
ordinary momentum when $\eta$ and $\mu$ are small.

### Learning-rate schedules

A large initial rate moves quickly; a smaller late rate reduces stochastic wandering. Common
schedules are linear decay, power-law decay, and exponential decay. The useful invariant is not
the schedule's name but whether the observed training curve falls rapidly without becoming
unstable. Warmup is often added when early gradients or adaptive moments are poorly calibrated.

The curves below start at the same learning rate. Linear decay reaches zero at the planned horizon;
exponential decay removes a fixed fraction per step; inverse-time decay has a longer tail. These
are shapes to understand, not universal recommendations—the schedule must match the update budget.

### RMSProp and Adam

RMSProp tracks an exponential average of squared gradients,
$\mathbf r_\tau=\beta_2\mathbf r_{\tau-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf g_\tau^2$, and divides componentwise by
$\sqrt{\mathbf r_\tau}+\delta$. Directions with persistently large gradients receive smaller
steps.

Adam also tracks the first moment
$\mathbf s_\tau=\beta_1\mathbf s_{\tau-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf g_\tau$. Starting both moments at
zero biases them low. Expanding the recursion gives
$\mathbb E[\mathbf s_\tau]=(1-\beta_1^\tau)\mathbb E[\mathbf g]$, so Adam uses

$$
\widehat{\mathbf s}_\tau=\frac{\mathbf s_\tau}{1-\beta_1^\tau},\quad
\widehat{\mathbf r}_\tau=\frac{\mathbf r_\tau}{1-\beta_2^\tau},\quad
\Delta\mathbf w=-\eta\frac{\widehat{\mathbf s}_\tau}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf r}_\tau}+\delta}.
$$

The square, square root, division, and addition here are elementwise. Adam changes the optimizer's
geometry; it does not remove the need to choose a learning rate or inspect generalization.

### What adaptive rescaling actually does

Consider a two-parameter gradient $\mathbf g=(100,1)$. Plain gradient descent with
$\eta=10^{-3}$ proposes $(-0.1,-0.001)$, so the first coordinate dominates. If these magnitudes
persist, RMSProp's second moment approaches $\mathbf r=(10^4,1)$. Dividing by
$\sqrt{\mathbf r}$ produces an approximately normalized direction $(1,1)$. The method has not
made the first derivative smaller; it has changed the metric used to convert a derivative into a
step.

This coordinatewise treatment explains both the appeal and the limitation. It handles axis-aligned
differences in scale, but it does not remove correlations between parameters. If a narrow valley is
rotated relative to the coordinate axes, a diagonal preconditioner cannot align itself perfectly
with the Hessian eigenvectors.

Adam combines this rescaling with momentum. Written for one coordinate,

$$
s_\tau=\beta_1s_{\tau-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_\tau,qquad
r_\tau=\beta_2r_{\tau-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_\tau^2.
$$

The ratio $\widehat s_\tau/\sqrt{\widehat r_\tau}$ is close to the sign of a constant gradient,
but it becomes smaller when signs fluctuate because cancellation affects the first moment while
squares keep the second moment large. Adam therefore favours coordinates whose gradients are both
large *and consistent*.

The stabilizer $\delta$ is not only protection against division by zero. When
$\sqrt{\widehat r}\ll\delta$, the update is approximately
$-\eta\widehat s/\delta$, so $\delta$ sets the effective scale in very low-gradient coordinates.
Moving it outside rather than inside the square root changes that low-magnitude behaviour.

### Convergence is not the same as generalization

Optimization asks whether iterates approach a stationary point of the training objective.
Generalization asks whether the resulting function predicts new data. A method can reduce training
loss faster yet reach a worse test solution because the optimizer itself supplies an **implicit
bias**. Mini-batch noise, momentum, adaptive scaling, initialization, and stopping time all affect
which member of a large set of low-training-error solutions is selected.

This is especially important in overparameterized networks. There may be many global minima with
essentially zero training error. “The optimizer converged” therefore does not identify a unique
function, nor does a lower final training loss prove that the learned function is better.

## Normalization

**Data normalization** standardizes input coordinate $i$ as
$\widetilde x_{ni}=(x_{ni}-\mu_i)/\sigma_i$. Its sample mean is $0$ and variance is $1$. For linear
regression this improves Hessian conditioning: changing one parameter no longer moves the output
by a scale a million times larger than another.

For a design matrix $X$, the squared-error Hessian is $X^TX$. If one feature is multiplied by
$c$, its corresponding diagonal curvature is multiplied by $c^2$. A single learning rate must then
be small enough for that enlarged curvature. Standardization does not guarantee perfect
conditioning—correlated features can still create a narrow valley—but it removes arbitrary units
as a source of poor conditioning.

For a feature with training values $x_{1i},\ldots,x_{Ni}$, compute

$$
\mu_i=\frac1N\sum_nx_{ni},\qquad
\sigma_i^2=\frac1N\sum_n(x_{ni}-\mu_i)^2.
$$

The same stored $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ must transform validation, test, and production inputs.
Recomputing them on the test set leaks information and makes the deployed mapping differ from the
one evaluated.

**Batch normalization** standardizes each hidden feature using mini-batch statistics, then learns
an affine scale and shift. Training uses batch statistics; inference uses running population
estimates. Its effect is not merely a cure for “internal covariate shift”: it also changes the
optimization landscape and injects batch-dependent noise.

For feature $i$ in a batch of $B$ examples,

$$
\mu_i^{\mathcal B}=\frac1B\sum_{n\in\mathcal B}a_{ni},\qquad
(\sigma_i^{\mathcal B})^2=\frac1B\sum_{n\in\mathcal B}(a_{ni}-\mu_i^{\mathcal B})^2,
$$

$$
\widehat a_{ni}=\frac{a_{ni}-\mu_i^{\mathcal B}}
{\sqrt{(\sigma_i^{\mathcal B})^2+\epsilon}},\qquad
z_{ni}=\gamma_i\widehat a_{ni}+\beta_i.
$$

The small $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero. The learned $\gamma_i$ and $\beta_i$ are essential:
normalization would otherwise forbid the layer from representing a useful nonzero mean or scale.
During inference, using the current example's “batch” would make the prediction depend on which
other examples happen to accompany it. Running training estimates avoid that dependence.

**Layer normalization** computes statistics across a single example's hidden coordinates. It is
independent of batch size and uses the same rule at training and inference, which makes it natural
for transformers and sequence models. Batch norm couples examples; layer norm couples features.

If a layer has $D$ hidden coordinates, layer normalization uses

$$
\mu_n=\frac1D\sum_{i=1}^D a_{ni},\qquad
\sigma_n^2=\frac1D\sum_{i=1}^D(a_{ni}-\mu_n)^2,
$$

followed by $z_{ni}=\gamma_i(a_{ni}-\mu_n)/\sqrt{\sigma_n^2+\epsilon}+\beta_i$. Statistics are
computed separately for each example, so there is no running-average state and no train/inference
mismatch. In a transformer, the normalized axes are the hidden features of one token; tokens and
examples remain separate.

### Exercise 7.1: diagonalizing the quadratic error

> **Problem.** Starting from the quadratic approximation at a stationary point, expand the
> displacement in the orthonormal eigenvectors of the Hessian and show that the error separates
> into a sum $\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2$.

**Solution.**

At a stationary point the linear Taylor term is zero, so set
$\Delta\mathbf w=\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\star$ and start from
$E=E^\star+\frac12\Delta\mathbf w^T H\Delta\mathbf w$. Because the eigenvectors form a basis,
$\Delta\mathbf w=\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbf u_j$. Substitute this expression on both sides:

$$
\Delta\mathbf w^TH\Delta\mathbf w
=\left(\sum_i\alpha_i\mathbf u_i^T\right)
H\left(\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbf u_j\right)
=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\mathbf u_i^TH\mathbf u_j.
$$

Use $H\mathbf u_j=\lambda_j\mathbf u_j$, then orthonormality
$\mathbf u_i^T\mathbf u_j=\delta_{ij}$. The Kronecker delta removes every cross term:

$$
\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\lambda_j\delta_{ij}
=\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2.
$$

Hence $E=E^\star+\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2$. Rotating into the Hessian eigenbasis has
turned one coupled multivariable quadratic into independent one-dimensional quadratics.

### Exercise 7.2: positive definiteness and eigenvalues

> **Problem.** Prove both directions of the claim that a real symmetric Hessian is positive
> definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are strictly positive.

**Solution.**

First assume $H$ is positive definite. Choose the nonzero test vector $\mathbf v=\mathbf u_i$.
Then $\mathbf u_i^TH\mathbf u_i=\lambda_i\mathbf u_i^T\mathbf u_i=\lambda_i>0$. Thus every
eigenvalue is positive.

For the converse, assume all $\lambda_i>0$. Expand an arbitrary nonzero vector as
$\mathbf v=\sum_i c_i\mathbf u_i$. Repeating the calculation from Exercise 7.1 gives
$\mathbf v^TH\mathbf v=\sum_i\lambda_i c_i^2$. At least one $c_i$ is nonzero; every term is
nonnegative and that term is positive. Therefore the sum is positive for every nonzero
$\mathbf v$, which is exactly the definition of positive definiteness.

### Exercise 7.3: the minimum condition

> **Problem.** For the quadratic error model centred at a stationary point, prove that the point is
> a strict minimum exactly when the Hessian is positive definite.

**Solution.**

If $H$ is positive definite, Exercise 7.2 gives
$\Delta\mathbf w^TH\Delta\mathbf w>0$ whenever $\Delta\mathbf w\ne0$. Consequently
$E(\mathbf w)>E(\mathbf w^\star)$ everywhere except at $\mathbf w^\star$, so the stationary point
is a strict local minimum of the quadratic model.

Conversely, suppose the quadratic has a strict minimum at $\mathbf w^\star$. Then its increase
$\frac12\Delta\mathbf w^TH\Delta\mathbf w$ must be positive for every nonzero displacement.
Since a displacement can be any vector, this is precisely $H\succ0$. The zero-gradient condition
alone is insufficient: a negative eigenvalue would give a descending direction and mixed signs
would make the point a saddle.

### Exercise 7.4: Hessian of linear regression

> **Problem.** For one-input linear regression $y(x)=wx+b$ with sum-of-squares error, derive the
> $2\times2$ Hessian in $(w,b)$, calculate its trace and determinant, and use them to show that the
> stationary point is a minimum for nondegenerate input data.

**Solution.**

Let $y_n=wx_n+b$ and $E=\frac12\sum_n(y_n-t_n)^2$. Differentiating once gives
$E_w=\sum_n(y_n-t_n)x_n$ and $E_b=\sum_n(y_n-t_n)$. Since
$\partial y_n/\partial w=x_n$ and $\partial y_n/\partial b=1$, differentiating again gives

$$
\mathbf H=\begin{bmatrix}\sum x_n^2&N\bar x\\N\bar x&N\end{bmatrix},\quad
\operatorname{tr}H=\sum_nx_n^2+N>0.
$$

The determinant is
$N\sum_nx_n^2-(\sum_nx_n)^2=N^2[(N^{-1}\sum_nx_n^2)-\bar x^2]=N^2\sigma_x^2$.
For nonconstant inputs it is positive. A real $2\times2$ symmetric matrix has eigenvalue sum equal
to its trace and product equal to its determinant. A positive product says the eigenvalues have the
same sign; a positive sum says that sign is positive. Thus the unique stationary point is a minimum.

### Exercise 7.5: Hessian of logistic regression

> **Problem.** Repeat the Hessian calculation for a logistic-sigmoid binary classifier trained by
> cross-entropy. Show that its trace and determinant are positive except for degenerate data, and
> hence that its stationary point is a minimum.

**Solution.**

For $y_n=\sigma(wx_n+b)$ and negative cross-entropy, the sigmoid and loss derivatives cancel to
give the same first derivatives as above: $E_w=\sum_n(y_n-t_n)x_n$ and
$E_b=\sum_n(y_n-t_n)$. Now $partial y_n/\partial w=c_nx_n$ and
$\partial y_n/\partial b=c_n$, where $c_n=y_n(1-y_n)>0$. Therefore

$$
H=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_nc_nx_n^2&\sum_nc_nx_n\\
\sum_nc_nx_n&\sum_nc_n\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its trace is positive. Let $C=\sum_nc_n$ and weighted mean
$\mu_c=C^{-1}\sum_nc_nx_n$. Completing the square gives
$\det H=C\sum_nc_n(x_n-\mu_c)^2>0$ unless all inputs coincide. The same trace/determinant argument
proves both eigenvalues positive. Notice that targets disappear from the Hessian explicitly, while
the parameters remain through $y_n$.

### Exercise 7.6: axes of constant-error ellipses

> **Problem.** For a positive-definite quadratic error, show that constant-error contours are
> ellipses aligned with the Hessian eigenvectors and find how each semi-axis length depends on its
> eigenvalue.

**Solution.**

In eigen-coordinates a constant rise $C$ satisfies
$\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i^2=C$. Along axis $j$, set every other $\alpha_i=0$:
$\frac12\lambda_j\alpha_j^2=C$, hence the two intercepts are
$\alpha_j=\pm\sqrt{2C/\lambda_j}$. Therefore the axes align with eigenvectors and their semi-axis
lengths are inversely proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$. Large curvature means a short axis.

### Exercise 7.7: independent coefficients in a quadratic

> **Problem.** Count the independent nonconstant coefficients in a general local quadratic error
> function of $W$ parameters, using the symmetry of the Hessian.

**Solution.**

A general $W\times W$ matrix has $W^2$ entries. Symmetry leaves $W$ diagonal entries and one from
each off-diagonal pair: $W+(W^2-W)/2=W(W+1)/2$. Add the $W$ entries of the linear coefficient
$\mathbf b$. Ignoring the constant offset, the local quadratic therefore has
$W(W+1)/2+W=W(W+3)/2$ independent coefficients.

### Exercise 7.8: variance of the sample mean

> **Problem.** Let $x_1,\ldots,x_N$ be independent samples with mean $\mu$ and variance
> $\sigma^2$. Derive the mean squared deviation of their sample mean from $\mu$.

**Solution.**

For iid samples with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,
$\mathbb E[x_nx_m]=\mu^2+\delta_{nm}\sigma^2$. With
$\bar x=N^{-1}\sum_nx_n$,

$$
\mathbb E[\bar x^2]=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{n,m}\mathbb E[x_nx_m]
=\mu^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{N}.
$$

Since $\mathbb E[\bar x]=\mu$,
$\mathbb E[(\bar x-\mu)^2]=\mathbb E[\bar x^2]-\mu^2=\sigma^2/N$. Averaging $N$ independent
observations reduces variance by $N$ and standard error by $\sqrt N$.

### Exercise 7.9: deriving He initialization

> **Problem.** Consider a ReLU layer with fan-in $M$, independent zero-mean Gaussian weights, and
> symmetric pre-activations. Choose the weight variance so that activation variance is preserved
> from one layer to the next.

**Solution.**

For $a_i^{(l)}=\sum_{j=1}^Mw_{ij}z_j^{(l-1)}$, zero-mean independent weights make
$\mathbb E[a_i^{(l)}]=0$. Independence also makes variances add:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(a_i^{(l)})
=M\mathbb E[w_{ij}^2]\mathbb E[(z_j^{(l-1)})^2].
$$

If $w_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\epsilon^2)$, the first expectation is $\epsilon^2$. For a symmetric
pre-activation distribution with variance $\lambda^2$, ReLU keeps the positive half, so
$\mathbb E[\operatorname{ReLU}(a)^2]=\lambda^2/2$. Thus the next variance is
$M\epsilon^2\lambda^2/2$. Equating it to $\lambda^2$ gives
$\epsilon^2=2/M$ and $\epsilon=\sqrt{2/M}$.

### Exercise 7.10: gradient descent in eigen-coordinates

> **Problem.** For the quadratic error surface, express a gradient-descent update in the Hessian
> eigenbasis and derive the independent recurrence obeyed by each eigen-coordinate.

**Solution.**

The quadratic gradient is $H(\mathbf w-\mathbf w^\star)=\sum_i\lambda_i\alpha_i\mathbf u_i$.
Meanwhile the update is $\Delta\mathbf w=\sum_i\Delta\alpha_i\mathbf u_i$. Set this equal to
$-\eta\nabla E$ and multiply by $\mathbf u_j^T$. Orthonormality selects one component:
$\Delta\alpha_j=-\eta\lambda_j\alpha_j$. Hence
$\alpha_j^{(\tau+1)}=(1-\eta\lambda_j)\alpha_j^{(\tau)}$, the recurrence used in the convergence
analysis.

### Exercise 7.11: Nesterov versus ordinary momentum

> **Problem.** Taylor-expand the gradient at Nesterov's look-ahead point and show that, when the
> learning-rate and momentum displacement are treated as small, its update agrees with ordinary
> momentum to first order.

**Solution.**

Nesterov evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point
$\mathbf w+\mu\Delta\mathbf w_{old}$. Taylor expansion gives
$\nabla E(\mathbf w+\mu\Delta\mathbf w_{old})
=\nabla E(\mathbf w)+\mu H\Delta\mathbf w_{old}+O(\mu^2)$.
Multiplying by $-\eta$ makes the difference from ordinary momentum $-\eta\mu H\Delta\mathbf w$.
If $\eta$ and $\mu$ are both treated as small quantities of order $\varepsilon$, this is
$O(\varepsilon^2)$ and disappears at first order. The methods differ once those higher-order
look-ahead effects matter.

### Exercise 7.12: bias correction for an exponential average

> **Problem.** Starting from an exponentially weighted running mean initialized at zero, unroll the
> recurrence, calculate its expectation for iid data, identify its finite-time bias, and construct
> an unbiased estimator.

**Solution.**

Unroll $m_n=\beta m_{n-1}+(1-\beta)x_n$ from $m_0=0$:
$m_n=(1-\beta)\sum_{k=1}^n\beta^{k-1}x_{n-k+1}$. For iid observations of mean $\mu$,

$$
\mathbb E[m_n]=(1-\beta)\mu\sum_{k=1}^n\beta^{k-1}
=(1-\beta^n)\mu.
$$

The zero initialization biases early estimates toward zero. Division by $1-\beta^n$ gives
$\widehat m_n=m_n/(1-\beta^n)$ with expectation $\mu$.

### Exercise 7.13: orthogonality after an exact line search

> **Problem.** Minimize the error along the line $\mathbf w+\lambda\mathbf d$ and prove that at an
> exact interior line-search minimum, the new gradient is orthogonal to the search direction.

**Solution.**

Parameterize the search line by $\mathbf v(\lambda)=\mathbf w+\lambda\mathbf d$. The chain rule
gives $dE(\mathbf v(\lambda))/d\lambda
=\sum_i d_i\,\partial E/\partial v_i=\mathbf d^T\nabla E$. At an interior line-search minimum
$\lambda^\star$, this derivative is zero. Therefore
$\mathbf d^T\nabla E(\mathbf w+\lambda^\star\mathbf d)=0$: the new gradient is perpendicular to
the direction just searched.

### Exercise 7.14: mean and variance after standardization

> **Problem.** Using the sample definitions of a feature's mean and variance, prove directly that
> subtracting that mean and dividing by that standard deviation produces sample mean zero and
> sample variance one.

**Solution.**

Let $\widetilde x_{ni}=(x_{ni}-\mu_i)/\sigma_i$, with $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i^2$ defined from the
same $N$ samples. Then
$N^{-1}\sum_n\widetilde x_{ni}=(N\sigma_i)^{-1}(\sum_nx_{ni}-N\mu_i)=0$. Using that zero mean,

$$
\frac1N\sum_n\widetilde x_{ni}^2
=\frac1{N\sigma_i^2}\sum_n(x_{ni}-\mu_i)^2=1.
$$

The result uses the population-style divisor $N$. If sample variance used $N-1$, the matching
standardized variance convention would need the same divisor.

## Summary

- The Hessian eigenvectors are independent local directions; their eigenvalues set curvature and
  the stable learning-rate range.
- Mini-batches trade gradient variance against hardware efficiency, while initialization keeps
  forward and backward signals at usable scales.
- Momentum accumulates consistent directions; RMSProp and Adam rescale coordinates using running
  gradient moments.
- Data, batch, and layer normalization standardize different axes and solve different practical
  problems.
- Optimization finds low training error; regularization and validation determine whether that
  solution predicts well.
