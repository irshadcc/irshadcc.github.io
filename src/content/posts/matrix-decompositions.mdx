## Background

A matrix can look like a flat table of numbers, but multiplication turns that table into a
**linear map**: a rule that rotates, stretches, reflects, or collapses vectors while preserving
addition and scaling. The entries tell us everything, yet they rarely make the map's important
structure obvious.

A **matrix decomposition**, or **factorization**, rewrites one matrix as a product of simpler
matrices. This matters because structured matrices are easier to understand and cheaper to work
with. A triangular factor turns a hard solve into substitution. An orthogonal factor changes
coordinates without changing lengths. A diagonal factor exposes independent directions.

This post builds one picture for that idea, then develops four decompositions in order: LU for
linear systems, QR for least squares, eigendecomposition for invariant directions, and the
singular value decomposition (SVD) for the geometry and effective rank of any matrix. We will
work through each with real numbers, compare what the factors reveal, and finish with a guide for
choosing among them.

## The common idea: change the representation

Matrix products are functions applied from right to left. If $A=BC$, then

$$
Ax = B(Cx).
$$

Instead of applying the opaque map $A$ in one step, first apply $C$, then $B$. A useful
decomposition chooses factors whose actions have names: eliminate, rotate, scale, or permute.
The product has not changed. Only its representation has.

Decompositions also separate *work that depends on $A$* from *work that depends on the input*.
Factoring an $n\times n$ dense matrix usually costs on the order of $n^3$ operations. Once it is
factored, solving for each new right-hand side costs only on the order of $n^2$. That bargain is
especially valuable when the same matrix is reused.

## LU: save the work of elimination

**LU decomposition** records Gaussian elimination as

$$
A=LU,
$$

where $L$ is lower triangular and $U$ is upper triangular. For a general numerical matrix the
safer statement is $PA=LU$, where the **permutation matrix** $P$ records row swaps made by
partial pivoting. Pivoting avoids dividing by a zero or dangerously small pivot.

Consider

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

To remove the $2$ below the first pivot, subtract one half of row 1 from row 2. The multiplier
$1/2$ goes into $L$ and the eliminated result goes into $U$:

$$
L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1/2&1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
U=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\0&2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one entry of $LU$: the bottom-right entry is
$(1/2)(2)+(1)(2)=3$, exactly $A_{22}$. The other entries reconstruct $A$ in the same way.

Now solve $Ax=b$ for $b=[2,4]^T$. Introduce $y=Ux$ and solve in two easy passes:

$$
Ly=b
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix},
$$

then

$$
Ux=y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=\begin{bmatrix}-1/4\\3/2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first system uses **forward substitution**, from its top row down. The second uses
**back substitution**, from its bottom row up. Check the answer directly:
$Ax=[4(-1/4)+2(3/2),\,2(-1/4)+3(3/2)]^T=[2,4]^T$.

<DecompositionGeometry kind="lu" />

The grid makes the elimination view concrete. A triangular factor changes one coordinate using
coordinates already visited. In two dimensions, that operation is a shear: parallel grid lines
stay parallel while their angle changes. Forward and back substitution undo these ordered shears.

When $A$ is symmetric positive definite, LU has a more economical relative: the **Cholesky
decomposition** $A=LL^T$. It stores one triangular factor and is the standard choice for such
systems. Symmetric positive definite means $A=A^T$ and $x^TAx>0$ for every nonzero $x$.

## QR: separate directions from coordinates

The **QR decomposition** writes

$$
A=QR,
$$

where the columns of $Q$ are orthonormal and $R$ is upper triangular. **Orthonormal** means the
columns are mutually perpendicular unit vectors, so $Q^TQ=I$. Multiplication by $Q$ preserves
Euclidean lengths: $\lVert Qx\rVert_2=\lVert x\rVert_2$. That makes orthogonal transformations
numerically valuable because they do not magnify existing errors merely by changing coordinates.

QR is the natural tool for **least squares**: finding $x$ that minimizes
$\lVert b-Ax\rVert_2$ when there are more equations than unknowns. Take

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The columns of $A$ are not perpendicular. Gram--Schmidt gives the reduced QR factors

$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix}
1/\sqrt3&-1/\sqrt2\\
1/\sqrt3&0\\
1/\sqrt3&1/\sqrt2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
R=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3&\sqrt3\\0&\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Because $Q$ preserves length, the closest point in the column space is obtained by solving

$$
Rx=Q^Tb
=\begin{bmatrix}5/\sqrt3\\1/\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Back substitution gives $x_2=1/2$ and $x_1=7/6$. The fitted values are

$$
Ax=\begin{bmatrix}7/6\\5/3\\13/6\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
r=b-Ax=\begin{bmatrix}-1/6\\1/3\\-1/6\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check the geometry: $A^Tr=[0,0]^T$. The residual is perpendicular to every column of $A$, so no
small move within the column space can bring the fit closer to $b$.

<DecompositionGeometry kind="qr" />

The plot shows the same optimality condition in two dimensions. The fitted vector $A\hat x$ is
the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto the column space. The remaining residual $r$ meets that
space at a right angle; moving the fit in either direction along the line only increases its
length.

The normal equations $A^TAx=A^Tb$ produce the same exact solution, but they square the
2-norm condition number: $\kappa_2(A^TA)=\kappa_2(A)^2$. QR avoids that loss of sensitivity and
is generally the better direct method for a full-rank least-squares problem.<Sidenote>Production
QR implementations normally use Householder reflectors rather than classical Gram--Schmidt.
The latter is convenient for explaining the columns but can lose orthogonality in floating-point
arithmetic.</Sidenote>

## Eigendecomposition: find invariant directions

An **eigenvector** $v\ne0$ is a direction that a square matrix does not turn away from itself:

$$
Av=\lambda v.
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is its **eigenvalue**. If a real symmetric matrix has enough eigenvectors,
the spectral theorem collects them into an orthogonal matrix $Q$:

$$
A=Q\Lambda Q^T.
$$

The columns of $Q$ are eigenvectors and diagonal $\Lambda$ holds their eigenvalues. Read from
right to left, $Q^T$ changes into eigenvector coordinates, $\Lambda$ scales each coordinate
independently, and $Q$ changes back.

For

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix},
$$

the unit eigenvectors are $q_1=[1,1]^T/\sqrt2$ and
$q_2=[1,-1]^T/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalues $4$ and $2$. Thus

$$
Q=(1/\sqrt 2)\,\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Follow $x=[3,1]^T$ through the three stages:

| stage | coordinates |
| --- | ---: |
| $x$ | $[3,1]^T$ |
| $Q^Tx$ | $[2\sqrt2,\sqrt2]^T$ |
| $\Lambda Q^Tx$ | $[8\sqrt2,2\sqrt2]^T$ |
| $Q\Lambda Q^Tx$ | $[10,6]^T$ |

Direct multiplication gives $Ax=[10,6]^T$, so the pipeline checks out. More importantly, the
factorization explains the answer: the component along $[1,1]^T$ grows by four while the
component along $[1,-1]^T$ grows by two.

<DecompositionGeometry kind="eigen" />

Watch the two colored axes rather than the ellipse boundary. They retain their directions under
$A$, but their lengths become $4$ and $2$. Every other point combines those two independent
scalings, which is why the transformed circle is an ellipse aligned with the eigenvectors.

This makes repeated powers almost trivial:

$$
A^k=Q\Lambda^kQ^T.
$$

That identity explains long-run linear dynamics, covariance principal axes, and why the largest
magnitude eigenvalues dominate repeated multiplication. But eigendecomposition is not a
universal orthogonal factorization. A general real matrix may have complex eigenvalues or too few
independent eigenvectors. The clean $Q\Lambda Q^T$ form belongs to real symmetric matrices (and,
over the complex numbers, Hermitian matrices).

## SVD: expose the geometry of any matrix

The **singular value decomposition** works for every real $m\times n$ matrix, including
rectangular and rank-deficient ones:

$$
A=U\Sigma V^T.
$$

The columns of $V$ are orthonormal input directions. The nonnegative diagonal entries
$\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots$ of $\Sigma$ stretch those directions. The columns of $U$ are
the corresponding orthonormal output directions. Geometrically: rotate or reflect, stretch along
perpendicular axes, then rotate or reflect again.

Take the rank-deficient matrix from the diagram:

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its SVD is

$$
U=(1/\sqrt 5)\,\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\2&1\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},
\quad
V^T=(1/\sqrt 5)\,\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\-2&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first singular direction $v_1=[1,2]^T/\sqrt5$ is stretched by $5$ into
$u_1=[1,2]^T/\sqrt5$. The perpendicular direction
$v_2=[-2,1]^T/\sqrt5$ is multiplied by zero. Check it by hand:
$Av_2=[-2+2,\,-4+4]^T/\sqrt5=0$. The entire input plane collapses onto one line, so the matrix
has rank one.

<DecompositionGeometry kind="svd" />

The right plot has no area because one stretch is zero. This is the geometric meaning of lost
rank: many inputs that differ only along $v_2$ become the same output, so the map cannot be
inverted along that direction.

The SVD turns several abstract quantities into readable entries:

- The number of nonzero singular values is the rank.
- The largest stretch is $\lVert A\rVert_2=\sigma_1$.
- For a full-rank matrix, sensitivity is measured by
  $\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_1/\sigma_{\min}$.
- The pseudoinverse reverses nonzero stretches:
  $A^+=V\Sigma^+U^T$.

Keeping only the first $k$ singular triplets gives

$$
A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^T.
$$

The Eckart--Young theorem says this is a best rank-$k$ approximation in both spectral and
Frobenius norm. The discarded Frobenius error is
$\lVert A-A_k\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}$. This is why the SVD underlies compression,
denoising, latent semantic analysis, and principal component analysis: it orders independent
directions by how much of the matrix they carry.

## Choosing the decomposition

No factorization is simply "best." Choose the structure that matches the question and exploit
extra properties of the matrix when they exist.

| question | usual choice | structure gained | important condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solve $Ax=b$ for a general square $A$ | pivoted LU | two triangular solves | nonsingular $A$ |
| Solve a symmetric positive-definite system | Cholesky | one triangular factor and its transpose | $A=A^T$, $x^TAx>0$ |
| Fit an overdetermined full-rank system | QR | orthonormal basis plus triangular solve | independent columns |
| Find principal directions of a symmetric map | eigendecomposition | invariant axes and their scales | real symmetric $A$ |
| Diagnose rank or compute a minimum-norm solution | SVD | ordered input/output directions | works for any $m\times n$ matrix |
| Compress to rank $k$ | truncated SVD | best rank-$k$ approximation | choose an error tolerance |

Three practical cautions matter. First, do not form $A^{-1}$ merely to solve $Ax=b$; factor and
solve instead. Second, never omit pivoting from a general-purpose LU implementation just because
a classroom example does not need it. Third, numerical rank is scale- and tolerance-dependent:
a tiny singular value is not literally zero, but treating it as zero may be the stable choice.

The mathematical factors are often not stored as the diagram suggests. LAPACK, for example,
can represent $Q$ as a product of Householder reflectors rather than materializing every entry.
That saves work and storage while preserving the same factorization concept.

## Summary

- A decomposition replaces one opaque linear map with a sequence of structured maps.
- LU records elimination and makes repeated linear solves cheap; practical LU uses pivoting.
- QR separates orthonormal directions from coordinates and gives a stable route to least squares.
- Eigendecomposition reveals invariant directions, with the clean orthogonal form guaranteed for
  real symmetric matrices.
- SVD works for rectangular and rank-deficient matrices, exposing rank, sensitivity, null spaces,
  and optimal low-rank approximations.
