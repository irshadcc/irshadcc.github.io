## Background

A team ships a new checkout page and sales go up the next week. Did the page cause it? Maybe. But
the week may also have had a holiday, a marketing email or simply more visitors. When you compare
"before" with "after", time changes together with the product, so you cannot tell which one
moved the number.

An **A/B test**, also called an **online controlled experiment**, fixes this. Each user is
randomly sent to one of two versions: the **control** (A, what exists today) or the **treatment**
(B, the change). Both groups use the product during the same days. Because a coin flip decides
who goes where, the two groups are alike in every way except the change. So if the treatment group
buys more, the change is the most likely reason.

This post follows Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu's book *Trustworthy Online Controlled
Experiments* (2020).<Sidenote>The first author's name is Ron Kohavi; the book is sometimes searched for as "Kahavi".</Sidenote>
It walks through the book's five parts in order, one section per chapter, in plain language:

| book part | chapters | section here |

## Part I: The basics

### Why experiment

Ideas are cheap; knowing which ones help is hard. Most teams believe their ideas will work, but
when ideas are tested, most of them do not move the metric they were meant to move. The book
reports that at Microsoft about a third of experiments improved their target metric, and at Bing
and Google only about 10–20% did. Without testing, a team ships the losers along with the winners
and never learns which was which.

Controlled experiments sit at the top of the **hierarchy of evidence**: the ranking of ways to
learn what causes what. From weakest to strongest:

| evidence | example | why it can mislead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anecdote, expert opinion | "Users told me they hate the menu" | A few loud voices, no comparison |
| Before/after comparison | Sales the week after launch vs the week before | Time changed too: seasons, campaigns |
| Observational study | Users who use feature X buy more | Heavy users use X *and* buy more |
| Randomized controlled experiment | 50% get X, 50% do not | Only the change differs between groups |

Small changes can be worth a lot. One example from the book: a Bing engineer proposed making ad
headlines longer by moving text from the line below into the title. The idea sat in the backlog for
months. When it was finally tested, revenue rose by about 12%, worth over 100 million dollars a year
in the US alone. Nobody predicted it; the experiment found it.

Three conditions make experiments useful, and the book calls them **tenets**:

1. **The organization wants data-driven decisions** and has written down what "better" means
   (the metrics, see [the OEC](#the-overall-evaluation-criterion)).
2. **It can afford the infrastructure and tests** and can ship changes that are cheap enough to try.
3. **It accepts that it is poor at guessing** which ideas work.

### An end-to-end example

Suppose an online shop's checkout team thinks a shorter checkout form will increase purchases.
(The book's own example tests adding a coupon-code box to checkout; the steps are the same.)

**Step 1: write down the plan before starting.**

| choice | example |
| --- | --- |
| Hypothesis | A shorter form increases the share of users who buy |
| Randomization unit | Signed-in user |
| Control / treatment | Current form / short form |
| Main metric | Purchases ÷ users who reached checkout, over 14 days |
| Guardrails | Refund rate, page load time, support contacts |
| Practical significance | A lift smaller than 0.5 percentage points is not worth the work |

The **randomization unit** is what gets assigned to A or B. A **metric** is a number computed
from user behaviour. The **practical significance** boundary is the smallest change the business
cares about. It is a business choice, and it matters as much as any statistics.

**Step 2: run it.** Ten thousand users go to each version. Control has 1,000 purchases (10.0%).
Treatment has 1,050 purchases (10.5%). The treatment is 0.5 percentage points (pp) better.

**Step 3: ask whether that gap is real.** A 0.5 pp gap could be the form, or it could be luck: if
you split 20,000 people into two random halves, the halves never buy at exactly the same rate.
The p-value answers how big a gap luck alone tends to make.

### The p-value, in plain words

Imagine a world where the short form does **nothing**: both groups buy at exactly 10%. Run the same
experiment in that world thousands of times. Each run gives a slightly different gap, just from who
happened to land in which group. Most gaps are near zero; a few are large.

The **p-value** asks one question:

> In that no-effect world, how often does luck alone produce a gap **at least as big as the one we
> saw**?

The figure runs that thought experiment: 4,000 simulated experiments with 10,000 users per arm in
which the change does nothing. Look at where the orange line (our +0.5 pp) falls. Many grey bars
lie beyond it on either side. Those bars, coloured orange, are the no-effect experiments that
looked as good as ours, or as bad.

<NullWorld mode="pvalue" baseline={0.1} n={10000} observed={0.005} />

In this run, 936 of 4,000 no-effect experiments (23.4%) produced a gap of 0.5 pp or more in either
direction. So the p-value is about 0.23; the exact formula in [Part V](#the-statistics) gives 0.24.
A gap like ours happens almost one time in four *even when the change does nothing*. That is not
rare, so the data give us no good reason to believe the form helped.

Now drag "Observed gap" to 1.0 pp. Almost no grey experiments reach that far, so the p-value drops
to about 0.02. Such a gap would be surprising if the form did nothing, so we start to believe it
did something. Drag "Users per arm" up and the grey hill gets narrower: with more users, luck makes
smaller gaps, so the same 0.5 pp becomes harder to explain by chance.

**The decision rule.** Before the test, teams pick a cut-off, usually 0.05. If the p-value is below
it, the result is called **statistically significant**. The cut-off is called the **significance
level**, written $\alpha$ (alpha). Using $\alpha = 0.05$ means: when the change truly does
nothing, we will wrongly call it a win about 5% of the time. That mistake is a **false positive**
(or Type I error).

Three common misreadings:

| it is tempting to say | what is actually true |
| --- | --- |
| "p = 0.24, so there is a 24% chance the form does nothing" | The p-value assumes the form does nothing and asks how surprising the data are. It is not the chance that the assumption is true. |
| "p is above 0.05, so the form has no effect" | It means we could not tell. Here the 95% confidence interval runs from −0.34 to +1.34 pp: a small loss, nothing, or a useful gain are all still possible. |
| "p is tiny, so the effect is big" | With millions of users, even a 0.01 pp gap can have a tiny p-value. The p-value says nothing about size; the confidence interval does. |

A **confidence interval** is the range of effects that fit the data. For our test it is −0.34 to
+1.34 pp, which is wide: the experiment was too small to settle the question. That leads to power.

### Power, in plain words

The p-value guards against believing in effects that are not there. **Power** guards against the
opposite mistake: missing an effect that *is* there.

> If the change really works by a certain amount, how often will our experiment notice?

Think of a smoke detector. Its false-alarm rate is $\alpha$: how often it beeps when there is no
fire. Its power is how often it beeps when there *is* a fire. A detector that never beeps has no
false alarms and is useless.

The figure adds a second world, in blue, where the short form really adds 1 pp (10% → 11%). The
dashed lines are the cut-off: with 10,000 users per arm and $\alpha = 0.05$, a gap must be beyond
±0.83 pp to count as significant. Each blue experiment beyond the dashed line is a success; each
faded blue one is a real effect we missed.

<NullWorld mode="power" baseline={0.1} n={10000} lift={0.01} />

Here 65.6% of the blue experiments clear the line, so the power is about 66% (the formula gives
63.6%). One time in three, this experiment would fail to detect a real 1 pp improvement. Missing a
real effect is a **false negative** (or Type II error).

Drag "Users per arm" to the right. Both hills get narrower, they overlap less, and more blue
experiments clear the line. That is the main way to get more power: more users. Teams usually aim
for 80% power at the smallest effect they care about, the **minimum detectable effect** (MDE).

The next figure turns this into a planning tool. It shows power against sample size; the marker shows
how many users per arm are needed for 80% power.

<PowerExplorer baseline={0.1} lift={0.01} alpha={0.05} targetPower={0.8} />

For a 10% baseline and a 1 pp MDE, the answer is 14,751 users per arm. Our test had 10,000, which
is why it came out inconclusive:

| users per arm | power to detect 10% → 11% |
| ---: | ---: |
| 1,000 | 11.3% |
| 5,000 | 37.1% |
| 10,000 | 63.6% |
| 15,000 | 80.7% |
| 30,000 | 97.9% |

Four things raise power:

- **More users.** Noise shrinks with the square root of the sample size, so detecting an effect
  half as big needs about **four times** as many users.
- **A bigger real effect.** Bold changes are easier to detect than tweaks.
- **A less noisy metric.** "Did the user buy?" is less noisy than "how much did they spend?" (see
  [variance](#variance-and-sensitivity)).
- **A looser $\alpha$.** Easier to pass, but more false alarms; this is a trade, not free power.

**Reading the result.** Putting p-value, confidence interval and practical significance together
gives a short decision table:

| result | what to do |
| --- | --- |
| Significant, and the whole interval is above the practical boundary | Launch (if guardrails pass) |
| Significant, but the effect may be smaller than the boundary | Probably not worth it; maybe rerun with more power |
| Not significant, interval is narrow around zero | The change does little; stop |
| Not significant, interval is wide (our case) | Inconclusive; run longer or with more users |

### Twyman's law and trustworthiness

**Twyman's law** says, roughly: a number that looks surprising or extraordinary is usually wrong.
When a test shows a huge win, first suspect a bug. Typical traps:

- **Peeking.** Checking the p-value every day and stopping at the first value below 0.05 inflates
  false alarms. With 14 daily looks, about 22% of no-effect tests "win" instead of 5% (see the
  [A/A figure](#the-aa-test)).
- **Many metrics.** Look at 100 metrics and about 5 will be "significant" by luck alone.
- **Broken randomization.** If the split is not the planned 50/50, something filtered users
  unevenly; see [SRM](#sample-ratio-mismatch).
- **Survivorship and post-treatment filters.** Analysing "users who stayed for 30 days" ignores the
  ones the treatment drove away. Always analyse everyone who was assigned (**intention to treat**).
- **Leftovers from earlier tests.** Users who just saw a bad treatment may still be unhappy when
  re-used in a new test (**carryover**).
- **Novelty and primacy.** A new design gets clicks because it is new (**novelty effect**), or
  loses at first because users are used to the old one (**primacy effect**). Both fade over time.

**Simpson's paradox** shows how combining periods can flip a result. Suppose a test runs at 1%
treatment on day 1 and 50% on day 2:

| day | control users | control buys | treatment users | treatment buys |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Day 1 (1% ramp) | 99,000 | 1,980 (2.00%) | 1,000 | 23 (2.30%) |
| Day 2 (50/50) | 50,000 | 500 (1.00%) | 50,000 | 550 (1.10%) |
| Both days | 149,000 | 2,480 (1.66%) | 51,000 | 573 (1.12%) |

Treatment wins on each day but loses overall. The reason: day 1 had a higher buying rate, and
control has most of its users on day 1. Compare like with like (day by day), or analyse only the
period with a fixed split.

### Platform and culture

An organization usually grows into experimentation in stages. The book calls them **crawl, walk,
run, fly**:

| stage | roughly how many tests a year | what it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crawl | ~10 | Build basic logging and one-off analyses; prove a test can be trusted |
| Walk | ~50 | Standard metrics, A/A tests, SRM checks, a simple platform |
| Run | ~250 | Most changes are tested; an agreed OEC; many tests at once |
| Fly | thousands | Testing is the default for every change; results feed a shared memory |

Leadership matters as much as code. Leaders must accept that most ideas fail, judge teams by
outcomes in experiments rather than by features shipped, and be willing to stop a favourite project
when the data say no.

A platform has four parts: an interface where owners set up experiments, assignment of
users to variants, instrumentation that logs what happened, and the analysis that
turns logs into a scorecard. Click a box to see what it does.

<BoxDiagram nodes={platformNodes} edges={platformEdges} selected="assign" title="Parts of an experimentation platform" />

To run many tests at once, the assignment service splits users into **buckets** using a hash of the
user ID. Inside one **layer**, each user belongs to only one experiment, so tests that touch the
same feature never collide. Different layers re-hash the same users independently, so a checkout
test and a ranking test can share the same traffic. Hover over a user to follow them.

<ExperimentLayers users={12} buckets={16} />

The figure shows 12 users and 16 buckets so it fits on screen; real systems use thousands of
buckets. The bucket is computed as

$$
\text{bucket} = \operatorname{hash}(\text{user ID}, \text{layer ID}) \bmod B,
$$

where $B$ is the number of buckets. For example, with 10,000 buckets, checkout experiment E1 can own
buckets 0–999 (half control, half treatment, so 10% of traffic), and search experiment E2 can own
1,000–2,999 in the same layer. A ranking test in another layer can use all 10,000 buckets at once.
Never change the bucket count of a running layer: that reshuffles users between variants.

## Part II: Topics for everyone

### Speed matters

Many changes make a page slower as a side effect: a bigger model, an extra call, more images. How
much does slowness cost? You cannot test "make it faster" cheaply, but you can test the reverse: a
**slowdown experiment** adds a deliberate delay (say 100 ms) for the treatment group and measures the
loss.

If a small delay hurts the metric by some amount, a small speed-up of the same size helps by about
the same amount, as long as the change is small enough to treat the curve as a straight line. The
book reports that at Bing every 100 ms of speed-up was worth about 0.6% of revenue. This gives teams
an exchange rate: a feature that adds 50 ms must win at least about 0.3% revenue to break even.

Where you add the delay matters. Slowing the part users see first (the top of the page) hurts more
than slowing content far below.

### Organizational metrics

Before you can test, you need to agree on what to measure. The book sorts metrics by purpose:

| type | question it answers | example (video app) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Goal** (success) metric | What does the company ultimately want? | Long-term retention, revenue |
| **Driver** (surrogate) metric | What moves quickly and predicts the goal? | Days active per week, satisfied sessions |
| **Guardrail** metric | What must not get worse? | Page speed, crash rate, unsubscribes |
| **Diagnostic** (debug) metric | Why did something move? | Clicks per position, error counts |

Good driver metrics are **aligned** with the goal, **actionable** (teams can move them), **sensitive**
(they move within a test's duration) and **hard to game**. Watch out for Goodhart's law: once a
number becomes a target, people find ways to move the number without moving the thing it stood for.

### The overall evaluation criterion

The **overall evaluation criterion** (OEC) is the one metric, or small weighted combination, that
decides whether a test wins. It should be measurable in a short test, yet predict long-term value.

Picking it is harder than it looks. The book tells of a Bing bug that showed worse search results:
queries per user went up about 10% and ad revenue per user about 30%. Users were searching more
because they could not find what they wanted. "Queries per user" would have rewarded a broken
product; "sessions per user" (people coming back) is a better OEC for search.

A worked example with made-up numbers: a shop tests sending more promotional emails. Per user,
treatment brings in 0.40 dollars more in email revenue, but 0.5% more users unsubscribe. If each
unsubscribed user would have brought 100 dollars of future email revenue, the hidden cost is
$0.005 \times 100 = 0.50$ dollars per user. The OEC "email revenue minus expected unsubscribe cost"
is $0.40 - 0.50 = -0.10$ dollars per user: the test loses, even though short-term revenue went up.

### Institutional memory and meta-analysis

Once a company has run hundreds of experiments, the collection itself is valuable. Keeping every
test's hypothesis, design and result in a searchable store (**institutional memory**) lets
teams:

- see which kinds of ideas tend to win and which tend to fail,
- learn how often metrics move together, to pick better drivers and OECs,
- avoid re-running a test someone already ran,
- estimate realistic effect sizes, which feeds power planning.

Studying many past experiments together is **meta-analysis**.

### Ethics

Experiments test changes on real people, so they need rules. The book borrows three principles
from the Belmont Report, the standard US guide for research on people:

- **Respect for persons:** be open with users and let them choose where possible.
- **Beneficence:** keep risk low and make benefits worth it.
- **Justice:** do not put the risks on one group and the benefits on another.

Useful questions before a test: Would a user be surprised or upset to learn about it? Is the risk
more than they face in normal use? Would we be comfortable shipping either variant to everyone?
(If every variant is something you would ship anyway, testing it is usually low risk.) Is the data
collected the minimum needed, and is it protected? Tests that deliberately harm or deceive users,
such as a social network changing people's feeds to see if it changes their mood, cross the line
and need review.

## Part III: Other ways to learn

### Complementary techniques

A/B tests tell you *what* happened at scale, not *why*. Other methods fill that gap. They trade
**scale** (how many users) against **depth** (how much you learn from each):

| method | users | good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logs analysis | Millions | Finding what people already do; sizing an opportunity |
| Human evaluation (raters) | Hundreds of judgements | Labelling relevance or quality |
| User experience research (lab, interviews, eye tracking) | ~10 | Seeing *why* users struggle |
| Focus groups | ~10 per group | Early reactions to an idea |
| Surveys | Thousands | Questions logs cannot answer (satisfaction, intent) |
| External data | Varies | Benchmarks, competitors, panels |

These methods generate ideas to test and help validate metrics. They rarely replace the test itself.

### Observational causal studies

Sometimes you cannot randomize: the change is a whole-company switch, a price rule, or something
users choose themselves. **Observational causal studies** try to estimate the effect from data that
were not randomized:

| method | idea in one line |
| --- | --- |
| Interrupted time series | Predict what the metric would have done without the change, compare with what it did |
| Interleaving | Mix two rankers' results in one list and see which one's items get clicked |
| Regression discontinuity | Compare users just above and just below a cut-off (e.g. age 21) |
| Instrumental variables | Use something random that pushes some users into the treatment |
| Propensity score matching | Compare treated users with similar-looking untreated users |
| Difference in differences | Compare the change over time in a treated region with an untreated one |

All of these rest on assumptions you cannot fully check, and they fail when there is a hidden common
cause. The book's example: Office 365 users who saw more error messages were *less* likely to
leave. Errors do not keep users; heavy users simply see more errors *and* are loyal. Studies that
compared observational estimates of ad effects with real experiments (at Facebook, for example)
found the observational ones were often far off. Use these methods when you must, and prefer an
experiment when you can.

## Part IV: Building an experimentation platform

### Client-side experiments

Web servers can change what users see instantly. Phone and desktop apps cannot: new code ships
through an app store, users update when they like, and many versions are in use at once. This
changes how tests work:

- **Ship the code early, switch it on later.** Put both variants in the app behind a **feature
  flag** and turn the treatment on from the server.
- **Expect delayed and missing data.** Phones go offline and batch their logs; some events arrive
  days late or never.
- **Trigger at the right moment.** A user is assigned when the app fetches its configuration, which
  may be long before they reach the changed screen.
- **Watch app health.** Measure crashes, start-up time, battery and data use as guardrails.

### Instrumentation

You can only analyse what you log. **Instrumentation** is the code that records what users saw and
did. Client-side logs (in the browser or app) see what users actually did: scrolls, what was on
screen, time spent. Server-side logs are more reliable and complete but miss what happened on the
device. Most platforms use both and join them with a shared ID. Every event should carry the
experiment and variant IDs, and teams should treat missing or broken logging as a bug that blocks
launch.

### Choosing a randomization unit

The randomization unit is the thing that gets assigned to A or B:

| unit | good for | problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Page view | Invisible backend changes (e.g. latency) | One user sees both variants |
| Session | Changes that do not carry across visits | Same user switches between variants |
| User (signed-in ID or cookie) | Most product changes | Cookies get deleted, so one person can become two |
| Group (company, household, market) | Collaborative or shared features | Far fewer units, so less power |

Two rules of thumb. First, the randomization unit should be at least as large as the unit you
analyse: if you randomize by user, compute metrics per user, not per page view. Second, if users
could notice that the product keeps changing, randomize by user so their experience stays
consistent.

### Ramping

A new change should not go to everyone at once. **Ramping** means increasing the treatment's share
of traffic in steps. The book balances three goals: **speed** (decide quickly), **quality**
(measure precisely) and **risk** (limit harm if something is broken). It describes four phases:

1. **Limit risk:** start small (employees, then 1%, then 5%) and watch for crashes and big drops.
2. **Measure:** move to the **maximum power ramp** (MPR), normally 50/50 because an even split gives
   the most power, and run for about a week to cover weekdays and weekends.
3. **Scale up:** ramp to 100% while watching servers and capacity.
4. **Long-term holdout (optional):** keep a small group, say 5%, on the old version for weeks or
   months to measure lasting effects.

Only the MPR phase is used for the main statistical decision; the early small phases are for
catching disasters, not for reading p-values.

### Scaling experiment analyses

Analysing thousands of experiments a day needs an automated pipeline:

1. **Process the data:** collect logs, remove bots, join client and server events, and attach each
   event to the user's variants.
2. **Compute:** first build one row per user and metric, then compute means, variances, confidence
   intervals and p-values for each experiment.
3. **Summarise:** a **scorecard** shows the OEC first, then guardrails and trust checks, then
   diagnostic metrics, with segments (country, device, new vs returning users) one click away.

## Part V: Analysing experiments

### The statistics

This section puts formulas behind the [p-value](#the-p-value-in-plain-words) and
[power](#power-in-plain-words) ideas from Part I.

**The difference and its noise.** The effect estimate is the difference in averages,
$\Delta = \bar Y_T - \bar Y_C$. Its **standard error** (how much $\Delta$ would wobble if you reran
the test) is

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\Delta) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_T^2}{n_T} + \frac{\sigma_C^2}{n_C}},
$$

where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of the metric across users and $n$ is the number of users in each
arm. For a conversion rate $p$, the variance per user is $p(1-p)$.

**The test statistic.** Divide the gap by its noise: $z = \Delta / \operatorname{SE}$. In the
no-effect world, $z$ follows a bell curve (standard normal), so the two-sided p-value is

$$
p\text{-value} = 2\bigl(1 - \Phi(|z|)\bigr),
$$

with $\Phi$ the normal cumulative distribution function. (For small samples the book uses the
two-sample *t*-test, which is almost identical once each arm has a few hundred users.)

**Worked example** (the checkout test). With a pooled rate of $2{,}050/20{,}000 = 0.1025$:

$$
\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{0.1025 \times 0.8975 \times \left(\tfrac{1}{10{,}000} + \tfrac{1}{10{,}000}\right)} = 0.00429,
\qquad z = \frac{0.005}{0.00429} = 1.17,
$$

so the p-value is $2(1 - \Phi(1.17)) = 0.244$. The 95% confidence interval is
$0.005 \pm 1.96 \times 0.00429$, or $-0.34$ to $+1.34$ pp. Check: $1.96 \times 0.429 = 0.84$, and
$0.5 - 0.84 = -0.34$.

**Sample size.** A handy rule from the book for 80% power at $\alpha = 0.05$ is

$$
n \approx \frac{16\,\sigma^2}{\delta^2} \text{ users per arm},
$$

where $\delta$ is the MDE. For the checkout test, $\sigma^2 = 0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.09$ and
$\delta = 0.01$, so $n \approx 16 \times 0.09 / 0.0001 = 14{,}400$, close to the exact 14,751. The
16 comes from $2 \times (1.96 + 0.84)^2 \approx 15.7$.

**When is the bell curve safe?** The normal approximation needs enough users. The book's rule of
thumb is $n > 355\, s^2$ per arm, where $s$ is the skewness of the metric. Revenue is often very
skewed: with $s = 10$ you need more than 35,500 users per arm. Capping extreme values reduces $s$.

**Many tests at once.** Each metric tested at 0.05 adds a 5% chance of a false alarm. Simple
defences: decide the OEC and a few key metrics in advance; use a stricter threshold for metrics you
did not expect to move (the book suggests tiers such as 0.05, 0.01 and 0.001); or use a correction
such as Benjamini–Hochberg. Be careful with surprising segment results: with 50 segments, a couple
will look "significant" by chance.

**Most significant results are not as strong as they look.** If only 10 of 100 ideas truly work and
the test has 80% power, you expect 8 true wins and $0.05 \times 90 = 4.5$ false ones. Only
$8 / 12.5 = 64\%$ of "winners" are real. Replicating surprising wins helps.

### Variance and sensitivity

A metric's variance controls how many users you need, so getting it right, and making it smaller,
pays off.

**Getting it right.** The simple formula assumes each row is an independent unit. Ratio metrics such
as **click-through rate** (CTR, clicks ÷ page views) break that when you randomize by user: one
user's page views are related. Treating each page view as independent makes the interval too narrow
and gives false wins. Use the **delta method** (a formula for the variance of a ratio of two
averages) or the **bootstrap** (resample users many times and measure the spread). The same care is
needed for relative changes ("+2%"), because the control mean in the denominator is also noisy.

**Making it smaller** (more sensitive tests):

- **CUPED** (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data): subtract the part of each user's
  metric that their behaviour *before* the test predicts. If pre-test data explain 36% of the
  variance, the variance shrinks to $1 - 0.36 = 0.64$ of its old value, and the required sample
  shrinks by the same factor.
- **Triggering:** analyse only users who could have been affected (see [triggering](#triggering)).
- **Capping and binarizing:** cap extreme values (bots, bulk buyers), or switch from "revenue" to
  "made a purchase".
- **Stratification:** split users into similar groups (by country, device) and combine.
- **Pick less noisy metrics:** a metric with smaller variance relative to its effect.

### The A/A test

An **A/A test** splits users into two groups that get the *same* experience. Since nothing differs,
it should "find" an effect only about 5% of the time, and its p-values should be spread evenly
between 0 and 1. If not, something in the platform or the analysis is broken.

The figure runs 4,000 A/A tests of 10,000 users per arm over 14 days. Checked once at the end, the
bars are flat and 5.0% land below 0.05. Tick "Peek after each of 14 days": now each test is
checked daily and stopped at the first p below 0.05, and 21.9% of tests "find" something that does
not exist.

<NullWorld mode="aa" baseline={0.1} n={10000} />

Common reasons an A/A test fails:

- **Wrong variance**, for example treating page views as independent when users were randomized.
- **Very skewed metrics** where the normal approximation is not yet valid.
- **Carryover**: one group was in a previous experiment and still behaves differently.
- **Bots, broken hashing or uneven data loss.**

Run A/A tests continuously in the background: they are the platform's smoke alarm.

### Triggering

Many changes affect only some users. A checkout change only matters to users who reach checkout.
Including everyone else adds noise without signal. **Triggering** means analysing only the users who
reached the point where the variants differ (in both arms, using the same rule).

A worked example with made-up numbers. 10% of users reach checkout; they spend 30 dollars on
average (standard deviation 60), and everyone else spends 2 dollars. The treatment raises
checkout users' spending by 3%.

- **Triggered analysis:** effect 0.90 dollars on a standard deviation of 60. With the
  [16σ²/δ² rule](#the-statistics), that needs about 71,000 triggered users per arm, which means
  about 711,000 assigned users per arm.
- **Everyone:** the effect is diluted to 0.09 dollars per user, and the standard deviation across all
  users is about 22.8. The rule gives about 1,028,000 users per arm, 1.45 times more.

To report the effect on the whole site, scale it back: overall change = triggered change × the
triggered users' share of the metric. Here $3\% \times (3 / 4.8) = 1.875\%$, because triggered users
bring 3.00 of the 4.80 dollars spent per user.

The trigger must be decided the same way in both arms. A good approach is **counterfactual
logging**: in control, also compute what the treatment would have shown and log whether it would
have been different.

### Sample ratio mismatch

If you planned 50/50, you should get close to 50/50. A **sample ratio mismatch** (SRM) means the
counts differ more than luck allows. It is the single most useful trust check: an SRM means some
users were lost or added unevenly, so the groups are no longer comparable and the results cannot be
trusted.

Check with a chi-squared test. Example: 50,000 users in control and 49,000 in treatment. Expected is
49,500 each, so

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{500^2}{49{,}500} + \frac{500^2}{49{,}500} = 10.1, \qquad p = 0.0015.
$$

That is very unlikely by chance: stop and debug. (A split of 50,000 vs 49,700 gives $p = 0.34$,
which is fine.) Common causes:

- The treatment redirects users to a new page, and some are lost on the way.
- The treatment crashes or logs differently, so some of its users disappear from the data.
- Bot filtering removes more users from one arm.
- The trigger condition depends on the treatment.
- Assignment was changed mid-test, or the hash is not uniform.

Other **trust guardrails** worth checking in every test: fraction of events logged
(**telemetry fidelity**), cache hit rates, cookie write rates, and any metric the treatment should
not be able to move (country mix, pre-test activity).

### Leakage and interference

The analysis assumes one user's variant does not affect another user's outcome (the **stable unit
treatment value assumption**, or SUTVA). That breaks in two ways:

- **Direct connections:** on a social network, a treatment user's new sharing feature sends
  messages to control users, so control changes too.
- **Indirect connections** through a shared resource: a two-sided marketplace (riders and drivers),
  advertiser budgets, a shared machine-learning model trained on both arms' clicks, or shared
  servers and caches.

When it happens, the measured difference is wrong, often too small or even the wrong sign. Fixes:

| approach | how |
| --- | --- |
| Split the resource | Give each arm its own budget, inventory or model |
| Geo randomization | Assign whole cities or regions |
| Time-based (switchback) | Switch the whole market between A and B in time blocks |
| Cluster randomization | Assign groups of connected friends together |
| Ego-centric randomization | Treat a user and their direct connections as one cluster |
| Rule of thumb | Credit actions with their downstream value (e.g. count messages received too) |

Each of these has fewer independent units than user-level tests, so it has less power.

### Long-term effects

What you measure in a two-week test may not be what you get after a year:

- **Users learn.** Novelty wears off; or users slowly learn to ignore ads, or to trust a better
  feature.
- **Network effects** take time to spread.
- **Delayed outcomes:** a subscription renewal happens after the test ends.
- **The world changes:** competitors, seasons and other launches shift the baseline.

The figure is an illustration (made-up curves, not data): a novelty effect that starts at +3% and
settles at +0.5%, and a primacy effect that starts negative and ends positive. A one-week test
would get both wrong.

Ways to measure long-term effects:

- **Run long:** keep the test going for months, while watching for cookie churn and drop-outs.
- **Cohort analysis:** follow a fixed group of users who were stable (for example, signed in).
- **Post-period analysis:** after the test, give both groups the same experience and see whether
  the old treatment group still behaves differently. Google used this to show that users learn to
  respond less to ads when ad load is high.
- **Time-staggered starts:** start several treatment groups at different times and compare.
- **Holdbacks and reverse tests:** after launching, keep a small group on the old version, or switch
  a group back, and measure the difference weeks later.

## A/B tests for recommendations and ads

The book's ideas apply directly to ranking and advertising systems, but these systems add their own
traps. A recommender chooses what users see, where it appears, and what tomorrow's model learns
from. An ad system adds an auction with budgets and prices. This section walks through the most
common problems, each with a small example.

**Pick the metric from the product question.** Click-through rate (CTR) is a diagnostic, not a goal:
clicks can come from a catchy thumbnail or a high position.

| system | randomization unit | main metric | guardrails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recommendation ranker | User | Satisfied sessions or retention per user | Latency, hides and reports, diversity |
| Ad campaign lift | User or region | Extra conversions caused by the ads | Ad frequency, complaints |
| Ad ranker or auction | User, or region/time if markets are shared | Revenue per user and advertiser value | Ad load, relevance, advertiser return |

#### Problem 1: the denominator changes

Treatment gets more users active but fewer minutes per active user. Who won?

| arm | assigned | active | minutes per active user | total minutes | minutes per assigned user |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Control | 10,000 | 6,000 | 30 | 180,000 | 18.00 |
| Treatment | 10,000 | 6,500 | 29 | 188,500 | 18.85 |

Per active user, treatment looks 3.3% worse; per assigned user, it is 4.7% better. "Active" happens
*after* the treatment and the treatment changed who is in that group, so always use all assigned
users for the main metric and report the rest as breakdowns.

Also do not treat each impression as an independent data point. If each user gives 20 impressions
and those are slightly correlated (correlation 0.05), the **design effect** is
$1 + (20 - 1) \times 0.05 = 1.95$: one million impressions carry about as much information as
512,821 independent ones. Compute metrics per user.

#### Problem 2: position bias

Items at the top get clicked more because they are at the top. A whole-ranker A/B test still answers
the launch question ("are users better off with the new ranker?"), but it does not tell you whether
individual items are more relevant. Compare the two policies per user; do not compare CTR on the
items each one happened to show.

#### Problem 3: the test changes its own training data

If both arms' models retrain on a shared log of clicks, control learns from treatment and vice versa,
which breaks SUTVA (Google researchers call this **symbiosis bias**). Options:

1. **Freeze both models** during the test: clean, but measures the short-term effect only.
2. **Train each model only on its own arm's data:** isolates learning, but each model sees less data.
3. **Split users and the training data together**, so each policy lives in its own world.
4. **Randomize clusters** of connected users, creators or markets.

Say in the report which one you used.

#### Problem 4: attribution is not incrementality

An advertiser sees 900 purchases after ad clicks. How many did the ads *cause*? Ads are shown to
people already likely to buy, so you need a test: hide the advertiser's ads from a random control
group. (A **ghost ad** design also logs, in control, the auctions the ad *would* have won.)

| arm | users | purchases | rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Ads hidden (control) | 50,000 | 2,000 | 4.00% |
| Ads shown (treatment) | 50,000 | 2,125 | 4.25% |

The lift is 0.25 pp, which is $50{,}000 \times 0.0025 = 125$ extra purchases, not 900 or 2,125. The
95% interval is 0.003 to 0.497 pp, with $p = 0.047$: significant, but barely, so the size is still
uncertain. If each purchase earns 80 dollars of margin and the campaign cost 15,000 dollars, the
incremental return on ad spend is $125 \times 80 / 15{,}000 = 0.67$: the campaign loses money. The
attribution count would have claimed $900 \times 80 / 15{,}000 = 4.8$.

#### Problem 5: auctions and budgets connect the arms

If treatment wins more auctions early in the day, it can spend an advertiser's daily budget, leaving
fewer ads for control later. The arms are no longer independent. Randomize by region if markets are
separate, or run a **switchback** test that switches the whole market between A and B in time blocks.

#### Guardrails with a harm margin

"No significant drop" is a weak guardrail: a small test almost never shows a significant drop. Instead
pick a **non-inferiority margin**: the largest harm you accept. The guardrail passes only if the upper
end of the confidence interval for the harm is below that margin.

Example: complaint rate is 0.20% in control and 0.22% in treatment, and we accept at most +0.05 pp.
With 100,000 users per arm the increase is 0.020 pp with standard error 0.0205 pp. The one-sided 95%
upper bound is $0.020 + 1.645 \times 0.0205 = 0.0537$ pp, which is above 0.05: **fail**, because we
cannot rule out unacceptable harm. With 200,000 users per arm and the same rates, the bound is 0.0438
pp: **pass**. So size the test for guardrails too, not only for the main metric. For very rare
harms, remember the rule of three: if zero events occur in $n$ users, the 95% upper bound on the rate
is about $3/n$.

#### Sample sizes for typical metrics

**Watch time.** Mean 20 minutes per user per day, standard deviation 40, MDE 1 minute (5%). Using
$n = 2\sigma^2 (1.96 + 0.84)^2 / \delta^2$:

$$
n = \frac{2 \times 40^2 \times 2.80^2}{1^2} \approx 25{,}117 \text{ users per arm}.
$$

With CUPED removing 36% of the variance, that drops to $25{,}117 \times 0.64 = 16{,}075$. If users
come in clusters of 100 with within-cluster correlation 0.02, multiply by the design effect
$1 + 99 \times 0.02 = 2.98$, giving about 74,849.

**Rare ad conversion.** Baseline 2.0%, MDE 5% relative (2.0% → 2.1%, only 0.1 pp). The two-proportion
formula gives 315,206 users per arm. Doubling the MDE to 10% relative cuts this to 80,682, about a
quarter, because sample size scales with $1/\delta^2$.

#### Holdouts

A **holdout** is a random group that does not get a change. Different holdouts answer different
questions:

| holdout | question |
| --- | --- |
| Normal control | Should this one change launch? |
| Campaign suppression | What did this ad campaign cause? |
| Long-lived holdout (e.g. 2% of users for a quarter) | What is the total, lasting effect of all launches? |

Adding up the lifts of individual tests usually overstates the total, because effects overlap and
fade. A long-lived holdout measures the bundle directly. Keep its assignment fixed, apply safety
fixes to everyone, and keep it as small as possible, since those users miss improvements.

#### Quick interview answers

- **Offline NDCG went up, online engagement went down?** Offline logs come from the old ranker and
  only show feedback on items it chose; latency or diversity may also have changed. Trust the online
  test.
- **CTR up 3%, revenue down 2%?** Do not ship on CTR. Break revenue down into ad load, clicks, price
  and conversion, and decide on the pre-chosen OEC.
- **Purchases arrive up to 7 days after a click?** Give every user the full 7-day window before
  comparing, so control and treatment are measured alike.
- **How long to run?** Until the planned sample size is reached, covering at least full weeks, and
  long enough for delayed outcomes and novelty to settle.

## Summary

- Random assignment makes the two groups alike except for the change, so a difference in outcomes
  measures what the change caused.
- The **p-value** asks: if the change did nothing, how often would luck give a gap this large? It is
  not the chance the change does nothing, and it says nothing about size. The **confidence interval**
  shows the size.
- **Power** is the chance the test detects an effect that really exists. Choose the MDE first, then
  the sample size (about $16\sigma^2/\delta^2$ per arm for 80% power); half the effect needs four
  times the users.
- Trust before impact: follow Twyman's law, run A/A tests, check for sample ratio mismatch, do not
  peek, and analyse everyone assigned.
- A good OEC predicts long-term value; guardrails protect what must not get worse; interference,
  feedback loops and long-term effects need special designs.
