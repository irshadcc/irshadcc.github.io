## Background

A product metric rises after a new ranking model ships. The model may have caused the rise, but
the calendar, a marketing campaign, or a different mix of users may have caused it too. A
before-and-after comparison cannot separate those explanations because time changed along with
the product.

An **A/B test**, or **online controlled experiment**, solves this by randomly assigning units to
a **control**, which receives the current experience, or a **treatment**, which receives the
change. Both groups run at the same time. Randomization makes their expected composition equal,
so a systematic difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment. This is why controlled
experiments are useful for model launches as well as button colours: they measure causal impact,
not merely offline model quality.

This post follows Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang, and Ya Xu's *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments*.<Sidenote>The author's name is Ron Kohavi; the book is sometimes found through the misspelling “Kahavi.”</Sidenote>
We will design one experiment, distinguish statistical power from the p-value, compare testing
methods, apply them to recommendation and advertising systems, and then build up the layers and
buckets that let a platform run many experiments at once.

## From a product question to an experiment

Suppose a checkout team believes a shorter form will increase completed purchases. A useful
experiment specification fixes six choices *before* looking at results:

| choice | example |
| --- | --- |
| Hypothesis | A shorter form increases checkout conversion |
| Randomization unit | Signed-in user |
| Control | Existing form |
| Treatment | Short form |
| Primary metric | Purchases / eligible users over 14 days |
| Guardrails | Refund rate, page latency, support contacts |

The **randomization unit** is the object assigned once to a variant. User-level assignment keeps
a returning user in one experience. Session-level assignment gives more independent-looking
observations, but the same person can see both forms; that can cause learning or confusion and
violates the assumption that units do not affect one another. For a collaborative feature, even
user assignment can be wrong: everyone in a company or conversation may need the same variant,
so the tenant or group becomes the unit.

The **analysis unit** is the object over which the metric is computed. If assignment is by user,
an analysis that treats every page view as independent invents precision: page views from one
person are correlated. Aggregate to the user or use a variance estimator that respects the
clusters.

The **overall evaluation criterion** (OEC) is the small set of metrics used to decide whether the
change is good. The primary metric should capture the intended value; guardrails catch damage
elsewhere. A ranking change may improve clicks while making pages slower. Declaring hundreds of
metrics to be independent reasons to ship all but guarantees chance “wins.”

### The estimand and estimator

Let $Y_i(1)$ be user $i$'s outcome under treatment and $Y_i(0)$ the outcome under control. The
individual treatment effect $Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)$ cannot be observed: each user sees only one variant.
The target is therefore the **average treatment effect**

$$
\tau = \mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)].
$$

With random assignment, the difference in sample means is an unbiased estimator:

$$
\widehat\tau = \overline Y_T-\overline Y_C.
$$

Randomization does the causal work. The statistical test then asks whether the observed
difference is distinguishable from sampling noise.

## What a p-value says

Start with a **null hypothesis** $H_0$: the treatment effect is zero. The alternative $H_1$ says
it is not zero. For two conversion rates, a large-sample test statistic is

$$
z = \frac{\widehat p_T-\widehat p_C}
{\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)(1/n_T+1/n_C)}},
$$

where $\widehat p$ pools successes across both arms. Under $H_0$ and the test assumptions, $z$
is approximately standard normal.

The **p-value** is the probability, *assuming the null hypothesis and the model are true*, of a
test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. For a two-sided z-test,

$$
p\text{-value}=2\left[1-\Phi(|z|)\right].
$$

It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the
result happened “by chance.” It does not measure the size or business value of the effect. A tiny
effect can have a tiny p-value with enough traffic; a valuable effect can miss significance in a
small experiment.

### A worked result

Take 10,000 users in each arm. Control has 1,000 purchases and treatment has 1,050:

$$
\widehat p_C=0.100,\qquad
\widehat p_T=0.105,\qquad
\widehat\tau=0.005.
$$

The pooled rate is $2{,}050/20{,}000=0.1025$, giving

$$
\operatorname{SE}_{H_0}
=\sqrt{0.1025(1-0.1025)(1/10{,}000+1/10{,}000)}
=0.004289.
$$

Therefore $z=0.005/0.004289=1.166$ and the two-sided p-value is $0.244$. At a preselected
significance level $\alpha=0.05$, we do not reject $H_0$.

“Not statistically significant” does not mean “no effect.” A 95% confidence interval using the
unpooled standard error is $[-0.34, 1.34]$ percentage points. The data are compatible with a
small loss, no effect, or a useful gain. The experiment is inconclusive at the precision the team
asked for.

The **significance level** $\alpha$ limits the long-run probability of a **Type I error**: rejecting
a true null hypothesis. If thousands of valid A/A tests compare identical variants at
$\alpha=0.05$, about 5% will be significant. This statement concerns the testing procedure over
repetitions, not the truth of one result.

## Statistical power

An experiment can control false positives and still miss real improvements. A **Type II error**
occurs when the test fails to reject $H_0$ even though a specified effect exists. If its
probability is $\beta$, then **statistical power** is

$$
\text{power}=1-\beta
=\Pr(\text{reject }H_0\mid\text{specified effect is real}).
$$

Power is a property of a test design at a particular true effect, not a label attached to the
observed p-value. Teams commonly plan for 80% or 90% power at a **minimum detectable effect**
(MDE): the smallest effect worth reliably detecting. An MDE is a product choice before it becomes
a statistical input.

For two equally sized groups with baseline rate $p_0$, target rate $p_1$, two-sided $\alpha$, and
target power $1-\beta$, the normal approximation gives the per-arm sample size

$$
n \approx
\frac{\left[
z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)}+
z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)+p_1(1-p_1)}
\right]^2}{(p_1-p_0)^2},
\qquad \bar p=\frac{p_0+p_1}{2}.
$$

For $p_0=10\%$, an absolute MDE of one percentage point, $\alpha=5\%$, and 80% power,
$z_{0.975}=1.960$ and $z_{0.8}=0.842$. Substitution gives 14,751 users per arm, or 29,502 total.
This is a planning approximation; very rare events and small samples need exact or simulation-based
calculations.

The curve explains why the worked experiment was inconclusive. With 10,000 users per arm, it had
only 63.6% power to detect a true move from 10% to 11%. Repeating that design when the effect is
exactly one point would fail to reach significance about 36.4% of the time.

| users per arm | power for 10% → 11% |
| ---: | ---: |
| 1,000 | 11.3% |
| 5,000 | 37.1% |
| 10,000 | 63.6% |
| 15,000 | 80.7% |
| 30,000 | 97.9% |

Four levers increase power:

- **More units.** Standard error falls roughly as $1/\sqrt n$, so halving the MDE usually needs
  about four times the traffic.
- **A larger true effect.** Its signal sits farther from the null distribution.
- **Less variance.** Better metrics, stratification, or CUPED can remove predictable noise.
- **A larger $\alpha$.** This makes rejection easier but permits more false positives; it is a
  trade, not free power.

Unequal allocation also costs power for a fixed total sample. A 50/50 split minimizes the variance
when observations cost the same. Teams sometimes begin at 1% treatment to limit blast radius,
then ramp toward 50% after safety metrics look healthy.

### Power is not the positive predictive value

Even 80% power and 5% significance do not imply that 80% or 95% of significant results are true.
That probability also depends on how many tested ideas truly work. If only 10 of 100 ideas have
the planned effect, an illustrative batch expects 8 true positives and $0.05\times90=4.5$ false
positives. Only about $8/(8+4.5)=64\%$ of significant results would be true in that simplified
model. Better hypotheses, lower $\alpha$, replication, and multiple-testing control all matter.

## Different ways to run a test

“A/B testing” names a family of designs and analyses. The right choice depends on whether the goal
is inference, safe rollout, or reward during the experiment.

### Fixed-horizon frequentist A/B

Choose the MDE, $\alpha$, power, sample size, metric, and duration in advance. Analyze once at the
end with a z-test, t-test, bootstrap, or randomization test appropriate to the metric. This is the
default because its error rates are straightforward when the assumptions hold.

Do not check an ordinary p-value each hour and stop at the first value below 0.05. Every extra look
is another chance for noise to cross the boundary, inflating the false-positive rate. Also run for
at least a full business cycle when day-of-week effects matter; reaching the sample size on Friday
does not make a Monday-only sample representative.

### Sequential tests

A **sequential test** allows planned interim looks while preserving an error guarantee. Group
sequential designs spend the Type I error across checkpoints; always-valid p-values and confidence
sequences remain valid under continuous monitoring. Sequential methods can stop early for a clear
win, harm, or futility, but their stopping rule and estimator must match. Ordinary fixed-horizon
thresholds do not become sequential merely because a dashboard updates continuously.

### A/B/n and factorial tests

An **A/B/n test** compares several treatments with one control. It explores more alternatives but
creates more opportunities for a false winner, so use a family-wise procedure such as Holm's method
or control the false discovery rate when that matches the decision problem.

A **factorial design** independently randomizes two or more factors, such as button text and button
colour. A $2\times2$ design estimates both main effects and their interaction. It is more efficient
than isolated tests when factors do not interact, and it reveals when the combination behaves
differently from the sum of its parts.

### Cluster and switchback experiments

User randomization fails when one user's treatment changes another user's outcome. Marketplaces
have shared inventory; social products have networks; delivery algorithms affect a common pool of
drivers. A **cluster-randomized test** assigns whole stores, regions, teams, or network clusters.
A **switchback test** alternates control and treatment for the whole market across time blocks.
Both usually have fewer effective independent units than a user test, so power calculations must
include within-cluster or within-period correlation.

### Bayesian experiments

A Bayesian analysis combines a prior with the likelihood to produce a posterior distribution for
the effect. It can answer questions such as $\Pr(\tau>0\mid\text{data})$ or the probability that
the gain exceeds a business threshold. Those are not p-values. Bayesian designs make assumptions
through the prior and decision rule, and repeated monitoring is safe only relative to a specified
Bayesian stopping and loss framework.

### Multi-armed bandits

A **multi-armed bandit** adapts traffic toward variants that currently appear better, reducing
lost reward during allocation. That is an optimization goal. A balanced randomized test is usually
better for precise causal estimates, guardrail measurement, and learning about segments. Adaptive
allocation also complicates conventional estimators. Use bandits for persistent choices with
rapid feedback; use experiments when learning the treatment effect is the primary goal.

## A/B tests for recommendations and ads

A ranking system does not change one isolated button. It chooses which items receive exposure,
where they appear, and which interactions become tomorrow's training data. An ads system adds an
auction: candidates compete for limited slots, advertisers have budgets, and the platform must
balance user value, advertiser value, and revenue. These feedback loops make randomization more
important, but they also make a naive user-level A/B test easier to misread.

The product question must come before the model metric. For recommendations, a useful question is
“does this ranking policy improve users' experience over the next four weeks?” For ads, it may be
“does this campaign cause purchases that would not otherwise happen?” or “does this auction change
improve total marketplace value?” These are three different **estimands**. They require different
units, metrics, and sometimes different experimental designs.

| system and decision | randomization unit | primary metric | important guardrails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recommendation ranker | User or account | Sessions, satisfied watch time, or retention per eligible user | Latency, hides/reports, diversity, creator or seller concentration |
| Ad creative or advertiser lift | User, cookie, or geography | Incremental conversions, profit, or revenue per assigned unit | Frequency, user complaints, organic conversions |
| Platform ad ranker or auction | User when markets are separable; otherwise geo or time block | Revenue or welfare per request/user | Ad load, relevance, latency, advertiser ROI and spend concentration |

CTR is usually diagnostic, not a complete objective. A click can be caused by a tempting thumbnail,
a high position, or an accidental tap. A recommendation can reduce clicks yet increase completed
plays because users choose more carefully. An ad ranker can raise click-through rate (CTR) while
lowering revenue if it selects cheap ads or while harming advertiser value if clicks do not convert.
Netflix describes offline evaluation as a filter and A/B tests of retention and medium-term
engagement as the basis for product decisions; Google researchers similarly study immediate
behaviour as a surrogate for much sparser long-term revisits.

### Problem 1: the denominator changes

**Interview question.** Treatment increases the number of active users but lowers watch time per
active user. Did the recommender win?

Suppose 10,000 users are assigned to each arm. In control, 6,000 users become active and watch 30
minutes each. In treatment, 6,500 become active and watch 29 minutes each.

| arm | assigned users | active users | minutes / active user | total minutes | minutes / assigned user |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Control | 10,000 | 6,000 | 30 | 180,000 | 18.00 |
| Treatment | 10,000 | 6,500 | 29 | 188,500 | 18.85 |

Among active users, treatment looks 3.3% worse. Per assigned user, it is 4.7% better. Conditioning
on “became active” is dangerous because activity happens after treatment: the treatment changed who
entered that denominator. Use all eligible assigned users for the primary intention-to-treat metric.
Then report activation rate and minutes per active user as decomposition metrics. The correct launch
decision still depends on quality guardrails; 8,500 extra minutes are not a win if they came from
low-quality or harmful consumption.

This example also explains why impression-level tests are often wrong. If assignment is by user,
heavy users create many correlated impressions. Treating one million impressions as one million
independent observations makes the confidence interval too narrow. Aggregate outcomes per user or
use a cluster-robust variance estimator. For intuition, if each user contributes 20 observations and
the within-user correlation is 0.05, the standard **design effect** is

$$
1+(20-1)(0.05)=1.95.
$$

One million rows then contain only about $1{,}000{,}000/1.95=512{,}821$ independent-observation
equivalents under this simplified equal-cluster model.

### Problem 2: position bias and the changing slate

**Interview question.** Treatment's CTR is higher. How do you know its items are more relevant and
not merely shown higher on the page?

A **slate** is the ordered set of items shown in one request. Position is part of a ranking policy:
the first item is examined more often than the tenth. Therefore a whole-policy A/B test answers the
right launch question—whether users do better under the entire treatment slate—but it does *not*
identify an item's intrinsic relevance. “Treatment caused more clicks” and “treatment selected
better items” are different claims.

Solve the launch question by assigning users persistently and comparing policy-level outcomes per
eligible user or request. Keep layout, item-card design, and ad load fixed if the goal is to isolate
the ranker. Log the candidate set, scores, positions, final slate, and exposure. Break the result down
into a metric tree: impressions → examined or viewable items → clicks → qualified consumption or
conversion. If the question is item relevance, collect randomized position data or use a separately
validated examination model; ordinary click logs confound relevance with exposure.

Do not compare click-through rate on the items that each policy happened to show as if the item sets
were fixed. Treatment may explore harder cases, expose more long-tail items, or generate more total
impressions. The end-to-end randomized policy comparison remains causal even when the slates differ;
post-treatment filtering of those slates generally does not.

### Problem 3: the experiment changes its own training data

Recommenders learn from clicks, plays, purchases, and skips generated by the current policy. If both
variants retrain from a shared log, control influences treatment's model and treatment influences
control's model. This violates the “no hidden versions of treatment” part of the stable unit treatment
value assumption (SUTVA). Recent work calls the resulting contamination **symbiosis bias**.

Choose the design that matches the launch decision:

1. **Frozen-model test.** Freeze both candidate models and compare only serving policies. This gives
   a clean short-run answer, but not the steady-state effect after retraining.
2. **Data-diverted test.** Train each model only on feedback from its own arm. This isolates learning,
   but each model receives less data than it would after launch, so model quality and learning speed
   change.
3. **User–corpus co-diversion.** Partition both users and the feedback corpus consistently so each
   policy serves and learns within its own world. This better represents a learning policy, at the
   cost of operational complexity and statistical power.
4. **Cluster randomization.** Assign connected users, creators, sellers, or markets together when
   their behaviour affects one another. Analyze at the cluster level and plan for the loss of
   effective sample size.

A good report labels the target explicitly: “two-week effect with frozen models” is not an estimate
of “fully ramped effect after three months of online learning.” Run long enough to observe novelty
and learning, plot the treatment effect by experiment age, and retain a small long-lived holdout when
the long-term effect matters.

### Problem 4: ad attribution is not incrementality

**Interview question.** An advertiser reports 900 purchases after ad clicks. How many purchases did
the ads cause?

The number cannot be recovered from exposed users alone. Ad delivery targets people who are likely
to buy, so a high conversion rate after exposure mixes selection with causation. Randomly suppress
the advertiser's eligible ads for a control group, while treatment users enter the normal auction.
Compare outcomes by original assignment. A **ghost-ad** design additionally records control auctions
in which the focal ad would have won, creating a more precise “would have been exposed” comparison
without charging for or displaying the focal ad.

Consider 50,000 users per arm over a fixed attribution window:

| arm | purchases | conversion rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Suppressed control | 2,000 | 4.00% |
| Eligible treatment | 2,125 | 4.25% |

The estimated incremental lift is $0.25$ percentage points, or $6.25\%$ relative to control. That
corresponds to $50{,}000\times0.0025=125$ incremental purchases in the treatment population—not the
2,125 purchases that happened after assignment and not the 900 that an attribution rule credited.
The unpooled standard error is 0.126 percentage points, so the approximate 95% confidence interval is
$[0.003, 0.497]$ percentage points and the two-sided p-value is 0.047.

If contribution margin is \$80 per purchase and campaign spend is \$15,000, incremental return on ad
spend is

$$
\operatorname{iROAS}=\frac{125\times\$80}{\$15{,}000}=0.67.
$$

An attribution-based calculation using 900 purchases would report 4.8. The experiment answers the
budget question because it measures what happened *because of* eligibility. The interval is also a
warning: barely crossing 0.05 does not make the economic estimate precise. Power the test for the
smallest profitable lift, and allow for delayed conversions before closing the attribution window.

### Problem 5: auctions and shared inventory create interference

**Interview question.** Why can a user-randomized test of a new ad auction be biased even with a
perfect hash?

Advertiser budgets and pacing couple the two arms. If treatment wins more impressions early, it can
consume a campaign's daily budget and leave fewer bids for control later. Competing ads can change
prices. A seller's or creator's limited inventory can likewise move between recommendation arms.
One unit's treatment has changed another unit's outcome, violating SUTVA.

First identify the interference boundary. If budgets are partitioned by market and users do not
cross markets, randomize geographic clusters. If the marketplace cannot be partitioned, alternate
the whole market between control and treatment in randomized time blocks: a **switchback
experiment**. Blocks must be long enough for stable measurement, and transitions need a washout when
budget pacing, cached rankings, or learned state carries over. Analyze block-level outcomes with
time-of-day and day-of-week controls; the number of requests is not the number of independent units.

For an advertiser-specific lift test, user-level suppression or ghost ads may still be appropriate
because the estimand is the advertiser's incremental effect. For a platform-wide auction mechanism,
the equilibrium response of advertisers may take weeks. A short switchback estimates the immediate
mechanical effect, not the eventual equilibrium after bids and budgets adapt.

### Guardrail metrics: define how the experiment can lose

A **guardrail metric** measures harm that the primary metric may hide. The primary metric answers
“did the change create the intended value?” A guardrail answers “did it remain acceptable on every
dimension we refuse to trade away?” Guardrails are decision constraints, not decorative numbers on
the experiment dashboard.

For example, a recommendation ranker may increase watch time by recommending sensational content.
An ads ranker may increase revenue by adding more ads, selecting poorly matched high bids, or slowing
the page. The primary metric can improve while the product, users, advertisers, or content ecosystem
becomes worse.

#### A guardrail hierarchy for recommendations and ads

Use a small set that covers distinct failure modes:

| guardrail class | recommendation examples | advertising examples | likely action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Correctness and safety | Broken slates, policy-violating content, hide/report rate | Invalid creatives, billing errors, policy violations | Stop or roll back immediately |
| Reliability | Empty-result rate, serving errors, fallback rate | Auction failures, missing ads, billing pipeline errors | Stop ramp; investigate |
| Performance | Ranking latency, page-load time, device resource use | Auction latency, page-load time, timeout rate | Block launch beyond a preset budget |
| User experience | Uninstalls, complaints, satisfaction, retention | Ad hides, complaints, session abandonment, organic engagement | Require non-inferiority or explicit trade-off review |
| Marketplace health | Creator coverage, catalog diversity, seller concentration | Advertiser ROI, reach, spend concentration, small-advertiser participation | Extend test or block if ecosystem harm is material |
| Integrity of measurement | Exposure loss, sample ratio mismatch, event-join rate | Conversion-log loss, auction-eligibility mismatch, billing reconciliation | Invalidate the experiment rather than interpret impact |

Some metrics are **invariants**, not outcome guardrails. Assignment should not change pre-treatment
country, device, or historical activity. A sample ratio mismatch or treatment-dependent logging loss
means the experiment may be untrustworthy; it is not a product trade-off that a large primary win can
cancel.

#### Pick guardrails from the mechanism

Do not copy the same scorecard into every experiment. Start with a causal story about how the change
could cause harm.

- A heavier recommendation model can improve relevance but increase p95 and p99 latency, timeouts,
  fallback ranking, and battery use. Those are its first guardrails.
- A diversity reranker can improve long-term discovery while reducing immediate CTR. CTR is then a
  diagnostic or secondary outcome, not automatically a hard guardrail.
- Higher ad load can increase short-run revenue but reduce organic clicks, session depth, retention,
  and user satisfaction. Those user metrics must constrain the revenue decision.
- A new auction objective can improve platform revenue while reducing advertiser conversions or
  return on ad spend. Measure both advertiser value and the concentration of gains across large and
  small advertisers.
- A new exploration policy can help cold-start items but expose low-quality content. Guard on hides,
  reports, qualified consumption, and per-user exposure caps—not only aggregate clicks.

Metrics should be sensitive enough to react during the experiment and attributable to the change.
Annual retention may represent the right value but cannot protect a two-week ramp by itself. Use a
validated short-term proxy for monitoring and preserve a long-lived holdout for the slower outcome.

#### Predefine the acceptable harm margin

“No statistically significant regression” is a weak guardrail rule. An underpowered metric can have
$p>0.05$ even when substantial harm is plausible. Instead define a **non-inferiority margin**
$\Delta_G$: the largest degradation the business is willing to accept.

For a metric where higher is worse, such as complaint rate, let

$$
d=\widehat p_T-\widehat p_C.
$$

The treatment passes a one-sided 5% non-inferiority check when the upper 95% confidence bound is
below the harm margin:

$$
d+z_{0.95}\operatorname{SE}(d) < \Delta_G,
\qquad z_{0.95}=1.645.
$$

This reverses the usual burden of proof. The question is not “can we prove complaints increased?”
but “can we rule out an unacceptable increase?”

**Worked guardrail.** Suppose control complaints are 0.20%, treatment complaints are 0.22%, and the
maximum acceptable increase is 0.05 percentage points. With 100,000 users per arm, the observed
increase is 0.02 percentage points and its standard error is 0.0205 percentage points. The one-sided
upper bound is

$$
0.020+1.645(0.0205)=0.0537\text{ percentage points}.
$$

Because 0.0537 exceeds the 0.05-point margin, the guardrail does not pass. The result is not proof of
harm; it is insufficient evidence that unacceptable harm is absent. With 200,000 users per arm and
the same observed rates, the smaller standard error gives an upper bound of 0.0438 points, which
passes. Guardrail precision therefore belongs in the power plan.

For a metric where lower is worse, such as advertiser conversion value, use the lower confidence
bound and require it to stay above the negative margin. For latency percentiles and heavy-tailed
metrics, bootstrap or use a distribution-aware method rather than applying a mean-based normal test
blindly.

#### Power the experiment for guardrails too

Sizing only for the primary metric can produce “primary wins, safety unknown.” Calculate sample size
for each hard guardrail's harm margin, then use the largest requirement among the primary metric and
the launch-blocking guardrails. Rare events such as severe reports or billing failures may require
much more traffic than engagement.

Some harms are too rare or serious for an ordinary significance test. Enforce deterministic quality
checks, rate limits, and automated rollback thresholds as well. Zero observed severe events does not
prove zero risk: with no events in $n$ independent observations, the rough 95% “rule of three” upper
bound on the event probability is $3/n$.

#### Many guardrails and repeated monitoring

Ten guardrails tested independently at 5% create many chances to block a good treatment because of
noise. At the same time, a blanket Bonferroni correction can make important safety checks
insensitive. Classify metrics before launch:

1. **Hard guardrails** represent non-negotiable safety, correctness, legal, or reliability limits.
   Give each an explicit threshold and conservative monitoring rule.
2. **Launch guardrails** use prespecified non-inferiority margins. Control the family-wise error rate
   when the decision requires all of them to pass; Holm's procedure is less conservative than plain
   Bonferroni.
3. **Diagnostic metrics** explain the result and generate hypotheses. Do not let a chance movement
   become a retroactive launch criterion.

Continuous safety monitoring is operationally necessary, but ordinary fixed-horizon p-values are not
valid stopping rules. Use alert thresholds designed for repeated looks, confidence sequences, or a
separate safety policy with clearly documented error trade-offs. A rollback for an obvious outage
does not need to wait for statistical significance.

#### Make the launch rule explicit

A useful pre-registered rule looks like this:

| primary metric | hard guardrails | launch guardrails | decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wins or is practically positive | Pass | Pass | Launch or ramp |
| Wins | Any fail | Any result | Stop or roll back |
| Wins | Pass | Inconclusive | Continue to planned sample; do not silently waive |
| Neutral | Pass | Pass | Usually do not launch unless cost, simplicity, or strategic value justifies it |
| Loses | Pass | Pass | Do not launch merely because it is safe |

Segment checks matter even when the aggregate passes. Inspect guardrails across device capability,
country, new versus established users, and marketplace participant size when there is a plausible
mechanism for heterogeneous harm. Prespecify critical segments and their margins; searching hundreds
of slices after the test will manufacture apparent regressions.

Finally, report effect sizes and confidence intervals for every guardrail. “Green” should mean the
data rule out the predefined unacceptable harm—not merely that a two-sided p-value stayed above
0.05.

### Holdout experiments

A **holdout** is a randomly selected population that deliberately does not receive a change. The
word is used for several designs that answer different questions:

| holdout | treatment population | control population | question answered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Experiment control | Candidate ranker or ad policy | Current production policy | Should this one change launch? |
| Campaign suppression | Eligible for the advertiser's campaign | Focal advertiser suppressed | What conversions did this campaign cause? |
| Long-lived holdout | All launches in a product area | Frozen earlier experience | What is the cumulative, persistent effect after novelty fades? |
| Global holdout | Stack of many eligible launches | Baseline stack without those launches | What is the combined effect of the program? |
| Model-training holdout | Served and learned from treatment data | Served and learned from control data | What is the effect including feedback and retraining? |

The first row is an ordinary A/B test. The others exist because summing individual experiment lifts
usually does not recover the total product impact. Effects can overlap, interact, decay, or displace
one another. A long-lived or global holdout measures the bundle directly.

#### Designing a long-lived holdout

Suppose a recommendation organization launches dozens of ranking changes each quarter. Reserve,
for example, 2% of eligible users before those experiments begin. Keep their assignment sticky and
serve them the agreed baseline stack. The remaining 98% receives normal launches and continues to
participate in short-term experiments. Compare the two populations on retention, satisfied usage,
complaints, content diversity, and creator outcomes after 30, 60, and 90 days.

Several details determine what this estimate means:

- **Freeze the policy definition, not necessarily every binary.** Security fixes and correctness
  changes may need to reach both arms. Version the set of behavioural features withheld from the
  holdout so the contrast is reconstructable.
- **Keep eligibility symmetric.** Decide eligibility before treatment. Removing unhappy holdout
  users or adding only newly active treatment users breaks randomization.
- **Prevent leakage.** Account-level assignment is preferable when people use several devices.
  Shared households, social connections, caches, and cross-device identifiers can still contaminate
  the contrast.
- **Separate concurrent layers.** A global holdout for recommendations should not accidentally
  suppress unrelated payment or safety changes. Put the holdout at the correct product boundary.
- **Monitor the holdout itself.** Check sample ratio mismatch, invariant attributes, assignment loss,
  and metric logging over time. Attrition caused by treatment is an outcome, not a reason to drop a
  user from analysis.
- **Budget the opportunity cost.** A persistent control withholds improvements. Use the smallest arm
  that achieves useful precision, rotate only at planned boundaries, and apply non-negotiable safety
  fixes to everyone.

A rolling holdout is not equivalent to a persistent one. Re-randomizing 2% each week measures a
short exposure to the baseline and mixes users with different histories. A persistent holdout
measures cumulative treatment, but its baseline grows older. Teams often use both: short-lived
controls for individual launch decisions and a small persistent holdout for cumulative impact.

#### Holdouts for advertising

For advertiser lift, assign eligible users before the auction. In the treatment arm, let the focal
campaign compete normally. In the holdout arm, suppress only that campaign and allow other ads or
organic content to fill the slot. This measures incremental conversions against the realistic
alternative, rather than against an empty ad slot.

Three denominators must be logged separately:

1. **Eligible users:** could have entered the campaign experiment.
2. **Reached users:** actually received the advertiser's ad in treatment.
3. **Converting users:** completed the outcome inside the prespecified window.

The intention-to-treat effect divides by eligible assigned users. Dividing by reached treatment
users but by all control users is not randomized. Ghost ads can identify comparable control auction
opportunities, but their auction prediction and logging need validation through A/A-style checks.

Campaign holdouts can also interfere through budgets. Suppressing ads for control users may redirect
the saved budget toward treatment users, increasing their frequency. That total policy effect may be
exactly what the advertiser wants, but it is not the effect of one extra impression. Log spend,
frequency, reach, and auction eligibility; state whether the estimand holds budget fixed or lets the
campaign reallocate it.

### Define the MDE before sample size

The **minimum detectable effect** (MDE) is the smallest true change for which the chosen test has the
target probability of rejecting the null. It is not the smallest effect the metric can display, the
observed lift, or a guarantee that every significant result is worth shipping.

Choose the MDE from a decision threshold:

1. Translate the metric into product value. How much is one additional retained user, qualified
   hour, incremental purchase, or dollar of advertiser value worth?
2. Subtract implementation, inference, latency, support, and opportunity costs. Include downside on
   other sides of the marketplace.
3. Find the smallest effect whose expected value justifies launching. This is the practical MDE.
4. Check whether available traffic can measure it in a useful time. If not, improve the metric or
   design, accept a larger MDE, or admit that the experiment cannot answer the question precisely.

Always specify whether an MDE is **absolute** or **relative**. Moving conversion from 2.00% to 2.10%
is an absolute increase of 0.10 percentage points but a relative lift of 5%. Saying “a 5% MDE”
without the baseline is incomplete.

Statistical significance and practical significance form four different decisions:

| statistical result | business magnitude | interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Significant | Larger than practical threshold | Evidence supports shipping, subject to guardrails |
| Significant | Smaller than practical threshold | Precisely measured but probably not worth shipping |
| Not significant | Interval excludes valuable effects | Evidence supports stopping or accepting equivalence |
| Not significant | Interval includes valuable effects | Inconclusive; do not call it “no effect” |

For the third row, teams can predefine an equivalence or non-inferiority margin instead of trying to
infer “no harm” from $p>0.05$.

### Sample size for a recommendation metric

**Interview question.** Baseline watch time is 20 minutes per eligible user per day with a standard
deviation of 40 minutes. How many users are needed per arm to detect a one-minute increase with 80%
power and a two-sided 5% significance level?

For a difference in means with equal allocation, a planning approximation is

$$
n_{\text{per arm}} \approx
\frac{2\sigma^2(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2}{\delta^2},
$$

where $\sigma$ is the user-level standard deviation and $\delta$ is the absolute MDE. Here,
$\sigma=40$, $\delta=1$, $z_{0.975}=1.960$, and $z_{0.8}=0.842$:

$$
n \approx
\frac{2(40)^2(1.960+0.842)^2}{1^2}
=25{,}116.4.
$$

Round up to 25,117 users per arm, or 50,234 total. The one-minute MDE is a 5% relative increase over
the 20-minute baseline. The formula assumes one independent user-level outcome, stable variance,
equal allocation, and a normal approximation. Watch time is often zero-inflated and heavy-tailed, so
estimate variance from a representative pre-period and validate power with a bootstrap or simulation.

Traffic determines duration only after sample size. If 10,000 *new eligible, assignable* users enter
per day and allocation is 50/50, each arm receives about 5,000. Enrollment takes
$25{,}117/5{,}000=5.02$ days. The experiment should still span complete weekly cycles and allow the
outcome window to mature, so a planned 14-day run may be more defensible than stopping on day six.

#### Variance reduction and repeated observations

If pre-experiment watch time explains 36% of outcome variance, a correctly implemented CUPED-style
covariate can reduce variance by a factor of approximately $1-R^2=0.64$. Under the same assumptions,
the required sample becomes

$$
25{,}117\times0.64=16{,}075\text{ users per arm}.
$$

Use only covariates measured before assignment and apply the same transformation to both arms.
Estimate the expected reduction from historical or A/A data rather than assuming it. CUPED improves
precision; it does not repair selection bias, interference, or incorrect logging.

Repeated daily observations do not automatically multiply sample size. A user who contributes 14
days of watch time still represents one randomized unit. Either aggregate the 14-day outcome per user
or use a repeated-measures model that accounts for within-user correlation.

### Sample size for a rare ad conversion

**Interview question.** Purchase conversion is 2%. The smallest profitable effect is a 5% relative
lift. How much traffic is required for 80% power with two-sided $\alpha=0.05$?

The treatment target is

$$
p_1=0.02(1+0.05)=0.021,
$$

so the absolute MDE is only $0.001$, or 0.10 percentage points. Substituting $p_0=0.020$ and
$p_1=0.021$ into the two-proportion formula from the power section gives 315,206 users per arm, or
630,412 total.

Rare outcomes are expensive because most assigned users contribute zeros. Doubling the relative MDE
to 10% changes the target to 2.20% and reduces the requirement to 80,682 users per arm—not half as
many, but roughly one quarter, because sample size scales approximately with $1/\delta^2$.

Do not replace purchase conversion with CTR merely to obtain power unless CTR is a validated
surrogate for incremental profit. Better options include running longer, enlarging the eligible
population, using pre-treatment covariates, or pooling evidence through a prespecified model. For
very rare conversions, use simulation or exact methods and model delayed conversion maturity,
identity loss, and over-dispersion rather than trusting the normal approximation blindly.

### Adjust sample size for the real design

The textbook calculation is a starting point. Adjust it before reserving traffic:

- **Unequal allocation.** A small treatment arm protects users but increases variance. For a fixed
  total sample, 50/50 is most efficient when arms have similar cost and variance.
- **Clustering.** With equal cluster size $m$ and intracluster correlation $\rho$, multiply by the
  design effect $1+(m-1)\rho$. If clusters contain 100 users and $\rho=0.02$, the factor is 2.98;
  the recommendation example grows from 25,117 to about 74,849 users per arm.
- **Attrition and eligibility.** Inflate for expected loss only when it is assignment-independent.
  Treatment-caused inactivity is an outcome and must not be removed.
- **Multiple treatments.** Power each planned contrast and control the relevant family of tests.
  Reusing one control can save traffic, but correlated comparisons still require a valid correction.
- **Sequential monitoring.** Use group-sequential boundaries or always-valid methods and calculate
  power for that design. A fixed-horizon sample calculation does not authorize stopping whenever
  $p<0.05$.
- **Triggered experiments.** Base duration on the trigger rate, not total site traffic. Define the
  trigger upstream of treatment and retain assignment for everyone who qualifies.
- **Finite clusters or markets.** Ten million requests across eight geographic markets do not create
  ten million independent units. Power may be limited by eight clusters; use randomization inference
  or a longer switchback with enough randomized blocks.

Before launch, save the assumptions next to the experiment configuration: baseline mean or rate,
variance, absolute and relative MDE, $\alpha$, target power, allocation, eligible traffic per day,
trigger rate, outcome delay, planned duration, and any variance-reduction factor. After the test,
report confidence intervals and compare the observed control variance with the planning value.

### A practical experiment plan

For a new recommendation or ads ranker, a defensible sequence is:

1. Validate logging and randomization with an A/A test. Check sample ratio mismatch overall and by
   eligibility, device, country, and experiment day.
2. Run offline replay only as a gate. Historical logs were generated by the old policy, so offline
   NDCG, AUC, or revenue replay cannot establish the online causal effect of a new policy.
3. Start with a small treatment ramp for safety, then reach the allocation used in the power plan.
   Do not treat the ramp's repeated peeks as a fixed-horizon significance test.
4. Keep assignment sticky at the user or account level. Log assignment separately from actual
   exposure and analyze intention to treat.
5. Predeclare one primary metric, guardrails, an MDE, duration, and a delayed-outcome window. Add
   advertiser-, creator-, and content-side metrics for a multi-sided marketplace.
6. Diagnose the metric tree and segments, but do not promote whichever slice happens to be
   significant. Correct confirmatory families for multiple testing and replicate surprising effects.
7. Measure novelty, carryover, and learning dynamics. If the product changes future training data,
   state whether models were frozen or data was isolated.

#### More common interview questions

**Offline NDCG rose 5%, but online engagement fell. Why?** The logged test set reflects the old
policy and observes feedback only for exposed items. The offline metric may weight positions or
labels differently from the product objective; the new model may also add latency, reduce diversity,
or exploit a biased click label. Verify the serving path, latency and candidate coverage, then use
the randomized online result for the launch decision.

**CTR rose 3%, but revenue fell 2%. Ship?** Not from those facts. Decompose revenue into eligible
requests, ad load, click probability, conversion or advertiser value, price, and fill. Check the
predeclared primary metric and guardrails. A CTR-optimized ranker may prefer low-price ads. If revenue
is primary and the loss is precise and practically meaningful, do not ship merely because CTR won.

**Clicks arrive immediately but purchases take seven days. When do you read the result?** Assign by
event time, but mature each user's outcome for the full seven-day window. Either stop enrollment and
wait seven days or use a prespecified delayed-outcome method. Comparing mature control users with
immature treatment users creates a time-window bias.

**Can we analyze only users who saw a recommendation or ad?** Only when exposure is a valid
pre-treatment trigger that both variants reach under identical logic. “Saw the treatment card” or
“treatment won the auction” is post-treatment selection. Use intention to treat; use a ghost-ad or
other purpose-built design when the desired estimand is effect among those who would be exposed.

**How long should the test run?** Long enough to reach the planned sample size, cover full product
cycles, and mature delayed outcomes—while respecting the prespecified stopping rule. Recommendation
tests may need additional time for novelty to fade and feedback loops to develop. “One week” is not
a universal answer.

## Control, experiments, layers, and buckets

At platform scale, assignment must be stable, reproducible, and able to support concurrent tests.
The useful hierarchy is:

- A **variant** is one experience, usually control or treatment.
- An **experiment** owns a population slice and divides it among variants.
- A **layer** is a namespace of mutually exclusive experiments. One unit can enter at most one
  experiment in a layer.
- A **bucket** is a small allocation slot. An experiment reserves a set of buckets and maps those
  buckets to variants.

<ExperimentLayers users={12} buckets={16} />

A service can compute

$$
b=\operatorname{hash}(\text{unit ID},\text{layer ID},\text{salt})\bmod B,
$$

where $B$ is the bucket count. The hash must be deterministic and close to uniform. Adding the
layer ID re-randomizes the same user independently in each layer. Adding an experiment-specific
salt can independently split the users reserved for that experiment into variants.

Suppose a layer has 10,000 buckets. Checkout experiment E1 reserves buckets 0–999: 0–499 are
control and 500–999 treatment, so it receives 10% of eligible traffic in a 50/50 split. Search
experiment E2 can reserve 1,000–2,999 in the same layer without overlap. A ranking experiment in a
different layer may use all 10,000 buckets, allowing E1 and the ranking test to run concurrently.

Layers increase throughput, but only when cross-layer interactions are unlikely. A button-colour
test and a ranking-model test may safely overlap. Two tests that both change checkout should share
a layer and be mutually exclusive, or use an explicit factorial design. Hidden interactions make
each experiment's effect depend on the other experiment's assignment.

Do not bucket by `hash(user_id) % 100` and then change the modulus or boundaries casually. That can
reshuffle existing users between variants and produce carryover. Production systems use stable
hashes, many fine-grained buckets, versioned configuration, eligibility filters applied consistently,
and logged assignment exposure so the analysis can reconstruct what a user actually received.

### Triggering and exposure

Assignment is not the same as exposure. A user may be assigned to the checkout experiment but never
open checkout. **Triggered analysis** includes a unit once it reaches the point where treatment
could affect it, improving sensitivity by excluding unaffected traffic. The trigger must be defined
before assignment can influence it; filtering on a post-treatment action can bias the groups.

Analyze by the original assignment—**intention to treat**—when delivery is imperfect. Comparing
only users who successfully received treatment can destroy randomization because delivery failure
may correlate with device, geography, or outcome.

## Trust checks before reading impact

A polished p-value cannot repair a broken experiment. Check trustworthiness first.

1. **Run an A/A test.** Send both arms the same experience. Significant metrics should occur at
   their advertised rate, and confidence intervals should have their advertised coverage.
2. **Check sample ratio mismatch (SRM).** If a 50/50 experiment has a highly implausible assignment
   count, investigate missing logs, eligibility differences, bad hashing, or crashes before reading
   treatment effects.
3. **Check invariant metrics.** Assignment should not change pre-experiment attributes such as
   historical activity or country mix.
4. **Verify exposure and telemetry.** The variant served, assignment ID, randomization unit, trigger,
   and metric events must join without treatment-dependent loss.
5. **Inspect novelty and carryover.** Users may explore a new interface briefly, while cached state
   or learned behaviour can survive reassignment.
6. **Treat guardrails as decisions.** A primary-metric win does not excuse a material regression in
   latency, errors, safety, or long-term retention.

Kohavi, Tang, and Xu emphasize this distinction: calculating numbers is easy; obtaining numbers
that deserve trust requires engineering and diagnostic checks around the statistical test.

## Summary

- Random assignment makes control and treatment comparable in expectation, turning a product
  comparison into an estimate of causal effect.
- A p-value asks how surprising the observed statistic would be under the null; it is not the
  probability that the null is true or a measure of effect size.
- Power is the chance that a design detects a specified real effect. Choose the MDE, $\alpha$,
  target power, and sample size before reading the data.
- Fixed-horizon, sequential, factorial, cluster, Bayesian, and bandit methods answer different
  operational questions; their stopping and analysis rules are not interchangeable.
- Recommendation and ads experiments must account for position bias, post-treatment denominators,
  delayed outcomes, learning from shared data, auction interference, and multi-sided guardrails.
- Guardrails define unacceptable harm. Give hard and launch guardrails explicit margins, power the
  experiment to rule those margins out, and treat broken randomization or telemetry as invalidation.
- MDE is a product decision expressed as an absolute effect. Sample size then follows from the MDE,
  baseline variance, error rates, allocation, clustering, and the experiment's stopping rule.
- Short-term controls answer individual launch questions; persistent and global holdouts measure
  cumulative effects, decay, interactions, and feedback over time.
- Stable hashing assigns units to buckets. Mutually exclusive experiments share a layer, while
  separate layers let compatible experiments reuse traffic.
- Validate assignment, exposure, metric logging, and SRM before interpreting any impact estimate.
