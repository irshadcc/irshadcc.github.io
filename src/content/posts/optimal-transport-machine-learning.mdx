## Background

Machine learning often asks whether two collections of points are similar. A generator produces
images that should resemble a dataset. A model trained on studio photographs must work on webcam
images. A clustering algorithm compares two histograms. Ordinary point-by-point error is not
enough, because the two collections do not come with matching pairs.

**Optimal transport** (OT) supplies the missing matching. It treats each probability distribution
as a pile of mass, considers every legal way to move one pile into the other, and chooses the
cheapest. This matters because its distance uses the geometry of the sample space: moving a point
by one unit costs less than moving it by one hundred.

This post develops the mathematics behind three influential machine-learning papers: Cuturi's
entropic regularization and Sinkhorn algorithm, Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou's Wasserstein GAN,
and Courty and collaborators' use of transport for domain adaptation. We will build the primal
problem from probability, derive its dual intuition, compute a complete two-by-two example, and
then connect the same objects to each application.

## Probability foundations

Optimal transport compares probability distributions, so we first need to be precise about what
those words mean. The main objects are simpler than their notation suggests.

### Outcomes, random variables, and distributions

A **sample space** $\Omega$ is the set of possible outcomes of an experiment. For a coin toss,
$\Omega$ could be `heads, tails`. For an image generator, an outcome might include every random
number used to create one image.

A **random variable** is a function that turns an outcome into a value we care about:

$$
X:\Omega\to\mathcal X.
$$

The space $\mathcal X$ is the set of possible values. It could be the real line, a list of class
labels, or the space of all $256\times256$ RGB images. The randomness is in the sampled outcome;
once the outcome is fixed, $X$ is an ordinary deterministic function.

The **probability distribution** of $X$ tells us how likely its possible values are. For a
discrete variable, the probability mass function is

$$
p_X(x)=\Pr(X=x),
\qquad
p_X(x)\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_x p_X(x)=1.
$$

For example, let $X$ be the number of defective parts in a small batch, with

| $x$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $p_X(x)$ | $0.50$ | $0.30$ | $0.20$ |

The probability of at least one defect is $0.30+0.20=0.50$. The three probabilities behave like
three piles of mass with total mass one. This is exactly the picture that optimal transport uses.

A continuous variable usually has a **probability density** $p_X(x)$. Probability is the area
under the density, not the height at one point:

$$
\Pr(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b p_X(x)\,dx,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)\,dx=1.
$$

The broader term **probability measure**, often written $\mu$, covers discrete masses,
continuous densities, and distributions that mix both. Writing $X\sim\mu$ means “sample $X$
according to distribution $\mu$.” This measure notation is useful in OT because a dataset or a
generator need not have a convenient density.

### Expectations are weighted averages

The **expectation** of a function $f(X)$ is its probability-weighted average. In the discrete and
continuous cases respectively,

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)]
=\sum_x f(x)p_X(x),
\qquad
\mathbb E[f(X)]
=\int f(x)p_X(x)\,dx.
$$

For the defect example, the expected number of defects is

$$
\mathbb E[X]
=0(0.50)+1(0.30)+2(0.20)
=0.70.
$$

This does not predict $0.70$ defects in one batch. It says the average approaches $0.70$ over
many independent batches. Later, transport cost will also be an expectation: if $(X,Y)$ is a
random source--target pair, then a plan pays $\mathbb E[c(X,Y)]$.

### From a dataset to a distribution

Machine learning starts with samples $x_1,\ldots,x_N$, not the unknown population distribution.
The **empirical distribution** puts equal probability $1/N$ on each observation:

$$
\widehat\mu_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\delta_{x_i}.
$$

The symbol $\delta_{x_i}$ is a **point mass**: all its probability sits at $x_i$. If the data are
$(1,1,4,6)$, the empirical distribution puts mass $0.50$ at 1, $0.25$ at 4, and $0.25$ at 6.
Repeated observations combine at the same location.

An empirical expectation is therefore the familiar sample average:

$$
\mathbb E_{X\sim\widehat\mu_N}[f(X)]
=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i).
$$

This connection is important. When OT compares two minibatches, it usually compares two empirical
distributions. The abstract measure notation eventually becomes a finite matrix problem.

### Joint distributions and marginals

A **joint distribution** describes two variables together. For discrete $X$ and $Y$, write
$P_{ij}=\Pr(X=x_i,Y=y_j)$. Summing a joint distribution over one variable recovers the other
variable's **marginal distribution**:

$$
\Pr(X=x_i)=\sum_jP_{ij},
\qquad
\Pr(Y=y_j)=\sum_iP_{ij}.
$$

Knowing the marginals does not determine the joint distribution. If $X$ has probabilities
$a=(0.50,0.50)$ and $Y$ has $b=(0.25,0.75)$, one possible joint distribution assumes
independence:

$$
P_{\mathrm{independent}}=ab^\mathsf T
=\begin{bmatrix}
0.125&0.375\\
0.125&0.375
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its row sums are $(0.50,0.50)$ and its column sums are $(0.25,0.75)$. But many other matrices
have the same sums. A coupling is precisely one of these possible joint distributions. Optimal
transport searches over them and selects one using geometry and cost.

## From distributions to transport plans

Let a source distribution put masses $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ at points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, and let a
target distribution put masses $b_1,\ldots,b_m$ at $y_1,\ldots,y_m$. The masses are non-negative
and each list sums to one.

A **coupling**, or **transport plan**, is a non-negative matrix $P\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$.
Its entry $P_{ij}$ says how much mass travels from $x_i$ to $y_j$. A legal plan must send all
source mass and deliver exactly the requested target mass:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{ij}=a_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{ij}=b_j.
$$

In matrix notation these constraints are $P\mathbf 1_m=a$ and
$P^\mathsf T\mathbf 1_n=b$. The set of all such plans is written $U(a,b)$. A coupling is a
joint probability distribution whose marginals are $a$ and $b$: it creates a relationship
between two distributions without changing either one.

Now choose a **ground cost** $C_{ij}=c(x_i,y_j)$. For points on a line we might use
$C_{ij}=|x_i-y_j|$. The discrete Kantorovich problem is the linear program

$$
\operatorname{OT}(a,b)
=\min_{P\in U(a,b)}\langle P,C\rangle
=\min_{P\in U(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}C_{ij}.
$$

The inner product simply adds “mass times travel cost” over every route. The optimization is
global. Sending one source to its nearest target greedily can consume capacity that another
source needs more.

### A complete two-by-two example

Put source masses $a=(0.50,0.50)$ at positions $(0,3)$ and target masses
$b=(0.25,0.75)$ at positions $(1,4)$. With distance as the cost,

$$
C=
\begin{bmatrix}
|0-1| & |0-4|\\
|3-1| & |3-4|
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&4\\2&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Every legal plan is determined by one number, $x=P_{11}$:

$$
P(x)=
\begin{bmatrix}
x&0.50-x\\
0.25-x&0.25+x
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 0\le x\le0.25.
$$

The interval comes from requiring all four entries to be non-negative. Substitution turns the
matrix optimization into a line:

$$
\langle P(x),C\rangle
=x+4(0.50-x)+2(0.25-x)+(0.25+x)
=2.75-4x.
$$

So the minimum occurs at $x=0.25$, with cost $1.75$. Check one route: the lower-right entry is
$P_{22}=0.25+0.25=0.50$, and it contributes $0.50\times1=0.50$ to the cost. Move the slider to
see why increasing $x$ replaces expensive crossed routes with short ones.

## Wasserstein distance

When the ground cost is a metric raised to power $p$, the **$p$-Wasserstein distance** is

$$
W_p(\mu,\nu)
=\left(
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}
\int \|x-y\|^p\,d\pi(x,y)
\right)^{1/p}.
$$

Here $\mu$ and $\nu$ may be continuous distributions, $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is their set of couplings,
and $\pi$ plays the role of the matrix $P$. The integral is the continuous version of the sum.
For $p=1$, it is the least average distance that mass must travel.

This is not the same as comparing probability values at fixed locations. Consider two point
masses: $\mu=\delta_0$ at zero and $\nu_\theta=\delta_\theta$ at $\theta$. For every non-zero
$\theta$, their supports do not overlap.

| discrepancy | value when $\theta\ne0$ | as $\theta\to0$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu\|\nu_\theta)$ | $+\infty$ | stays $+\infty$ |
| Jensen--Shannon divergence | $\log 2$ | stays $\log 2$ |
| $W_1(\mu,\nu_\theta)$ | $|\theta|$ | tends to $0$ |

The Wasserstein value changes smoothly because it sees that the locations are getting closer.
This weak notion of convergence is one reason OT is useful when data occupy thin, low-dimensional
sets inside a high-dimensional space. It does not mean Wasserstein distance is always statistically
or computationally better; estimating it accurately becomes difficult in high dimension, a form
of the curse of dimensionality.

## Why a dual problem appears

The transport plan is the **primal** view: explicitly decide where every unit of mass goes. The
**dual** view assigns potentials to locations. For discrete OT, linear-programming duality gives

$$
\max_{f\in\mathbb R^n,\,g\in\mathbb R^m}
\left(
\sum_i a_i f_i+\sum_j b_j g_j
\right)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
f_i+g_j\le C_{ij}.
$$

Why this constraint? Think of $f_i+g_j$ as a quoted price for route $(i,j)$. It cannot exceed
the real cost $C_{ij}$. Therefore every feasible quote is a lower bound on every transport
plan. At the optimum, strong duality says the best lower bound equals the cheapest plan.

For $W_1$ with distance as the cost, the Kantorovich--Rubinstein theorem compresses the two
potentials into one function:

$$
W_1(\mu,\nu)
=\sup_{\|f\|_L\le1}
\left(
\mathbb E_{X\sim\mu}[f(X)]
-\mathbb E_{Y\sim\nu}[f(Y)]
\right).
$$

A function is **1-Lipschitz** when $|f(x)-f(y)|\le\|x-y\|$: its output cannot change faster
than its input. The maximizing function, called a Kantorovich potential, gives high scores where
source mass dominates and low scores where target mass dominates, but the Lipschitz constraint
limits how sharply those scores can change.<Sidenote>The theorem requires regularity conditions,
including finite first moments. The displayed form is the one used in the WGAN argument.</Sidenote>

For the two point masses, choose $f(x)=-x$ when comparing $\delta_0$ against a positive
$\delta_\theta$. It is 1-Lipschitz, and
$f(0)-f(\theta)=0-(-\theta)=\theta$. The dual therefore recovers the primal cost of moving one
unit of mass by distance $\theta$.

## Entropy makes transport computable

Exact discrete OT is a linear program. Marco Cuturi's 2013 paper proposed adding negative
entropy to obtain a problem that is smooth and much faster to solve:

$$
P_\varepsilon
=\arg\min_{P\in U(a,b)}
\left[
\langle P,C\rangle
+\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(\log P_{ij}-1)
\right].
$$

The parameter $\varepsilon>0$ controls the trade-off. Small $\varepsilon$ stays close to exact
OT but is harder to compute. Large $\varepsilon$ rewards diffuse plans more strongly, making the
solution smoother but more biased toward spreading mass across many routes.

The optimizer has the special form

$$
P_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v),
\qquad K_{ij}=e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}.
$$

The **Gibbs kernel** $K$ gives cheap routes large initial weights and expensive routes small
weights. The vectors $u$ and $v$ then scale rows and columns until the required marginals are met.
The Sinkhorn updates are

$$
u\leftarrow a\oslash(Kv),
\qquad
v\leftarrow b\oslash(K^\mathsf Tu),
$$

where $\oslash$ means element-wise division. Each $u$ update fixes the row sums; each $v$ update
fixes the column sums. Fixing one side perturbs the other, so the algorithm alternates.

In the lower half of the explorer, iteration 1 has row sums $(0.241,0.759)$ but exact column
sums $(0.250,0.750)$. After eight iterations the row sums are approximately $(0.500,0.500)$ as
well. The resulting plan is approximately

$$
P_\varepsilon=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.242&0.258\\
0.008&0.492
\end{bmatrix}
\quad(\varepsilon=1).
$$

Its unregularized travel cost is about $1.783$, slightly above the exact $1.750$, because the
entropy term keeps a little mass on expensive routes. In real implementations, log-domain
updates are important when $C/\varepsilon$ is large; direct exponentiation can underflow.

## Paper 1: Wasserstein GANs

A **generative adversarial network** (GAN) trains a generator distribution $P_\theta$ against a
learned discriminator. Arjovsky, Chintala, and Bottou proposed minimizing $W_1(P_r,P_\theta)$,
where $P_r$ is the data distribution. Directly optimizing over all couplings of real and generated
samples would be impractical, so WGAN uses the dual:

$$
\max_{\|f_w\|_L\le1}
\mathbb E_{x\sim P_r}[f_w(x)]
-\mathbb E_{z\sim p(z)}[f_w(g_\theta(z))].
$$

The neural network $f_w$ is called a **critic**, because it outputs a real-valued score rather
than a probability of “real.” Training alternates between improving the critic and moving generated
samples in the direction that raises their critic score.

The point-mass example explains the hoped-for benefit. A divergence based only on overlapping
density can be flat or infinite while generated and real supports are disjoint. $W_1$ still reports
their distance, so it can supply a useful learning signal as the generator moves.

The theorem requires a 1-Lipschitz critic. The original paper enforced a compact parameter space
by clipping critic weights, an indirect and restrictive approximation. Later work introduced
gradient penalties, but those are a different paper and not part of the original WGAN result.
The important mathematical move is the duality: it replaces an impossible search over transport
plans with a learned scalar function under a slope constraint.

## Paper 2: Domain adaptation

In **unsupervised domain adaptation**, labeled source examples are available, but the target
examples have no labels. Imagine handwritten digits photographed by two cameras. Their pixel
distributions shift even though their semantic classes should remain the same.

Courty and collaborators proposed learning a coupling between empirical source and target
samples. A transported source point is the barycenter of the targets to which its row of mass is
sent:

$$
\widehat x_i
=\frac{1}{a_i}\sum_j P_{ij}y_j.
$$

For example, if a source point of mass $0.5$ sends $0.2$ to $y_1=1$ and $0.3$ to $y_2=4$, its
transported location is

$$
\widehat x
=\frac{0.2(1)+0.3(4)}{0.5}
=2.8.
$$

The classifier can then train on transported source features while retaining source labels. The
paper also adds regularization that encourages source samples with the same label to move together.
That extra structure matters: geometry alone can align the wrong classes when two domains contain
symmetries or different class proportions.

This exposes a general limitation of OT in machine learning. A cost matrix encodes what “similar”
means. Euclidean distance in raw pixels may reward the wrong match. Useful transport therefore
depends on a meaningful representation, a suitable ground cost, and sometimes label or structural
regularization.

## What the three papers teach together

Cuturi's paper addresses computation: entropy changes a linear program into matrix scaling.
The WGAN paper uses duality: a critic estimates transport discrepancy without constructing a
sample-to-sample plan. The domain-adaptation paper uses the primal plan itself: its entries become
soft correspondences that move labeled examples.

| question | mathematical object | machine-learning role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which points correspond? | primal coupling $P$ | align source and target samples |
| How different are two distributions? | Wasserstein value | training objective or metric |
| Can we avoid building the coupling? | dual potential $f$ | neural critic in WGAN |
| Can we solve the discrete problem quickly? | entropic plan $P_\varepsilon$ | differentiable, scalable approximation |

These are not interchangeable tools. Use a primal plan when the correspondence is useful. Use a
dual model when samples are plentiful but a full cost matrix is too large. Use entropic OT when a
dense, smooth approximation and fast matrix operations are acceptable.

## Summary

- A dataset defines an empirical probability distribution, and a coupling is a joint distribution
  with fixed marginals; optimal transport chooses the coupling with the lowest expected cost.
- Wasserstein distance respects the geometry between samples, so nearby disjoint distributions
  can be close even when density-based divergences are not.
- Kantorovich duality replaces transport routes with constrained potentials; for $W_1$, the
  potential is 1-Lipschitz.
- Entropic regularization gives the factorization $P=\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)$,
  and Sinkhorn iteration finds the scaling vectors by alternating marginal corrections.
- WGAN uses the dual potential as a critic, while domain adaptation uses the primal coupling as a
  soft correspondence between datasets.
