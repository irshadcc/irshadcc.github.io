## Background

For a function of one variable, the second derivative answers a familiar question: does the
graph bend upward or downward? A function of many variables can bend differently in every
direction. One second derivative is no longer enough.

The **Hessian matrix** collects all second partial derivatives of a scalar-valued function. It
matters because the same matrix classifies stationary points, detects convexity, measures how
sensitive a gradient is, and chooses the scale and direction of a Newton optimization step. In
machine learning, it also explains why some parameter directions are stiff while others are
nearly flat.

This post builds the Hessian from directional derivatives, proves the main curvature tests, and
works through a quadratic example with real numbers. We then prove its link to convexity, derive
Newton's method, and finish with the practical reason large models usually use Hessian--vector
products rather than constructing the whole matrix. For a shape-first introduction to gradients,
Jacobians, and automatic differentiation, see [Matrix Calculus](/blog/matrix-calculus/).

## From a second derivative to a matrix

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ have continuous second partial derivatives. Its **gradient**
$\nabla f(x)$ is the vector of first partial derivatives. The Hessian is the Jacobian of that
gradient:

$$
H_f(x)=\nabla^2 f(x)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1\partial x_n}\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For $f(x,y)=x^2+xy+3y^2$, differentiate once:

$$
\nabla f(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}2x+y\\x+6y\end{bmatrix},
$$

and differentiate each gradient entry again:

$$
H_f(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&6\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The diagonal entries measure bending along the coordinate axes. The off-diagonal entries are
**mixed partial derivatives**: they measure how the slope in one coordinate changes as another
coordinate moves. The $xy$ term above creates both off-diagonal ones.

### Why the Hessian is symmetric

If the mixed second partial derivatives are continuous near $x$, Clairaut's theorem gives

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}
=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}.
$$

Therefore $H_{ij}=H_{ji}$, so $H=H^T$. Continuity is a sufficient condition, not decoration:
without suitable regularity, both mixed partials can exist at a point and disagree. Symmetry is
what lets us use real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors to describe curvature.

## The Hessian measures directional curvature

A direction is a unit vector $u$. Restrict the multivariable function to the line through $x$ in
that direction:

$$
g(t)=f(x+tu).
$$

This turns the problem back into one-variable calculus. Apply the chain rule once:

$$
g'(t)=\nabla f(x+tu)^T u.
$$

Apply it again. The derivative of the gradient is the Hessian, so

$$
g''(t)=u^T H_f(x+tu)u.
$$

At $t=0$ we obtain the central identity

$$
\boxed{D_u^2 f(x)=u^T H_f(x)u}.
$$

This is a proof, not an analogy: the quadratic form $u^THu$ is exactly the ordinary second
derivative seen by an observer walking along $u$.

For our example, take $u=(3/5,4/5)$. Then

$$
Hu=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&6\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}0.6\\0.8\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}2.0\\5.4\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
u^THu=0.6(2.0)+0.8(5.4)=5.52.
$$

So a slice of the surface in this direction has second derivative $5.52$.

### Eigenvalues are the principal curvatures

Because a real symmetric Hessian has an orthonormal eigendecomposition $H=Q\Lambda Q^T$, write
$u=Qz$. Orthogonal matrices preserve length, so $\lVert z\rVert=\lVert u\rVert=1$. Then

$$
u^THu=z^T\Lambda z=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i z_i^2.
$$

The nonnegative weights $z_i^2$ sum to one. Directional curvature is therefore a weighted
average of the eigenvalues, and

$$
\lambda_{\min}\le u^THu\le\lambda_{\max}.
$$

Equality occurs at the corresponding eigenvectors. For our $2\times2$ Hessian, the eigenvalues
are $4-\sqrt5\approx1.764$ and $4+\sqrt5\approx6.236$. These are the smallest and largest
possible curvatures of any unit-length slice.

## The quadratic approximation

The gradient gives the best local linear model. The Hessian supplies the missing bend. For a
small displacement $h$, second-order Taylor expansion says

$$
f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^Th+\frac12h^TH_f(x)h+r(h),
$$

where $r(h)/\lVert h\rVert^2\to0$ as $h\to0$. The notation says that the remainder becomes
negligible relative to a squared step.

One way to prove the formula is to define $\phi(t)=f(x+th)$ and apply the one-variable Taylor
theorem at $t=0$. The chain-rule identities above give $\phi'(0)=\nabla f(x)^Th$ and
$\phi''(0)=h^TH_f(x)h$. Substituting $t=1$ yields the displayed expansion.<Sidenote>The precise
remainder can also be written as an integral involving the Hessian along the segment from $x$ to
$x+h$. Continuity of the Hessian makes it little-o of $\lVert h\rVert^2$.</Sidenote>

At $x=(1,-1)$, our quadratic has $f(x)=3$ and $\nabla f(x)=(1,-5)$. Choose
$h=(-0.2,0.1)$. The three terms are

| contribution | value |
| --- | ---: |
| current value, $f(x)$ | $3.00$ |
| linear change, $\nabla f(x)^Th$ | $-0.70$ |
| curvature correction, $\tfrac12h^THh$ | $0.05$ |
| predicted $f(x+h)$ | $2.35$ |

The function is already quadratic, so the remainder is zero and the prediction is exact:
$f(0.8,-0.9)=2.35$. For a general smooth function it is a local approximation.

## Classifying stationary points

A **stationary point** $x_\star$ satisfies $\nabla f(x_\star)=0$. At such a point the linear
Taylor term vanishes, leaving

$$
f(x_\star+h)-f(x_\star)=\frac12h^TH_f(x_\star)h+r(h).
$$

The signs of the Hessian eigenvalues now classify the local shape:

| Hessian at $x_\star$ | eigenvalue signs | conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| positive definite | all positive | strict local minimum |
| negative definite | all negative | strict local maximum |
| indefinite | both signs | saddle point |
| semidefinite but not definite | zeros and one sign | test is inconclusive |

### Proof of the second-derivative test

Suppose the smallest eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\min}>0$. The eigenvalue bound gives
$h^THh\ge\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2$. Because
$r(h)=o(\lVert h\rVert^2)$, there is a small enough neighborhood in which

$$
|r(h)|\le\frac14\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2.
$$

For every nonzero $h$ in that neighborhood,

$$
f(x_\star+h)-f(x_\star)
\ge\frac12\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2
-\frac14\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2>0.
$$

Thus $x_\star$ is a strict local minimum. Applying the same argument to $-f$ proves the maximum
case. If $H$ is indefinite, choose unit eigenvectors $v_+$ and $v_-$ with positive and negative
eigenvalues. For sufficiently small $t$, the leading $t^2$ term makes
$f(x_\star+tv_+)-f(x_\star)$ positive and
$f(x_\star+tv_-)-f(x_\star)$ negative. Every neighborhood contains values on both sides, so the
point is a saddle.

### Why a zero eigenvalue is inconclusive

Positive semidefinite means only $h^THh\ge0$; it gives no positive quadratic lower bound in a
zero-curvature direction. In one dimension, all three functions $x^4$, $-x^4$, and $x^3$ have
$f'(0)=f''(0)=0$, yet the origin is respectively a strict minimum, a strict maximum, and neither.
Higher-order terms decide the result.

## Convexity: a global consequence of local curvature

A function is **convex** on a convex set when every chord lies above its graph:

$$
f((1-t)x+ty)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
$$

For a twice continuously differentiable function on an open convex domain,

$$
f\text{ is convex}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_f(x)\succeq0
\text{ for every }x.
$$

Here $H\succeq0$ means **positive semidefinite**: $u^THu\ge0$ for every $u$.

### Proof in both directions

Fix any points $x,y$ and restrict $f$ to their line segment:

$$
g(t)=f(x+t(y-x)),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
$$

If every Hessian is positive semidefinite, then

$$
g''(t)=(y-x)^TH_f(x+t(y-x))(y-x)\ge0.
$$

A twice differentiable one-variable function with nonnegative second derivative is convex, so
$g(t)\le(1-t)g(0)+tg(1)$. Substituting the definition of $g$ proves convexity of $f$.

Conversely, suppose $f$ is convex. Its restriction $g(t)=f(x+tu)$ to every line is convex.
Where $g''(0)$ exists, one-variable convexity gives $g''(0)\ge0$. But
$g''(0)=u^TH_f(x)u$. This holds for every $u$, so the Hessian is positive semidefinite.

This theorem is stronger than the stationary-point test. A positive definite Hessian at one
point proves only a local minimum there. A positive semidefinite Hessian throughout a convex
domain proves a global shape, so every local minimum is global.

## Newton's method uses curvature to choose a step

At a current point $x$, replace $f(x+p)$ by its quadratic Taylor model

$$
m(p)=f(x)+g^Tp+\frac12p^THp,
$$

where $g=\nabla f(x)$ and $H=H_f(x)$. Differentiate this model with respect to $p$:

$$
\nabla_p m(p)=g+Hp.
$$

If $H$ is positive definite, the model has a unique minimum. Setting its gradient to zero gives

$$
\boxed{Hp_N=-g}.
$$

The **Newton step** $p_N$ is found by solving this linear system, not by explicitly forming
$H^{-1}$. Gradient descent uses only $-g$ and one global learning rate. Newton's method divides
the gradient by curvature in each eigenvector direction: large-curvature directions get short
steps and flat directions get long ones.

Return to $f(x,y)=x^2+xy+3y^2$ at $(1,-1)$. We already found
$g=(1,-5)$, so

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&6\end{bmatrix}p_N
=-\begin{bmatrix}1\\-5\end{bmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p_N=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

One step reaches $(0,0)$, the exact minimizer. This always happens for a strictly convex
quadratic because its Taylor model is the function itself. For a nonquadratic objective, the
model is only local; line searches, trust regions, or damping are commonly used to stop an
overconfident step when the Hessian is indefinite or poorly conditioned.

### A small logistic-regression example

For binary labels $y_i\in\{-1,1\}$, feature rows $x_i^T$, and $L_2$ regularization $\lambda>0$,
consider

$$
L(w)=\sum_i\log(1+e^{-y_i x_i^Tw})+\frac\lambda2\lVert w\rVert^2.
$$

Writing $p_i=\sigma(x_i^Tw)$ and $W=\operatorname{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$, differentiation gives

$$
\nabla^2L(w)=X^TWX+\lambda I.
$$

This Hessian is positive definite when $\lambda>0$. Indeed, for nonzero $v$,

$$
v^T\nabla^2L(w)v=(Xv)^TW(Xv)+\lambda\lVert v\rVert^2>0,
$$

because every diagonal entry of $W$ is nonnegative. The proof simultaneously establishes strict
convexity and uniqueness of the minimizer.

Take feature rows $(1,0),(1,1),(1,2),(1,3)$, labels $(-1,-1,1,1)$, $w=(0,0)$, and
$\lambda=0.5$. Since every $p_i=0.5$, a direct computation gives

$$
g=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
H=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&1.5\\1.5&4.0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
p_N=\begin{bmatrix}-0.8\\0.8\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvalues are approximately $0.797$ and $4.703$, both positive. The intercept and slope
are coupled through the off-diagonal $1.5$, and the Newton solve accounts for that coupling.

## Large problems: use the Hessian without storing it

An $n$-parameter model has an $n\times n$ Hessian. Storing it costs $O(n^2)$ numbers and a dense
factorization costs $O(n^3)$ operations, which is unrealistic for modern neural networks.

Many algorithms need only a **Hessian--vector product** (HVP), $Hv$. It is the directional
derivative of the gradient:

$$
Hv=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\nabla f(x+tv)\right|_{t=0}.
$$

Automatic differentiation can compute this product without materializing all $n^2$ entries.
Krylov methods such as conjugate gradients then approximately solve $Hp=-g$ using repeated HVPs.
The approach preserves useful curvature information while keeping memory closer to that of a
gradient computation.<Sidenote>PyTorch exposes full Hessians and HVPs through function transforms.
Its documentation shows an HVP as a Jacobian--vector product of the gradient, which is precisely
the identity above.</Sidenote>

The Hessian also explains conditioning. For a positive definite quadratic, the condition number
$\kappa(H)=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measures the ratio between the stiffest and flattest
directions. A large ratio makes fixed-step gradient descent zig-zag or crawl. Newton scaling
normalizes those unequal curvatures in the ideal quadratic case, though solving the Newton system
can itself become numerically difficult when $\lambda_{\min}$ is tiny.

## What the Hessian does not tell you

- It is local. Curvature at one point does not describe a distant part of a nonconvex surface.
- A positive semidefinite Hessian at one stationary point does not prove a minimum; zero
  eigenvalues leave room for higher-order behavior.
- The simple eigenvalue picture assumes a symmetric Hessian, which follows under the usual
  continuity conditions on mixed partials.
- Parameters can create symmetries and redundant directions. A zero eigenvalue may reflect a
  reparameterization rather than an interesting flat basin.
- An indefinite Hessian makes the raw Newton direction unreliable for minimization, because the
  quadratic model is unbounded below along a negative-curvature direction.

## Summary

- The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient and collects every second partial derivative.
- The identity $D_u^2f(x)=u^TH_f(x)u$ turns the matrix into curvature along any direction; its
  eigenvectors give the principal directions and its eigenvalues give their curvatures.
- At a stationary point, definite Hessians prove strict minima or maxima, indefinite Hessians
  prove saddles, and zero eigenvalues make the test inconclusive.
- A twice differentiable function on a convex domain is convex exactly when its Hessian is
  positive semidefinite everywhere.
- Newton's method minimizes the local quadratic model by solving $Hp=-g$; large-scale methods
  often access the same curvature through Hessian--vector products.
