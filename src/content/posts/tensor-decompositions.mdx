## Background

A lot of data has more than two indices. A video is height × width × time. A recommender log is
user × item × day. A fluorescence experiment measures intensity for every sample × emission
wavelength × excitation wavelength. Such a **tensor**, a $d$-way array of numbers, is usually
flattened into a matrix so that the tools we know (PCA, the SVD, low-rank factorizations) can be
applied. Flattening throws away the fact that two of the indices were separate, and the
resulting matrix can be enormous: one dimension becomes a product of several.

A **tensor decomposition** writes a tensor as a combination of a few small pieces instead, keeping
every mode separate. This matters for two reasons. It *compresses*: a $100 \times 100 \times 100$
tensor has a million entries, but a good decomposition may need a few thousand numbers. And it
*explains*: under mild conditions the pieces of a CP decomposition are unique, so they often
correspond to real causes in the data, something a matrix factorization cannot promise.

This post follows Ballard and Kolda's textbook *Tensor Decompositions for Data
Science*.<Sidenote>The final draft is free to read at [users.wfu.edu/ballard/pdfs/tensor_textbook.pdf](https://users.wfu.edu/ballard/pdfs/tensor_textbook.pdf). Section and example numbers below refer to it.</Sidenote>
It first builds the vocabulary: fibers, slices and unfoldings, then three operations that every
algorithm uses. It then covers the three main decompositions in turn: **CP** (a sum of rank-1
tensors), **Tucker** (a small core tensor times one matrix per mode) and the **tensor train** (a
chain of small 3-way tensors). Each comes with the algorithm that fits it and a worked example.
It ends with a guide to choosing between them. If matrix factorizations are rusty, read
[Matrix Decompositions](/blog/matrix-decompositions/) first; the SVD does much of the work here.

## Tensors, fibers and slices

A $d$-way tensor $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_1 \times n_2 \times \cdots \times n_d}$ has $d$
**modes**, and mode $k$ has size $n_k$. The number of modes $d$ is the **order**. A vector is a
tensor of order 1 and a matrix one of order 2; "higher-order" means $d \ge 3$. We write
entries as $x_{ijk}$ or $\mathcal{X}(i, j, k)$, with indices starting from 1.

Most of this post uses one running example, a $3 \times 4 \times 2$ tensor whose entries are
1 to 24, numbered so that the first index changes fastest:

$$
\mathcal{X}(:, :, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 & 10 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 & 11 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathcal{X}(:, :, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 13 & 16 & 19 & 22 \\ 14 & 17 & 20 & 23 \\ 15 & 18 & 21 & 24 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

This **column-major** (or **natural**) order, where $x_{ijk}$ is entry
$i + 3(j-1) + 12(k-1)$ of the list, is the order the book uses everywhere, and the numbers make it
visible in every figure below.<Sidenote>It is Fortran's and MATLAB's memory order. NumPy defaults to row-major, so pass `order="F"` to `reshape` to match the formulas here. See [Strided Tensors](/blog/strided-tensors/) for how a linear order becomes strides.</Sidenote>

### Fibers and slices

A **fiber** is what you get by fixing every index but one. It generalizes the rows and columns of
a matrix. A **mode-$k$ fiber** lets index $k$ run and fixes the rest; for a 3-way tensor the
mode-1, mode-2 and mode-3 fibers are called **column**, **row** and **tube** fibers. Our
$3 \times 4 \times 2$ tensor has $4 \cdot 2 = 8$ column fibers of length 3, $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ row
fibers of length 4 and $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ tube fibers of length 2. Fibers are always treated as
column vectors.

A **slice** fixes one index instead and keeps the other two, giving a matrix. Fixing $i$ gives a
**horizontal** slice $\mathcal{X}(i, :, :)$, fixing $j$ a **lateral** slice
$\mathcal{X}(:, j, :)$ and fixing $k$ a **frontal** slice $\mathcal{X}(:, :, k)$. The two
matrices above are the frontal slices.

### Unfolding

Every algorithm in this post works by turning a tensor question into a matrix question. The
bridge is the **mode-$k$ unfolding** (or **matricization**) $\mathbf{X}_{(k)}$: the matrix
whose columns are the mode-$k$ fibers. It has $n_k$ rows, one per value of index $k$, and
$\prod_{\ell \ne k} n_\ell$ columns. The columns are put in natural order: among the remaining
indices, the lowest mode changes fastest (Definition 2.18 in the book). For the running example:

$$
\mathbf{X}_{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 4 & 7 & 10 & 13 & 16 & 19 & 22 \\
2 & 5 & 8 & 11 & 14 & 17 & 20 & 23 \\
3 & 6 & 9 & 12 & 15 & 18 & 21 & 24
\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\mathbf{X}_{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 & 13 & 14 & 15 \\
4 & 5 & 6 & 16 & 17 & 18 \\
7 & 8 & 9 & 19 & 20 & 21 \\
10 & 11 & 12 & 22 & 23 & 24
\end{bmatrix},
$$

$$
\mathbf{X}_{(3)} = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 3 & \cdots & 12 \\
13 & 14 & 15 & \cdots & 24
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\mathbf{X}_{(1)}$ is just the frontal slices side by side. In $\mathbf{X}_{(2)}$ each column
is a row fiber: column 1 is $\mathcal{X}(1, :, 1) = (1, 4, 7, 10)$, column 2 is
$\mathcal{X}(2, :, 1)$, and the index $i$ runs fastest across columns before $k$ moves on. In the
figure above, the colours in the unfolding match the colours in the cube, which is the whole
point: *columns of $\mathbf{X}_{(k)}$ are mode-$k$ fibers*. Choose *Slices* and the rows light up
instead: row $i$ of $\mathbf{X}_{(k)}$ holds the mode-$k$ slice $i$.

In NumPy the unfolding is one line, and it reproduces the unfoldings printed in the book's
Example 1.6:

```python
import numpy as np

def unfold(X, k):  # k is 0-based here
    return np.reshape(np.moveaxis(X, k, 0), (X.shape[k], -1), order="F")
```

The ranks of the unfoldings already say something about the tensor. Here
$\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_{(1)}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_{(2)}) = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_{(3)}) = 2$:
every fiber of $\mathcal{X}$ lies in a 2-dimensional space. We will meet this triple again as the
*multilinear rank*.

## Three operations

Three operations do almost all the work in tensor algorithms. Each has a definition on entries
and an equivalent definition through an unfolding, and the unfolded version is what you compute.

### Outer products and rank-1 tensors

The **outer product** of three vectors $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^m$,
$\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is the $m \times n \times p$ tensor

$$
(\mathbf{a} \circ \mathbf{b} \circ \mathbf{c})_{ijk} = a_i\, b_j\, c_k .
$$

A tensor that can be written this way is **rank 1**. It is the 3-way version of a rank-1 matrix
$\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top$. For example, with $\mathbf{a} = (1, 2, 3)$,
$\mathbf{b} = (2, 1, 0, 1)$ and $\mathbf{c} = (1, 3)$:

$$
\mathcal{R}(:, :, 1) = 1 \cdot \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 6 & 3 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathcal{R}(:, :, 2) = 3 \cdot \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & 0 & 3 \\ 12 & 6 & 0 & 6 \\ 18 & 9 & 0 & 9 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one entry: $r_{232} = a_2 b_3 c_2 = 2 \cdot 0 \cdot 3 = 0$, and indeed column 3 is zero in
both slices.

In the figure, the two rows of $\mathbf{R}_{(3)}$ are proportional (the second is three times the
first). That is true in every mode: each unfolding of a rank-1 tensor is a rank-1 matrix,

$$
\mathbf{R}_{(1)} = \mathbf{a}\,(\mathbf{c} \otimes \mathbf{b})^\top, \qquad
\mathbf{R}_{(2)} = \mathbf{b}\,(\mathbf{c} \otimes \mathbf{a})^\top, \qquad
\mathbf{R}_{(3)} = \mathbf{c}\,(\mathbf{b} \otimes \mathbf{a})^\top,
$$

where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product (Proposition 3.7). The reversed order
$\mathbf{c} \otimes \mathbf{b}$ comes from the column-major numbering: the later mode changes
slower, and the Kronecker product's left factor changes slower.

### The mode-k product

The **mode-$k$ product** (tensor-times-matrix, **TTM**) $\mathcal{Y} = \mathcal{X} \times_k \mathbf{U}$
multiplies every mode-$k$ fiber of $\mathcal{X}$ by the matrix $\mathbf{U}$. In unfolded form it
is a single matrix product (Definition 3.14):

$$
\mathbf{Y}_{(k)} = \mathbf{U}\, \mathbf{X}_{(k)} .
$$

If $\mathbf{U}$ is $q \times n_k$, mode $k$ of the result has size $q$. As a worked example, take
the running example and

$$
\mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

which replaces each column fiber $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ by its sum and the difference
$x_1 - x_3$. The result $\mathcal{Y} = \mathcal{X} \times_1 \mathbf{U}$ is $2 \times 4 \times 2$:

$$
\mathcal{Y}(:, :, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 15 & 24 & 33 \\ -2 & -2 & -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathcal{Y}(:, :, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 42 & 51 & 60 & 69 \\ -2 & -2 & -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one entry: the fiber $\mathcal{X}(:, 1, 2) = (13, 14, 15)$ maps to
$(13 + 14 + 15,\ 13 - 15) = (42, -2)$, which is $\mathcal{Y}(:, 1, 2)$.

Two facts make TTM easy to use. Products in different modes commute,
$\mathcal{X} \times_1 \mathbf{U} \times_3 \mathbf{W} = \mathcal{X} \times_3 \mathbf{W} \times_1 \mathbf{U}$,
and products in the same mode combine,
$\mathcal{X} \times_k \mathbf{U} \times_k \mathbf{V} = \mathcal{X} \times_k (\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U})$
(Propositions 3.15 and 3.19). When $\mathbf{U}$ has orthonormal columns,
$\mathcal{X} \times_k \mathbf{U}^\top$ **compresses** mode $k$ by projecting every fiber onto
$\mathbf{U}$'s columns. That is the basic step of every Tucker algorithm.

### Khatri–Rao product and MTTKRP

The **Khatri–Rao product** of two matrices with the same number of columns is the column-wise
Kronecker product: column $\ell$ of $\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}$ is
$\mathbf{c}_\ell \otimes \mathbf{b}_\ell$. If $\mathbf{C}$ is $p \times r$ and $\mathbf{B}$ is
$n \times r$, then $\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}$ is $np \times r$.

The **matricized tensor times Khatri–Rao product** (**MTTKRP**) is

$$
\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{X}_{(1)} (\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}), \qquad
m_{i\ell} = \sum_{j} \sum_{k} x_{ijk}\, b_{j\ell}\, c_{k\ell} .
$$

It is the most expensive step of CP algorithms, $O(mnpr)$ for a dense tensor, and is the kernel
that high-performance tensor libraries optimize. A small example: with

$$
\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{X}_{(1)} (\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}) = \begin{bmatrix} 28 & 81 \\ 32 & 87 \\ 36 & 93 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check $m_{11}$ by hand. Column 1 of $\mathbf{C}$ is $(1, 0)$, so only the front slice counts, and
column 1 of $\mathbf{B}$ weights its first row $(1, 4, 7, 10)$ by $(1, 0, 1, 2)$:
$1 + 7 + 2 \cdot 10 = 28$. The MTTKRP never needs $\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}$ formed
explicitly; the double sum is enough.

## CP decomposition

The **CP decomposition** (canonical polyadic, also known as CANDECOMP/PARAFAC) writes a tensor as a
sum of $r$ rank-1 tensors:

$$
\mathcal{X} \approx \sum_{\ell = 1}^{r} \mathbf{a}_\ell \circ \mathbf{b}_\ell \circ \mathbf{c}_\ell
= [\![ \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C} ]\!],
\qquad
x_{ijk} \approx \sum_{\ell=1}^{r} a_{i\ell}\, b_{j\ell}\, c_{k\ell}.
$$

The vectors are collected as columns of the **factor matrices**
$\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ and
$\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$, and the $r$ terms are the **components**. A tensor
stored this way is a **Kruskal tensor**. It stores $r(m + n + p)$ numbers instead of $mnp$.

CP is the natural generalization of writing a matrix as a sum of rank-1 matrices,
$\mathbf{X} \approx \sum_\ell \mathbf{a}_\ell \mathbf{b}_\ell^\top$. The book calls it a
higher-order analogue of PCA, with one important difference: CP does not ask the factors to be
orthogonal.

### Unfolded, CP is a matrix factorization

Adding up the unfoldings of each rank-1 term gives the key identity (Proposition 10.8):

$$
\mathbf{X}_{(1)} = \mathbf{A}\, (\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B})^\top, \qquad
\mathbf{X}_{(2)} = \mathbf{B}\, (\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{A})^\top, \qquad
\mathbf{X}_{(3)} = \mathbf{C}\, (\mathbf{B} \odot \mathbf{A})^\top .
$$

So a CP decomposition is three matrix factorizations at once, one per unfolding, that share
their factors. Each one alone is underdetermined. Together they pin the factors down, which is
where CP's uniqueness comes from.

### Tensor rank is strange

The **rank** of a tensor is the smallest $r$ for which an exact CP decomposition exists. For
matrices, rank is easy: the SVD computes it, and an $m \times n$ matrix has rank at most
$\min(m, n)$. For tensors, almost all of that breaks.

**Rank can exceed every dimension.** Kruskal's $2 \times 2 \times 2$ example (Example 9.1) is

$$
\mathcal{X}(:, :, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathcal{X}(:, :, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with factors

$$
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one entry: $x_{122} = \sum_\ell a_{1\ell} b_{2\ell} c_{2\ell} = 1 \cdot 0 \cdot (-1) + 0
\cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, matching $\mathcal{X}(1, 2, 2) = 1$. This tensor has
rank 3 over the reals, although each mode has size 2 and each unfolding has rank
2.<Sidenote>The rank also depends on the field. The book's Example 16.1 is a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor with rank 3 over $\mathbb{R}$ but rank 2 over $\mathbb{C}$.</Sidenote>
In general the only easy bound is that the rank is at most the product of all dimensions except
the largest. The maximum rank is known only for a handful of sizes: 3 for
$2 \times 2 \times 2$, 5 for $3 \times 3 \times 3$ (Table 16.1).

**Rank is NP-hard to compute.** Deciding whether $\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{X}) \le r$ is
NP-hard (Håstad 1990; Hillar and Lim 2013). A famous case is matrix multiplication. Multiplying
two $n \times n$ matrices is a fixed $n^2 \times n^2 \times n^2$ tensor, and a rank-$r$ CP
decomposition of it is an algorithm that uses $r$ scalar multiplications. Strassen's algorithm is
a rank-7 decomposition of the $2 \times 2$ case (instead of the obvious 8). For $3 \times 3$
the rank is known only to lie between 19 and 23.

**A best rank-$r$ approximation may not exist.** For matrices, truncating the SVD gives the best
rank-$r$ approximation. For tensors, the set of rank-$r$ tensors is not closed: a sequence of
rank-2 tensors can converge to a rank-3 tensor. Take the book's Example 16.2 with unit vectors
$\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2$:

$$
\mathcal{X} = \mathbf{e}_1 \circ \mathbf{e}_1 \circ \mathbf{e}_2 + \mathbf{e}_1 \circ \mathbf{e}_2 \circ \mathbf{e}_1 + \mathbf{e}_2 \circ \mathbf{e}_1 \circ \mathbf{e}_1
\quad (\text{rank } 3),
$$

$$
\mathcal{Y}_\alpha = \alpha \left(\mathbf{e}_1 + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{e}_2\right)^{\circ 3} - \alpha\, \mathbf{e}_1^{\circ 3}
\quad (\text{rank } 2).
$$

Expanding the cube, $\mathcal{Y}_\alpha = \mathcal{X} + O(1/\alpha)$, so the error goes to 0. But the
two rank-1 terms grow like $\alpha$ and cancel each other:

| $\alpha$ | $\lVert \mathcal{X} - \mathcal{Y}_\alpha \rVert / \lVert \mathcal{X} \rVert$ | norm of each rank-1 term |
| -------: | -------------------------------------------------: | -----------------------: |
|        1 |                                             1.1547 |                     2.83 |
|        5 |                                             0.2013 |                     5.30 |
|       10 |                                             0.1002 |                    10.15 |
|       50 |                                             0.0200 |                    50.03 |
|     1000 |                                             0.0010 |                  1000.00 |

So $\mathcal{X}$ has no best rank-2 approximation: every rank-2 candidate can be beaten by a
larger $\alpha$. The smallest rank that approximates a tensor arbitrarily well is its **border
rank**, here 2. In practice this shows up as **degeneracy**: an algorithm fitting too many
components finds two that grow huge and nearly cancel. The book notes it is uncommon on real data
and can be prevented by a small penalty on the factor norms (Section 9.4.2).

### Uniqueness

A matrix factorization $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top$ is never unique: for any invertible
$\mathbf{M}$, $(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}^{-\top})^\top$ is another one. PCA
picks one by demanding orthogonal factors, which is a convention, not a fact about the data.

CP has only two unavoidable ambiguities. The components can be listed in any order
(**permutation**), and within a component the vectors can be rescaled as long as the scales
multiply to 1, for example $(4\mathbf{a}) \circ (\tfrac12\mathbf{b}) \circ (\tfrac12\mathbf{c})$
(**scaling**). If those are the only freedoms, the decomposition is **essentially unique**.

**Kruskal's theorem** (Theorem 16.14) gives a sufficient condition. The **k-rank** of a matrix is
the largest $k$ such that *every* set of $k$ columns is linearly independent. An $r$-component
decomposition $[\![\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}]\!]$ is essentially unique if

$$
\operatorname{k-rank}(\mathbf{A}) + \operatorname{k-rank}(\mathbf{B}) + \operatorname{k-rank}(\mathbf{C}) \ge 2r + 2 .
$$

If $r$ is no larger than any dimension and the factors are random, each k-rank is $r$, the sum
is $3r$, and the condition holds for every $r \ge 2$. This is why CP is used to *find* things: if
the data really is a sum of a few rank-1 effects, CP recovers those effects, not just a subspace
that contains them.

### Fitting CP with alternating least squares

Fitting a rank-$r$ CP model means solving

$$
\min_{\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}} \ \lVert \mathcal{X} - [\![\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}]\!] \rVert^2 ,
$$

a nonconvex problem. But fix $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{C}$, and the unfolded identity turns it
into a linear least-squares problem in $\mathbf{A}$:
$\lVert \mathbf{X}_{(1)} - \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B})^\top \rVert_F^2$. **CP-ALS**
(alternating least squares) solves for $\mathbf{A}$, then $\mathbf{B}$, then $\mathbf{C}$, and
repeats. Each solve uses the normal equations, which simplify because
$(\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B})^\top(\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{C}^\top\mathbf{C} \ast \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B}$,
where $\ast$ is the elementwise (Hadamard) product:

$$
\mathbf{A} \left(\mathbf{C}^\top\mathbf{C} \ast \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{B}\right) = \mathbf{X}_{(1)}(\mathbf{C} \odot \mathbf{B}) .
$$

The left matrix is only $r \times r$, so the cost is dominated by the MTTKRP on the right. A full
iteration in NumPy, using the `unfold` above:

```python
def khatri_rao(C, B):
    return np.einsum("kr,jr->kjr", C, B).reshape(-1, C.shape[1])

def cp_als_step(X, A, B, C):
    A = np.linalg.solve((C.T @ C) * (B.T @ B), (unfold(X, 0) @ khatri_rao(C, B)).T).T
    B = np.linalg.solve((C.T @ C) * (A.T @ A), (unfold(X, 1) @ khatri_rao(C, A)).T).T
    C = np.linalg.solve((B.T @ B) * (A.T @ A), (unfold(X, 2) @ khatri_rao(B, A)).T).T
    return A, B, C
```

Each step can only lower the error, since it solves its subproblem exactly. The book's
Algorithm 11.1 adds column normalization, which keeps the scaling ambiguity from drifting, and
computes the error from small $r \times r$ matrices instead of forming the model.

### Worked example: unmixing three signals

The book's flagship CP example is fluorescence spectroscopy: 18 samples, each a mixture of three
chemicals, measured at 251 emission and 21 excitation wavelengths (Section 9.6). Each chemical
contributes (its concentration) × (its emission profile) × (its excitation profile), so the
noise-free tensor is exactly rank 3.

The example here is a scaled-down synthetic version: 10 samples × 30 emission × 15 excitation
points, three chemicals with Gaussian-shaped profiles, random concentrations, and Gaussian noise
of 2% of the tensor's norm.<Sidenote>Every number in this section comes from a NumPy script. The true factors have k-ranks 3, 3 and 3, so Kruskal's condition $9 \ge 2 \cdot 3 + 2$ holds and the exact decomposition is essentially unique.</Sidenote>

## Tucker decomposition

CP restricts every component to be one $\mathbf{a}_\ell \circ \mathbf{b}_\ell \circ \mathbf{c}_\ell$
combination. The **Tucker decomposition** relaxes this: each mode gets its own basis, and a small
**core tensor** $\mathcal{G}$ says how much every combination of basis vectors contributes.

$$
\mathcal{X} \approx \mathcal{G} \times_1 \mathbf{U} \times_2 \mathbf{V} \times_3 \mathbf{W}
= [\![\mathcal{G}; \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}, \mathbf{W}]\!],
\qquad
x_{ijk} \approx \sum_{\alpha=1}^{q} \sum_{\beta=1}^{r} \sum_{\gamma=1}^{s} g_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\, u_{i\alpha}\, v_{j\beta}\, w_{k\gamma} .
$$

Here $\mathcal{G}$ is $q \times r \times s$ and $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times q}$,
$\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$, $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times s}$. This is a
**rank-$(q, r, s)$** Tucker decomposition. CP is the special case where the core is
$r \times r \times r$ and diagonal.

<TensorFormats
	initial="tucker"
	caption="Tucker: a small core G, multiplied in each mode by a factor matrix. The colours match each factor to the core dimension it expands."
/>

Tucker stores $qrs + mq + nr + ps$ numbers. The **compression ratio** is
$mnp / (qrs + mq + nr + ps)$: for a $100 \times 100 \times 100$ tensor and a $25 \times 25 \times 25$
core it is $10^6 / (15625 + 7500) = 43.2$ (Example 4.2).

### Multilinear rank and an exact example

The **multilinear rank** of a tensor is the triple of its unfolding ranks,
$(\operatorname{rank}\mathbf{X}_{(1)}, \operatorname{rank}\mathbf{X}_{(2)}, \operatorname{rank}\mathbf{X}_{(3)})$.
Unlike tensor rank, it is cheap to compute: three matrix ranks. And a Tucker decomposition with
exactly the multilinear rank reproduces the tensor with zero error. Our running example has
multilinear rank $(2, 2, 2)$, so it is exactly a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ core with three
two-column factors.

The book's Example 4.1 shows an exact rank-$(2, 2, 2)$ decomposition of a $3 \times 3 \times 3$
tensor:

$$
\mathcal{G}(:, :, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathcal{G}(:, :, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathbf{V} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \
\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check $x_{111}$ by hand. Since $w_{11} = 0$ and $u_{12} = 0$, only terms with $\gamma = 2$ and
$\alpha = 1$ survive: $g_{112} u_{11} v_{11} w_{12} + g_{122} u_{11} v_{12} w_{12} =
1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 0 = 1$, which is the book's $x_{111}$.

Tucker is **never unique**. For any invertible $\mathbf{M}$,
$\mathcal{G} \times_1 \mathbf{M}^{-1}$ with $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}$ gives the same tensor. Only the
*subspaces* spanned by the factor columns are determined. So Tucker is used for compression and
for finding subspaces, not for reading meaning off individual factor columns. The freedom can be
used to make every factor orthonormal, which is what the algorithms below do.

### HOSVD and ST-HOSVD

If the factors are orthonormal, the best core for given factors is just the projection
$\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{X} \times_1 \mathbf{U}^\top \times_2 \mathbf{V}^\top \times_3 \mathbf{W}^\top$.
So the question is how to pick the factors. The **higher-order SVD** (**HOSVD**) picks each one
independently: $\mathbf{U}$ is the top $q$ left singular vectors of $\mathbf{X}_{(1)}$, the best
$q$-dimensional basis for the mode-1 fibers, and likewise for $\mathbf{V}$ and $\mathbf{W}$.

```text
HOSVD(X, (q, r, s))
  U ← top q left singular vectors of X(1)
  V ← top r left singular vectors of X(2)
  W ← top s left singular vectors of X(3)
  G ← X ×1 Uᵀ ×2 Vᵀ ×3 Wᵀ
```

Running it on Example 4.1's tensor gives $\mathcal{G}(:, :, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 8.3325 & -0.0960 \\ 0.1117 & 3.0290 \end{bmatrix}$
up to signs, the same core the book prints in Example 4.4, and an exact
reconstruction.<Sidenote>Singular vectors are only defined up to sign, so our factors and core differ from the book's by sign flips. The reconstruction matches the tensor to $10^{-15}$.</Sidenote>
It is a different exact decomposition from the one above, which illustrates the non-uniqueness.

The HOSVD error has a clean bound (Theorem 7.3): the squared error is at most the sum, over the
three modes, of the squared singular values that were thrown away,

$$
\lVert \mathcal{X} - \hat{\mathcal{X}} \rVert^2 \le
\sum_{i > q} \sigma_i(\mathbf{X}_{(1)})^2 + \sum_{j > r} \sigma_j(\mathbf{X}_{(2)})^2 + \sum_{k > s} \sigma_k(\mathbf{X}_{(3)})^2 .
$$

Each mode's discarded energy is at most the optimal error, so HOSVD is **quasi-optimal**: its
error is within a factor $\sqrt{d}$ of the best possible Tucker approximation of the same size.
The bound also gives a way to hit a target accuracy $\varepsilon$: truncate each mode's SVD so
that it discards at most $\varepsilon^2 \lVert \mathcal{X} \rVert^2 / d$.

The **sequentially truncated HOSVD** (**ST-HOSVD**) is the variant to use in practice. It
compresses each mode as soon as its factor is found, so later SVDs run on a smaller tensor:

```text
ST-HOSVD(X, (q, r, s))
  U ← top q left singular vectors of X(1);  G ← X ×1 Uᵀ    (now q × n × p)
  V ← top r left singular vectors of G(2);  G ← G ×2 Vᵀ    (now q × r × p)
  W ← top s left singular vectors of G(3);  G ← G ×3 Wᵀ    (now q × r × s)
```

It has the same $\sqrt{d}$ guarantee. For a $d$-way $n \times \cdots \times n$ tensor it costs
$O(n^{d+1})$ against HOSVD's $O(d\, n^{d+1})$, and it often gives a slightly smaller error.

### HOOI

The **higher-order orthogonal iteration** (**HOOI**) improves the factors iteratively. It
compresses the tensor with the current factors in every mode but one, then recomputes that mode's
factor from the SVD of the small result:

```text
HOOI(X, (q, r, s)), starting from any orthonormal V, W
  repeat
    U ← top q left singular vectors of (X ×2 Vᵀ ×3 Wᵀ)(1)
    V ← top r left singular vectors of (X ×1 Uᵀ ×3 Wᵀ)(2)
    W ← top s left singular vectors of (X ×1 Uᵀ ×2 Vᵀ)(3)
  G ← X ×1 Uᵀ ×2 Vᵀ ×3 Wᵀ
```

For an $n \times \cdots \times n$ tensor one HOOI iteration costs $O(n^d r)$, because its SVDs
are of small, already-compressed unfoldings. That is cheaper than ST-HOSVD's $O(n^{d+1})$ by a
factor of about $n / r$. The book reports that from a random start it typically
matches ST-HOSVD's error within a couple of iterations. It cannot choose ranks from an error
target, though, since it needs the ranks up front.

### Worked example: compressing a smooth tensor

Tucker shines on data that varies smoothly along each mode. Take the function tensor the book
uses in Section 8.3, in three modes:

$$
x_{ijk} = \frac{1}{t_i + t_j + t_k}, \qquad t_i = 1 + \tfrac{9(i - 1)}{n - 1}, \qquad n = 50 .
$$

It has $50^3 = 125{,}000$ entries. The singular values of its mode-1 unfolding fall by about a
factor of 15 each: relative to the largest, they are $1$, $0.064$, $0.0043$, $0.00029$,
$0.0000196$. Truncating all three modes to rank $r$:

| $r$ | HOSVD error | ST-HOSVD error | HOOI (3 iterations) | Bound (Theorem 7.3) | Compression |
| --: | ----------: | -------------: | ------------------: | ------------------: | ----------: |
|   1 |   $7.74 \times 10^{-2}$ |   $7.74 \times 10^{-2}$ |   $7.74 \times 10^{-2}$ |   $1.11 \times 10^{-1}$ |        828× |
|   2 |   $6.37 \times 10^{-3}$ |   $6.37 \times 10^{-3}$ |   $6.37 \times 10^{-3}$ |   $7.41 \times 10^{-3}$ |        406× |
|   3 |   $4.67 \times 10^{-4}$ |   $4.67 \times 10^{-4}$ |   $4.67 \times 10^{-4}$ |   $5.04 \times 10^{-4}$ |        262× |
|   4 |   $3.25 \times 10^{-5}$ |   $3.25 \times 10^{-5}$ |   $3.25 \times 10^{-5}$ |   $3.39 \times 10^{-5}$ |        188× |
|   6 |   $1.46 \times 10^{-7}$ |   $1.46 \times 10^{-7}$ |   $1.46 \times 10^{-7}$ |   $1.48 \times 10^{-7}$ |        112× |

Errors are relative, $\lVert \mathcal{X} - \hat{\mathcal{X}} \rVert / \lVert \mathcal{X} \rVert$.
A $4 \times 4 \times 4$ core plus three $50 \times 4$ factors, 664 numbers, reproduces all 125,000
entries with a relative error of $3 \times 10^{-5}$. The three algorithms agree to three significant figures here (they differ
in the fourth), which matches the book's observation that the choice of Tucker algorithm is
mostly about speed, not accuracy.

## Tensor train

Tucker has one weakness: the core has $r^d$ entries. For a $d$-way tensor that is still
exponential in $d$, the **curse of dimensionality**, just with a smaller base. For $d = 3$ this
does not matter; for $d = 10$ it does.

The **tensor train** (**TT**) decomposition, also known as a **matrix product state** in quantum
physics, avoids the $r^d$ core. It writes the tensor as a chain of 3-way **cores**
$\mathcal{G}_k$ of size $r_{k-1} \times n_k \times r_k$, with $r_0 = r_d = 1$, so that each entry
is a product of matrices:

$$
x_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_d} \approx
\underbrace{\mathcal{G}_1(i_1, :)}_{1 \times r_1}\,
\underbrace{\mathcal{G}_2(:, i_2, :)}_{r_1 \times r_2} \cdots
\underbrace{\mathcal{G}_d(:, i_d)}_{r_{d-1} \times 1} .
$$

	caption="Tensor train for d = 5 in tensor-network notation: each circle is a core, each line an index. Lines between cores (labelled r) are summed over; the dangling lines (labelled n) are the tensor's own indices."
/>

With every $r_k = r$, TT stores $(d - 2)\,n r^2 + 2nr$ numbers: linear in $d$. It is computed by
**TT-SVD** (Algorithm 8.2), which peels off one mode at a time. Reshape the tensor to an
$n_1 \times (n_2 \cdots n_d)$ matrix, keep the top $r_1$ left singular vectors as $\mathcal{G}_1$,
reshape the remaining $r_1 \times (n_2 \cdots n_d)$ factor to $(r_1 n_2) \times (n_3 \cdots n_d)$,
and repeat. Truncating each SVD to discard at most $\varepsilon^2 \lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert^2/(d-1)$
guarantees relative error $\varepsilon$, and the result is quasi-optimal within $\sqrt{d - 1}$
(Theorem 8.7).

The same function tensor in more modes shows the difference. Both methods were asked for the
same relative error $\varepsilon$, choosing their ranks automatically:

| Tensor | $\varepsilon$ | Full size | ST-HOSVD ranks | Tucker size | TT ranks $r_1, \dots, r_{d-1}$ | TT size |
| ------ | ------------: | --------: | -------------- | ----------: | ------------------------------ | ------: |
| $d = 5$, $n = 10$ | $10^{-2}$ | 100,000 | 2 in each mode | 132 | 2, 2, 2, 2 | 160 |
| $d = 5$, $n = 10$ | $10^{-6}$ | 100,000 | 5 in each mode | 3,375 | 5, 6, 6, 5 | 1,060 |
| $d = 8$, $n = 6$ | $10^{-2}$ | 1,679,616 | 2 in each mode | 352 | 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2 | 168 |
| $d = 8$, $n = 6$ | $10^{-6}$ | 1,679,616 | 5 in each mode | 390,865 | 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 5, 5 | 1,152 |

For 5 modes at low accuracy, Tucker is slightly smaller. For 8 modes at $10^{-6}$, Tucker's
$5^8 = 390{,}625$-entry core makes it 340 times larger than the tensor train. The book warns
that TT ranks and Tucker ranks are not comparable, and that for a 3-way tensor TT is just Tucker
compressed in two modes; which one wins depends on the data and on $d$.

## Choosing a decomposition

The book's closing chapter compares the decompositions on five traits (Table 17.1), adding the
truncated SVD of a "square" unfolding for reference:

| Trait | Tucker | Tensor train | CP | SVD of an unfolding |
| ----- | :----: | :----------: | :-: | :-----------------: |
| Size, $n \times \cdots \times n$ tensor | $r^d + dnr$ | $\approx d n r^2$ | $d n r$ | $\approx 2 r\, n^{d/2}$ |
| Rank computable for a given error | yes | yes | no (NP-hard) | yes |
| Quasi-optimal algorithm | yes ($\sqrt{d}$) | yes ($\sqrt{d-1}$) | no | yes (optimal) |
| Orthonormal factors | yes | yes | no | yes |
| Essentially unique | no | no | yes, under mild conditions | only with orthogonality imposed |

The rules of thumb that follow:

- **To find interpretable structure, use CP.** It is the only one with unique factors, so each
  component can be read as a real effect (a chemical, a neuron's response, a crime pattern). Pay
  for it by choosing $r$ by experiment and running ALS from several starts.
- **To compress a 3-, 4- or 5-way tensor to a guaranteed accuracy, use Tucker via ST-HOSVD.** It
  needs only SVDs, chooses its own ranks from $\varepsilon$, and allows cheap partial
  reconstruction: rebuilding one slice only needs the matching rows of the factors.
- **For many modes, use the tensor train.** It keeps storage linear in $d$ with the same kind of
  accuracy guarantee.
- **They combine.** CP can be run on a Tucker core instead of the full tensor, which is much
  cheaper (the book's CANDELINC, Section 17.2), and a CP model is a Tucker model with a diagonal
  core.

## Summary

- Fibers generalize rows and columns, and the mode-$k$ unfolding lines up the mode-$k$ fibers as
  columns. Every tensor algorithm here is a sequence of matrix operations on unfoldings: mode
  products $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}_{(k)}$, MTTKRPs and SVDs.
- CP writes a tensor as a sum of $r$ rank-1 tensors. It is compact and, unlike any matrix
  factorization, essentially unique under mild conditions (Kruskal's k-rank condition), so its
  factors can recover real underlying signals. But tensor rank is NP-hard, can exceed every
  dimension, and a best low-rank approximation may not exist.
- CP-ALS fits CP by solving one linear least-squares problem per factor in turn. It never
  increases the error, but it can stall in swamps.
- Tucker uses a core tensor and one orthonormal basis per mode. It is not unique, but
  HOSVD/ST-HOSVD compute it from SVDs with an error within $\sqrt{d}$ of optimal, at any requested
  accuracy.
- The tensor train replaces Tucker's $r^d$ core by a chain of small 3-way cores, so storage grows
  linearly with the number of modes.
