## Background

Every neural network comes with **hyperparameters** (HPs): numbers that training does not learn,
such as the learning rate, the initialization scale and the learning-rate schedule. Bad values
cost a lot. Training diverges, or it converges to a worse model. So people search for good
values, usually by training the model many times. For a model with billions of parameters,
one training run already costs millions of dollars, so a search is out of the question. The
common workaround is to tune a small model and hope the values carry over to the large one.
They often don't. GPT-3, for example, used a different learning rate for every model size.<Sidenote>Brown et al. (2020), Table 2.1: the learning rate falls from $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$ for the 125M model to $0.6 \times 10^{-4}$ for the 175B model, and each value was chosen separately.</Sidenote>

The paper *Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter
Transfer* by Greg Yang, Edward Hu and colleagues at Microsoft and OpenAI (2022) shows that the
hope fails because of how networks are **parametrized**: how their initialization and learning
rates are scaled as the width changes. In the usual parametrization, the best learning rate
drifts as a network gets wider. In the **Maximal Update Parametrization** (μP), it stays put.
This turns a hope into a procedure, **μTransfer**: write the target model in μP, tune a narrow
copy of it, and copy the tuned values to the wide model without touching it again. With it, the
authors tuned a 6.7B-parameter GPT-3 by tuning a 40M-parameter proxy, for 7% of the cost of
pretraining, and beat the published model.

This post covers the paper in the following order:

1. **The idea**, with a one-line example built on the central limit theorem.
2. **The failure**: learning rates drift with width in the standard parametrization (SP). We
   reproduce this on CIFAR-10.
3. **μP**: the scaling rules in one table, and what changes in practice.
4. **Why SP breaks**, worked through on a two-weight network.
5. **The mathematics of Appendix J**: how large $Av$ is for the three kinds of matrices that
   appear in training, the desiderata that single out μP, the derivation of every row of the
   table, and Lemma J.1, which shows three tables describe the same network.
6. **The infinite-width theory** of the predecessor paper, Tensor Programs IV: abc-parametrizations,
   the stability theorem, the Dynamical Dichotomy (feature learning *or* kernel regime, never both),
   why μP is the unique "maximal" parametrization, and an explicit infinite-width limit.
7. **Practice**: which HPs transfer, the `mup` package, and the paper's results.

Readers who know [the Hessian](/blog/hessian-matrix/) and [matrix calculus](/blog/matrix-calculus/)
have more than enough background. The only probability we need is the law of large numbers and
the central limit theorem, which we restate when we use them.

## The idea: the right parametrization makes the optimum stable

### A one-line example

Section 2 of the paper starts from the **central limit theorem** (CLT). If $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are
independent samples with mean 0 and variance 1, then

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt n}(x_1 + \dots + x_n) \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal N(0, 1) \qquad (n \to \infty).
$$

The factor $1/\sqrt n$ is the only scaling that gives a nontrivial limit. With $1/n$ the sum goes
to 0 (this is the **law of large numbers**, LLN); with $1$ its variance grows like $n$.

Now suppose we want to choose a number $c$ to minimize

$$
F_n(c) = \mathbb E_{x_1, \dots, x_n}\, f\big(c\,(x_1 + \dots + x_n)\big)
$$

for some bounded continuous $f$. Think of $x_1, \dots, x_n$ as the random initial weights of a
width-$n$ network, $c$ as a hyperparameter such as the learning rate, and $f$ as the loss after
training. Then $F_n$ is the expected loss over random initializations.

Write $c = \alpha / \sqrt n$. By the CLT,

$$
G_n(\alpha) := F_n(\alpha/\sqrt n) = \mathbb E\, f\Big(\alpha \cdot \tfrac{x_1 + \dots + x_n}{\sqrt n}\Big)
\;\longrightarrow\; G_\infty(\alpha) := \mathbb E\, f\big(\mathcal N(0, \alpha^2)\big).
$$

The function $G_n$ settles down to a fixed function of $\alpha$, so its minimizer $\alpha^*_n$
settles down too. Once $n$ is large, the best $\alpha$ for width $n$ is close to the best
$\alpha$ for any larger width $N$. We can tune at width $n$ and **copy $\alpha$**. Copying $c$
instead would be wrong by a factor $\sqrt{N/n}$, since $c^*_N \approx c^*_n \sqrt{n/N}$. The
paper calls $c = \alpha/\sqrt n$ the **correct parametrization** of this problem: the one in
which the optimal hyperparameter does not move.

Let's make it concrete. Take Rademacher $x_i$ ($+1$ or $-1$ with equal probability) and
$f(y) = 1 - e^{-(y^2 - 1)^2}$, which is bounded and smallest at $|y| = 1$. The sum
$S = x_1 + \dots + x_n$ equals $2K - n$ with $K \sim \text{Binomial}(n, 1/2)$, so $G_n$ can be
computed exactly. The two charts below show the same functions in the two parametrizations.

limit is $\alpha^*_\infty = 0.7335$ (all from a script). In the second chart, the minimizer of

$$
G_4(1) = \tfrac{6}{16}(1 - e^{-1}) + \tfrac{8}{16}\cdot 0 + \tfrac{2}{16}(1 - e^{-9}) = 0.2370 + 0.1250 = 0.3620,
$$

which is what the script gives.

### Every hyperparameter has to be parametrized

Appendix C of the paper adds a warning. A real network has many HPs that interact. Model this as

$$
F_n(c_1, \dots, c_k) = \mathbb E\, f\big((c_1 + \dots + c_k)(x_1 + \dots + x_n)\big).
$$

The correct parametrization is $\alpha_i = c_i \sqrt n$ for *every* $i$. Suppose we reparametrize
only $\alpha_1$ and leave $c_2, \dots, c_k$ fixed. The loss depends on
$(\alpha_1 + \sqrt n\,(c_2 + \dots + c_k))$, so the best $\alpha_1$ is

$$
\alpha_1^* = \alpha^* - \sqrt n\,(c_2 + \dots + c_k),
$$

which still runs off to $-\infty$. With $\alpha^* = 0.7335$ and a single fixed $c_2 = 0.1$, the
best $\alpha_1$ is $0.33$ at $n = 16$, $-0.87$ at $n = 256$ and $-5.67$ at $n = 4096$. **One
wrongly scaled hyperparameter forces the correctly scaled ones to compensate**, so their optima
drift too. This is why tuning only the learning rate in SP is not enough: SP also scales the
initialization and the output layer wrongly.

#### The formal vocabulary

Appendix A pins down the words used above.

- A **multiplier** is a fixed scalar $c$ inserted anywhere in the network ("multiply by $c$").
  A **parameter multiplier** replaces a weight $W$ by $cW$. The $1/\sqrt{d_\text{head}}$ in
  attention is a multiplier; it can also be read as a parameter multiplier on $W_q$, since
  $\langle k, q\rangle/\sqrt{d} = \langle k, (W_q/\sqrt d)\,x\rangle$. A parameter multiplier is
  *not* the same as a different initialization, because it also scales the gradient (we return
  to this in Lemma J.1). After training it can be folded into the weight.
- A **parametrization** is a rule for how to change HPs *when the width changes*, not a value
  for any particular width. An **abc-parametrization** scales, for each weight tensor, (a) its
  parameter multiplier, (b) its initialization and (c) its learning rate as powers of the width.
  "Initialize with variance $1/\text{fan\_in}$" is, in this sense, the rule "halve the
  variance when fan-in doubles".
- A parametrization admits **zero-shot transfer** of a set of HPs $\mathcal H$ with respect to a
  metric $\mathcal L$ if the optimal values of $\mathcal H$ converge as the width goes to
  infinity. The paper uses the training loss as $\mathcal L$.

## Hyperparameters don't transfer in the standard parametrization

The **standard parametrization** (SP) is what PyTorch does by default. For a two-hidden-layer
MLP with inputs $\xi \in \mathbb R^{d_\text{in}}$ and width $n$ it is (eq. 2 of the paper)

$$
f(\xi) = W^{3\top}\phi\big(W^{2\top}\phi(W^{1\top}\xi + b^1) + b^2\big), \quad
W^1 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac{1}{d_\text{in}}),\; W^{2}, W^3 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac1n),\; b^1 = b^2 = 0,
$$

with one learning rate $\eta$ for every parameter. The variance $1/\text{fan\_in}$ (LeCun
initialization) keeps every activation of order 1 *at initialization*. The trouble starts once
training begins.

We reran the paper's MLP experiment (its Fig. 3) on CIFAR-10: a two-hidden-layer ReLU MLP
trained with cross-entropy, widths 128 to 4096, sweeping the learning rate on a $\log_2$ grid.
To keep it to an hour on a laptop CPU we train for 1000 steps of batch 128 (2.6 epochs) instead
of 20 epochs, use Adam (the paper's Fig. 3 uses SGD), and report the
training loss on 10,000 training images, averaged over 2 seeds. These are our runs, scaled down from the paper's setup.<Sidenote>Inputs are standardized per pixel. μP uses base width $n_0 = 128$, so at width 128 the SP and μP networks are identical, which is why the width-128 curves coincide in the two charts. A missing point means at least one seed diverged (NaN loss).</Sidenote>

## μP in one table

#### The rules

μP changes three things relative to SP: the initialization of the output layer, the learning
rates of the input and output layers (and of all biases), and, in a Transformer, the attention
scale. Table 3 of the paper lists the rules by the role of each weight. Here $\text{fan\_in}$ and
$\text{fan\_out}$ are the input and output dimension of a weight, and a bias counts as an input
weight with $\text{fan\_in} = 1$. SP's value is in brackets where it differs.

| | Input weights and all biases | Output weights | Hidden weights |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Init. variance | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}^2$ $\;(1/\text{fan\_in})$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ |
| SGD learning rate | $\text{fan\_out}$ $\;(1)$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ $\;(1)$ | $1$ |
| Adam learning rate | $1$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ $\;(1)$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ $\;(1)$ |

The three columns are really three *shapes*. An **input weight** maps a finite dimension
(the 3072 pixels, the vocabulary) to a width; an **output weight** maps a width to a finite
dimension (10 classes, the vocabulary); a **hidden weight** maps a width to a width. Appendix B
calls parameters with two width dimensions **matrix-like**, those with one **vector-like** (input
and output weights, biases, layernorm gains) and those with none **scalar-like**. Only the scaling
with width matters: the table says "multiply the hidden Adam learning rate by $1/2$ when the
width doubles", not "use $1/1024$ at width 1024".

For the MLP above with SGD, μP is (eq. 3)

$$
\begin{aligned}
&W^1 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac{1}{d_\text{in}}),\quad W^2 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac1n),\quad
\boxed{W^3 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac{1}{n^2})},\\
&\boxed{\eta_{W^1} = \eta_{b^1} = \eta_{b^2} = \eta n},\quad \eta_{W^2} = \eta,\quad \boxed{\eta_{W^3} = \eta / n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In practice we insert constants so that μP *equals* SP at some **base width** $n_0$. With
$\tilde n = n / n_0$ (eq. 4):

$$
W^3 \sim \mathcal N(0, \tfrac{1}{n \tilde n}),\qquad \eta_{W^1} = \eta_{b^1} = \eta_{b^2} = \eta \tilde n,\quad \eta_{W^2} = \eta,\quad \eta_{W^3} = \eta/\tilde n.
$$

At $n = n_0$ every boxed factor is 1 and the network *is* the SP network. As $n$ grows, the two
diverge. The Adam version used in our experiment follows the third row of the table:

```python
nt = n / n0  # width multiplier
W1 = randn(n, d_in) / sqrt(d_in);  b1 = zeros(n); b2 = zeros(n)
W2 = randn(n, n) / sqrt(n)
W3 = randn(10, n) / sqrt(n * nt)          # SP: / sqrt(n)
opt = Adam([
    {"params": [W1, b1, b2], "lr": lr},   # input weights and biases: constant
    {"params": [W2, W3], "lr": lr / nt},  # hidden and output: 1/width
])
```

#### For a Transformer

Definition 4.1 adds one rule: the attention logit is $q^\top k / d$ instead of $q^\top k/\sqrt d$,
where $d = d_\text{head}$. We derive why below. Appendix B.1 walks through every Transformer
parameter: word and position embeddings and layernorm weights are input weights (their fan-in,
the vocabulary or context size or the constant 1, does not grow), $W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o$ and the MLP
matrices are hidden weights, and the unembedding is an output weight. To match a standard
Transformer at base head size $d_{\text{head},0}$, the logit is computed as

$$
\text{AttnLogit} = \alpha_\text{attn}\,\frac{\sqrt{d_{\text{head},0}}}{d_\text{head}}\, q^\top k,
$$

with a tunable multiplier $\alpha_\text{attn}$; the output logits get a tunable multiplier
$\alpha_\text{output}$ in the same way.

## Why SP breaks: a network with two weight vectors

#### One step of SGD

Section 5 of the paper uses the smallest network that shows the problem: one hidden layer, no
nonlinearity, scalar input and output,

$$
f(x) = V^\top U x, \qquad U, V \in \mathbb R^{n}.
$$

In SP, $U_\alpha \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $V_\alpha \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/n)$, so $f(x) = \Theta(|x|)$
at initialization: $V^\top U$ is a sum of $n$ independent zero-mean terms of variance $1/n$, so it
is roughly $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ by the CLT. Train with the loss $\tfrac12(f(x) - y)^2$. The gradients
are $\partial L/\partial V = \chi U x$ and $\partial L/\partial U = \chi V x$ with $\chi = f(x) - y$.
One SGD step with learning rate 1 gives

$$
V' = V + \theta U,\qquad U' = U + \theta V,\qquad \theta = -\chi x = \Theta(1).
$$

Multiply out the new output:

$$
f'(x) = V'^\top U' x = \big(V^\top U + \theta\, U^\top U + \theta\, V^\top V + \theta^2\, U^\top V\big)\,x.
$$

Now size each term. $U^\top U = \sum_\alpha U_\alpha^2$ is a sum of $n$ terms with mean 1, so by
the LLN it is $\approx n$. The other three are $\Theta(1)$. **So $f'$ grows linearly with the
width.** After one step, a wide enough SP network outputs garbage.

The culprit is the update $\theta U$ to $V$. It is *aligned* with $U$, the vector $V$ multiplies.
Before training, $V$ and $U$ are independent, so $V^\top U$ is a sum of random signs that
cancel (CLT, $\sqrt n$ growth, tamed by $V$'s $1/\sqrt n$ scale). After one step, part of $V$ is
a copy of $U$, so its products with $U$ no longer cancel (LLN, $n$ growth). This switch from CLT
to LLN is the whole story of μP.

#### The same step in μP

The table gives $V_\alpha \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/n^2)$ (output weight), $U_\alpha \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$,
and learning rates $\eta_V = 1/n$, $\eta_U = n$. One step gives $V' = V + \theta U/n$ and
$U' = U + \theta n V$, so

$$
f'(x) = \big(V^\top U + \theta n^{-1} U^\top U + \theta n V^\top V + \theta^2 U^\top V\big)\,x.
$$

Size each term again:

| Term | Size | Why |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| $V^\top U$ | $\Theta(n^{-1/2})$ | CLT: $n$ terms of variance $1/n^2$ |
| $\theta\, n^{-1} U^\top U$ | $\theta$ | LLN: $U^\top U \approx n$ |
| $\theta\, n V^\top V$ | $\theta$ | LLN: $V^\top V \approx n \cdot n^{-2}$ |
| $\theta^2 U^\top V$ | $\Theta(n^{-1/2})$ | CLT again |

So $f'(x) \to 2\theta x$: the output changes by an amount that does not depend on the width. The
initial output $V^\top U$ vanishes as $n \to \infty$, which is not a bug; we will see that every
feature-learning parametrization has this property (Corollary 3.10 below).

#### Some layers learn too fast, others too slow

Section 5 then shows the same imbalance in a Transformer trained with Adam (the paper's Fig. 5):
in SP, the output logits and the attention logits change by an amount that grows with width after
a single step, while the word embeddings change by a width-independent amount. If we shrink the
global learning rate to tame the logits, the embeddings stop learning. No single learning rate is
right for every layer. μP is designed so that every layer's activations move at the same,
width-independent speed.

## The mathematics behind μP

Appendix J derives the table from three ingredients: a way to talk about the size of a vector,
the sizes of $Av$ for the matrices that appear in training, and three desiderata. The arguments
are heuristic; Tensor Programs IV makes them rigorous for SGD, and the paper defers the Adam
version to a later paper.

#### Sizes of vectors

During training, each activation vector of a wide network has coordinates that look like
independent samples from one distribution (this is a theorem of the Tensor Programs series).
So we can describe a whole vector by the typical size of one coordinate.

**Definition J.1.** A vector $v \in \mathbb R^n$ has **$\Theta(n^a)$-coordinates** if
$\|v\|^2/n = \Theta(n^{2a})$ as $n \to \infty$. Similarly with $O$ and $\Omega$.

So $\Theta(1)$-coordinates means each entry stays the same size as the network widens. For such
a vector we can attach a random variable $Z^v$ (independent of $n$) that has the distribution of
one coordinate. If $u$ and $v$ are correlated, so are $Z^u$ and $Z^v$, and

$$
\frac{u^\top v}{n} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb E\,[Z^u Z^v].
$$

The rule of thumb behind every calculation: a sum of $n$ terms that look like samples of $X$ has
size

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\Theta(n) & \text{if } \mathbb E X \ne 0 \quad \text{(LLN)}\\
\Theta(\sqrt n) & \text{if } \mathbb E X = 0 \quad \text{(CLT)}.
\end{cases}
$$

### Three kinds of matrices

The question of Appendix J.1 is: if $v$ has $\Theta(1)$-coordinates, how large are the
coordinates of $Av$? The answer depends on what kind of matrix $A$ is, and on whether $v$ is
correlated with it.

**A Gaussian matrix** (a weight at initialization). Let $A_{\alpha\beta} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$.
If $v$ is independent of $A$, then $(Av)_\alpha = \sum_\beta A_{\alpha\beta} v_\beta$ is a sum of
$n$ zero-mean terms, so it is $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ by the CLT. To get $\Theta(1)$-coordinates we need
entries of size $1/\sqrt n$: variance $1/n$, the usual $1/\text{fan\_in}$.

The subtle case is when $v$ depends on $A$, which happens as soon as gradients flow back through
$A^\top$. The paper's example takes $v = A^\top \mathbf 1$ with $A_{\alpha\beta} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/n)$:

$$
(AA^\top \mathbf 1)_\alpha = \sum_{\beta,\gamma} A_{\alpha\beta}A_{\gamma\beta}
= \underbrace{\sum_\beta A_{\alpha\beta}^2}_{\text{LLN:}\;\approx 1}
+ \underbrace{\sum_\beta \sum_{\gamma \ne \alpha} A_{\alpha\beta}A_{\gamma\beta}}_{\text{CLT:}\;\approx \mathcal N(0, 1)}.
$$

The first sum has $n$ terms of mean $1/n$. The second has $n$ independent inner sums, each with
variance $(n-1)/n^2$, so their total has variance $\approx 1$. Every coordinate looks like
$\mathcal N(1, 1)$: still $\Theta(1)$. The correlation added a deterministic mean but did not
change the scaling. (Our simulation at $n = 4000$: mean $1.02$, variance $0.96$.) In general,
for a Gaussian $A$, entries of size $1/\sqrt n$ are the unique scaling that keeps $Av$ at
$\Theta(1)$, correlated or not; the proof of the general case is in Tensor Programs III.

**A tensor product matrix** (an SGD update). A gradient step on a weight $W$ with input $x$ and
backpropagated gradient $u$ adds the outer product $u x^\top$, and a batch adds a sum of them.
Take

$$
A = \frac{1}{n}\, u v^\top = \frac{u \otimes v}{n}.
$$

Then $Ax = u\,\frac{v^\top x}{n}$, and when the next input $x$ is correlated with $v$ (it usually
is: $x$ and $v$ are both activations of the same layer), $v^\top x/n \to \mathbb E[Z^v Z^x] \ne 0$
by the LLN. So $Ax \approx c\, u$ with $c = \mathbb E[Z^v Z^x]$, which has $\Theta(1)$-coordinates
distributed like $Z^u\,\mathbb E[Z^vZ^x]$. **Entries of size $1/n$, not $1/\sqrt n$, are right
for an update**, because the sum is LLN-like. For a sum of $k$ outer products,
$Z^{Ax} = \sum_i Z^{u_i}\,\mathbb E[Z^{v_i} Z^x]$.<Sidenote>Footnote 29 of the paper: if $x$ happens to be uncorrelated with $v$, then $v^\top x = \Theta(\sqrt n)$ and $Ax$ is only $\Theta(1/\sqrt n)$. This corner case doesn't come up in training.</Sidenote>

**A nonlinear tensor product matrix** (an Adam update). Adam divides the gradient by the root of a
running average of its square, coordinatewise. After $k$ steps with outer-product gradients
$u^i \otimes v^i$, the update has entries

$$
A_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_i\, u^i_\alpha v^i_\beta}{\sqrt{\sum_i \omega_i\,(u^i_\alpha v^i_\beta)^2}}
=: \psi(u^1_\alpha, \dots, u^k_\alpha, v^1_\beta, \dots, v^k_\beta),
$$

where $\gamma_i, \omega_i$ are the moving-average weights (ignoring $\epsilon$ and bias correction).
A matrix whose $(\alpha, \beta)$ entry is a fixed function of $\alpha$-indexed and $\beta$-indexed
vectors is a **nonlinear tensor product matrix**; the SGD case is $\psi = n^{-1}\sum_i u^i_\alpha v^i_\beta$.
Note that the Adam entries are $\Theta(1)$ whatever the gradient's size: the normalization removes
the scale. Suppose $\psi = n^{-1}\bar\psi$ with $\bar\psi$ independent of $n$ (a learning rate
$1/n$). Then by the LLN

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Ax)_\alpha &= \frac1n \sum_{\beta=1}^n \bar\psi(u^1_\alpha, \dots, u^k_\alpha, v^1_\beta, \dots, v^k_\beta)\, x_\beta \\
&\approx \mathbb E\big[\bar\psi(u^1_\alpha, \dots, u^k_\alpha, Z^{v^1}, \dots, Z^{v^k})\, Z^x\big]
=: \Psi(u^1_\alpha, \dots, u^k_\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$

So $Ax$ has $\Theta(1)$-coordinates distributed as $Z^{Ax} = \Psi(Z^{u^1}, \dots, Z^{u^k})$. With
SGD's $\bar\psi = u_\alpha v_\beta$ this gives $\Psi(z) = z\,\mathbb E[Z^v Z^x]$, the previous
result. Again entries $\Theta(1/n)$ are the unique right size, so **Adam's learning rate for a
hidden weight must scale like $1/n$**.

**A vector** (the readout layer). If $A \in \mathbb R^{1 \times n}$ is correlated with $v$, then
$Av$ is one LLN sum, $\Theta(n)$. Entries must be $\Theta(1/n)$.

Table 14 of the paper summarizes the three cases for entries of size $\Theta(1)$:

| $A$ | Gaussian $n \times n$ | (nonlinear) tensor product $n \times n$ | vector $1 \times n$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Entry size of $Av$ | $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ | $\Theta(n)$ |

We checked each case numerically, with $\Theta(1)$ entries and $v$ correlated with $A$ where it
matters: a Gaussian $A$ with independent $v$ and with $v = \text{relu}(A^\top \mathbf 1/\sqrt n)$;
a tensor product $u w^\top$ with $x = \tanh(w) + w/2$; the one-step Adam update
$\text{sign}(u_\alpha w_\beta)$ with $x = w$; and the vector $w^\top$ with the same $x$.

#### The desiderata

Appendix J.2 asks three things of a parametrization, at any fixed time during training as
$n \to \infty$:

1. **Every (pre)activation vector has $\Theta(1)$-coordinates.** If they grew or shrank with $n$,
   a wide network would leave the floating-point range (acute in fp16 and bf16), and a nonlinearity
   such as tanh would be either linear ($o(1)$ inputs) or saturated ($\omega(1)$ inputs).
2. **The output is $O(1)$**, for the same reasons applied to the loss. Combined with 3, it is
   $\Theta(1)$ after training, though it may vanish at initialization.
3. **Every parameter is updated as much as possible without causing divergence.** This is
   **maximal feature learning**: each layer contributes to the limit. It also guarantees that each
   layer's learning rate *matters* in the limit, so that it plays the same role at width 256 and at
   width 8192. A layer frozen at initialization in the limit would have a learning rate that does
   nothing at infinite width but something at finite width, and its optimum could not transfer.

### Deriving the table, layer by layer

Take every hidden width equal to $n$. Desideratum 1 says the input $x$ to each layer has
$\Theta(1)$-coordinates. We need two facts about gradients first.

*The backward signal has $\Theta(1/n)$-coordinates.* The gradient of the output with respect to
the last hidden activation is $\partial f/\partial x^L = (W^{L+1})^\top$, whose entries we are
about to make $\Theta(1/n)$. Backpropagating through a hidden layer multiplies by
$(W^l)^\top$, a Gaussian matrix with $1/\sqrt n$ entries (plus an update), which preserves
coordinate size, and by $\phi'$, which is $\Theta(1)$. So $\partial f/\partial h^l$ has
$\Theta(1/n)$-coordinates for every layer.

*A weight's gradient is an outer product.* For $h = Wx$, $\nabla_W f = (\partial f/\partial h)\,x^\top$.

Now each column of the table:

**Output weights** $W \in \mathbb R^{1 \times n}$ (or $d_\text{out} \times n$). The output $Wx$ is a
vector case: after training $x$ is correlated with $W$, so we need $W$ to have $\Theta(1/n)$
entries, both at initialization and in its updates. Initialization: variance $\Theta(1/n^2)$.
The gradient is $\chi\,x^\top$ with $\Theta(1)$ entries, so the SGD learning rate is $\Theta(1/n)$.
Adam's normalized update has $\Theta(1)$ entries, so Adam's learning rate is also $\Theta(1/n)$.

**Hidden weights** $W \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$. Initialization is a Gaussian matrix:
entries $\Theta(1/\sqrt n)$, variance $1/n$. The update is a (nonlinear) tensor product and needs
$\Theta(1/n)$ entries, so that $(W_0 + \Delta W)x$ stays $\Theta(1)$. For SGD the gradient
$(\partial f/\partial h)x^\top$ already has $\Theta(1/n)\cdot\Theta(1) = \Theta(1/n)$ entries, so the
learning rate is $\Theta(1)$. For Adam the update has $\Theta(1)$ entries, so the learning rate is
$\Theta(1/n)$.

**Input weights** $W \in \mathbb R^{n \times d}$, $d$ fixed. Here $W\xi$ sums over the *finite*
dimension $d$, so there is no LLN or CLT growth and $W$ itself needs $\Theta(1)$ entries:
initialization variance $\Theta(1)$, which is $1/\text{fan\_in}$ because $\text{fan\_in} = d$ is a
constant. The update also needs $\Theta(1)$ entries. The gradient $(\partial f/\partial h)\xi^\top$ has
$\Theta(1/n)$ entries, so SGD needs learning rate $\Theta(n)$; Adam's update is already $\Theta(1)$,
so Adam's learning rate is $\Theta(1)$.

**Biases** are input weights whose input is the constant 1, and follow the same column.

This reproduces every entry of the table, and explains the differences from SP. SP initializes
the output layer at $1/\sqrt n$ (CLT thinking, right only at initialization), and gives every layer
the same learning rate, which is $n$ times too large for Adam's hidden and output weights and,
for SGD, $n$ times too small for the input layer and $n$ times too large for the output layer.

#### Attention: $1/d$ instead of $1/\sqrt d$

The attention logit $q^\top k$ is a sum over $d = d_\text{head}$ coordinates. At initialization
$q = W_q x$ and $k = W_k x$ are nearly independent with zero mean, so $q^\top k = \Theta(\sqrt d)$ by
the CLT, which is why the original Transformer divides by $\sqrt d$. But training makes $q$ and $k$
correlated: that is what it means for a head to learn to attend. Then $q^\top k = \Theta(d)$ by the
LLN, and $q^\top k/\sqrt d$ grows like $\sqrt d$. Dividing by $d$ keeps the trained logit
$\Theta(1)$ (this is also the "infinite dimension to finite dimension" rule for output weights,
since an attention logit maps $\mathbb R^{d}$ to a scalar). Fig. 5 of the paper shows the attention
logits of an SP Transformer blowing up with width after one Adam step, and staying flat in μP.

The same argument covers growing the *number* of heads with $d_\text{head}$ fixed: viewing keys,
queries and values across all heads, attention acts coordinatewise along the head dimension, like a
nonlinearity, so the rules above apply unchanged.

### Lemma J.1: one network, three tables

The paper gives three equivalent tables: Table 3 above, Table 8 (used by the `mup` package) and
Table 9 (the style of Tensor Programs IV). They are equivalent because of a symmetry.

**Lemma J.1.** Consider a weight tensor $W$ that enters the network as $A\,W$ (multiplier $A$), is
initialized as $W \sim \mathcal N(0, B^2)$, and is trained with learning rate $C$. Let $f_t(\xi)$ be
the network's output after $t$ steps on any fixed sequence of batches. For every $\theta > 0$,
$f_t(\xi)$ is unchanged for all $t$ and $\xi$ if we replace

- with SGD: $A \to A\theta,\; B \to B/\theta,\; C \to C/\theta^2$;
- with Adam: $A \to A\theta,\; B \to B/\theta,\; C \to C/\theta$.

*Proof.* The network only ever sees the **effective weight** $\widetilde W = A W$. It suffices to
show that the trajectory of $\widetilde W$ is unchanged.

At initialization $\widetilde W_0 = A W_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, A^2 B^2)$, which depends only on the
product $AB$, and $AB$ is invariant: $(A\theta)(B/\theta) = AB$. (Using the same random draw,
$\widetilde W_0$ is literally the same matrix.)

By the chain rule the gradient with respect to $W$ is $A$ times the gradient with respect to
$\widetilde W$: writing $g_t = \nabla_{\widetilde W} L$, we have $\nabla_W L = A\,g_t$.

*SGD.* $W_{t+1} = W_t - C A g_t$, so $\widetilde W_{t+1} = \widetilde W_t - C A^2 g_t$. The update
depends only on $CA^2$, and $(C/\theta^2)(A\theta)^2 = CA^2$.

*Adam.* The update is $W_{t+1} = W_t - C\, m_t/\sqrt{v_t}$, where $m_t$ and $v_t$ are moving averages
of $\nabla_W L = A g_t$ and of its square. Scaling every gradient by $A > 0$ scales $m_t$ by $A$ and
$\sqrt{v_t}$ by $A$, so $m_t/\sqrt{v_t}$ is the same as for the gradients $g_t$; call it $u_t$. Then
$\widetilde W_{t+1} = \widetilde W_t - C A\, u_t$, which depends only on $CA$, and
$(C/\theta)(A\theta) = CA$.

In both cases $\widetilde W_0$ and every update of $\widetilde W$ are the same, and since $g_t$
depends only on the effective weights, induction on $t$ shows the whole trajectory, and so
$f_t$, is the same. $\blacksquare$

Two remarks. The Adam case assumes $\epsilon$ is negligible; if not, $\epsilon$ must be scaled
by $\theta$ too, since it is added to the gradient's scale. And the Lemma is why a parameter
multiplier is not the same as an initialization: changing only $B$ (or only $A$) changes the
trajectory.

We checked the Lemma on a 3-input, width-64 ReLU network: scaling the hidden weight's
$(A, B, C)$ with $\theta = 0.1$ and $\theta = 8$ changed the outputs over 20 steps by at most
$2 \times 10^{-16}$ (SGD) and $2 \times 10^{-15}$ (Adam with $\epsilon \to \epsilon\theta$), in
float64.

*Deriving Table 8 from Table 3.* For an output weight, Table 3 has multiplier $A = 1$, init standard
deviation $B = 1/\text{fan\_in}$, learning rate $C = \eta/\text{fan\_in}$. Apply the Lemma with
$\theta = 1/\text{fan\_in}$: $A = 1/\text{fan\_in}$, $B = 1$, and $C = \eta\,\text{fan\_in}$ for SGD,
$C = \eta$ for Adam. With $\theta = 1/\sqrt{\text{fan\_in}}$ instead: $A = 1/\sqrt{\text{fan\_in}}$,
$B^2 = 1/\text{fan\_in}$, $C = \eta$ for SGD and $\eta/\sqrt{\text{fan\_in}}$ for Adam, which is
Table 9. For an input weight (Table 3: $A = 1$, $B^2 = 1/\text{fan\_in}$ a constant, SGD
$C = \eta\,\text{fan\_out}$, Adam $C = \eta$), $\theta = \sqrt{\text{fan\_out}}$ gives Table 9's
multiplier $\sqrt{\text{fan\_out}}$, variance $1/\text{fan\_out}$, SGD learning rate $\eta$ and Adam
learning rate $\eta/\sqrt{\text{fan\_out}}$. The resulting tables:

| Table 8 (`mup` package) | Input weights and biases | Output weights | Hidden weights |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Init. variance | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ | $1$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ |
| Multiplier | $1$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ | $1$ |
| SGD LR | $\text{fan\_out}$ | $\text{fan\_in}$ | $1$ |
| Adam LR | $1$ | $1$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ |

| Table 9 (Tensor Programs IV style) | Input weights and biases | Output weights | Hidden weights |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Init. variance | $1/\text{fan\_out}$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ |
| Multiplier | $\sqrt{\text{fan\_out}}$ | $1/\sqrt{\text{fan\_in}}$ | $1$ |
| SGD LR | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| Adam LR | $1/\sqrt{\text{fan\_out}}$ | $1/\sqrt{\text{fan\_in}}$ | $1/\text{fan\_in}$ |

Table 8 has a practical advantage: every vector-like parameter (input weights, output weights,
biases) gets the *same* init and Adam learning rate, so the input embedding and the output
unembedding can be **tied** (share one matrix), as most language models do. Its output multiplier
$1/\text{fan\_in}$ reads as a general rule: any weight that maps an infinite dimension to a finite
one gets a $1/\text{fan\_in}$ multiplier, which covers both the output logits and the $1/d$ attention.

#### Different widths in different layers

So far all hidden widths were $n$. In a Transformer, $d_\text{model}$, $d_\text{ffn}$, $d_\text{head}$
and $n_\text{head}$ can all play the role of width (Appendix E.2). The rule is to replace $n$ by
**fan-in** for output and hidden weights (this preserves the forward pass) and by **fan-out** for
input weights. With Adam, if every layer's width goes to infinity, this keeps the coordinate
distribution of every activation the same whatever the ratios between widths, so one can tune with
$d_\text{ffn} = d_\text{model}$ and transfer to $d_\text{ffn} = 4 d_\text{model}$ (the paper's Fig. 12
varies $d_\text{ffn}/d_\text{model}$ from 0.5 to 16 and the best learning rate does not move). With
SGD the ratios change gradient sizes layer by layer, so the scaling is more involved. A convolution
with kernel $s_1 \times s_2$ is just $s_1 s_2 = \Theta(1)$ dense weights of shape
$\text{fan\_out} \times \text{fan\_in}$, and follows the same rules.

## The infinite-width theory: Tensor Programs IV

The paper leans on its predecessor, *Feature Learning in Infinite-Width Neural Networks* (Yang and
Hu, 2021, "Tensor Programs IV"), for the claim that μP is *the* right parametrization. Appendix J.3
uses its main theorem. Here is that theory for an MLP trained with SGD.

### abc-parametrizations

Take an MLP with $L$ hidden layers of width $n$:

$$
h^1 = W^1\xi,\quad x^l = \phi(h^l),\quad h^{l+1} = W^{l+1}x^l\;(l < L),\quad f(\xi) = W^{L+1}x^L.
$$

An **abc-parametrization** is a set of numbers $\{a_l, b_l\}_{l=1}^{L+1} \cup \{c\}$ such that

$$
W^l = n^{-a_l}\, w^l,\qquad w^l_{\alpha\beta} \sim \mathcal N(0, n^{-2b_l}),\qquad
\text{and SGD trains } w^l \text{ with learning rate } \eta\, n^{-c}.
$$

So $a_l$ is the parameter multiplier, $b_l$ the initialization and $c$ the learning rate, all as
powers of the width. The effective weight $W^l$ has initial variance $n^{-2(a_l + b_l)}$ and, since
its gradient with respect to $w^l$ carries a factor $n^{-a_l}$, its effective SGD learning rate is
$\eta\, n^{-(2a_l + c)}$. Some named examples (Table 1 of Tensor Programs IV):

| | $a_l$ | $b_l$ | $c$ |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| SP | $0$ | $0$ for $l = 1$, $\tfrac12$ otherwise | $0$ or $1$ |
| NTK parametrization (NTP) | $0$ for $l = 1$, $\tfrac12$ otherwise | $0$ | $0$ |
| Mean field ($L = 1$) | $0$, then $1$ | $0$ | $-1$ |
| μP | $-\tfrac12$, then $0$, then $\tfrac12$ (input, hidden, output) | $\tfrac12$ | $0$ |

For μP the effective init variances are $n^0, n^{-1}, n^{-2}$ and the effective SGD learning rates
$n^{1}, n^{0}, n^{-1}$ for input, hidden and output weights: exactly Table 3.

**The symmetry.** Shifting $a_l \to a_l + \theta$, $b_l \to b_l - \theta$ for every layer and
$c \to c - 2\theta$ changes nothing (eq. 5 of Tensor Programs IV). This is Lemma J.1 for SGD with
$A = n^{-a}$, $B = n^{-b}$, $C = n^{-c}$. So abc-parametrizations really form equivalence classes,
and below only quantities invariant under the shift appear: $a_l + b_l$, $2a_l + c$, and $r$.

### Stability: Theorem 3.3

A parametrization is **stable** if, as $n \to \infty$, the activations $h^l, x^l$ have
$\Theta(1)$-coordinates at initialization, and all activations and the output stay $O(1)$
throughout training. Define

$$
r := \min\big(a_{L+1} + b_{L+1},\; 2a_{L+1} + c\big) + c - 1 + \min_{l=1}^{L}\big[2a_l + \mathbb 1(l = 1)\big].
$$

Intuitively, $\Delta x^L_t$, the change in the last layer's features, has
$\Theta(n^{-r})$-coordinates.

**Theorem 3.3 (stability).** An abc-parametrization is stable if and only if

1. $a_1 + b_1 = 0$; $a_l + b_l = \tfrac12$ for $2 \le l \le L$; $a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} \ge \tfrac12$
   (activations $\Theta(1)$ and output $O(1)$ at initialization);
2. $r \ge 0$ (features don't blow up during training);
3. $2a_{L+1} + c \ge 1$ and $a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} + r \ge 1$ (the output doesn't blow up during training).

Each condition is one of the size calculations we have already done.

*Condition 1.* $h^1 = W^1\xi$ is a finite sum, so its coordinates have the size of $W^1$'s entries,
$n^{-(a_1+b_1)}$; this must be $n^0$. For $l \ge 2$, $h^l = W^l x^{l-1}$ is a CLT sum of $n$ terms
of size $n^{-(a_l + b_l)}$, giving $n^{1/2 - (a_l + b_l)}$; this must be $n^0$. The output is the
same CLT sum but only needs to be $O(1)$, hence $\ge$.

*Condition 2 (where $r$ comes from).* Consider one SGD step on a hidden layer $l$. The chain rule
gives $\nabla_{w^l} f = n^{-a_l}\,\partial f/\partial h^l\, x^\top$, so the effective weight changes by
$\delta W^l = -\eta\, n^{-c}\, n^{-2a_l}\,\chi\, (\partial f/\partial h^l)\, x^\top$. On the next forward
pass it contributes

$$
\delta W^l\,\bar x = -\eta\, n^{1 - c - 2a_l}\,\chi\,\frac{x^\top \bar x}{n}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial h^l},
$$

where $x^\top\bar x/n \to$ a nonzero constant by the LLN ($x$ and the new input $\bar x$ are
correlated). The backward signal $\partial f/\partial h^l$ has the size of the last layer's weights,
which is $n^{-(a_{L+1}+b_{L+1})}$ from initialization plus $n^{-(2a_{L+1}+c)}$ from its updates. So
$\delta W^l \bar x$ has coordinates of size $n^{-r_l}$ with

$$
r_l = \min\big(a_{L+1} + b_{L+1},\; 2a_{L+1} + c\big) + c - 1 + 2a_l.
$$

For $l = 1$ the input is $\xi$ and $\xi^\top\bar\xi$ is a finite sum with no factor $n$, which adds
$+1$: the $\mathbb 1(l = 1)$. A change in layer $l$ propagates forward, so the last layer's features
change by the largest contribution, $n^{-\min_l r_l} = n^{-r}$. Stability needs $r \ge 0$.

*Condition 3.* The output changes by $\Delta W^{L+1} x^L + W^{L+1}_0 \Delta x^L$ (plus a smaller cross
term). The first: $\Delta W^{L+1} = -\eta n^{-c-2a_{L+1}}\chi\, x^\top$, so
$\Delta W^{L+1}x^L = -\eta n^{-c-2a_{L+1}}\chi\, x^\top x$, an LLN sum of size
$n^{1 - (2a_{L+1} + c)}$. The second: $W_0^{L+1}$ has entries $n^{-(a_{L+1}+b_{L+1})}$, $\Delta x^L$
has entries $n^{-r}$, and they are correlated (the update of the features is driven by
$W_0^{L+1}$ through backpropagation), so this is also an LLN sum, of size
$n^{1 - (a_{L+1}+b_{L+1}) - r}$. Both must be $O(1)$. $\blacksquare$ (heuristically; the rigorous proof
is Theorem H.6 of Tensor Programs IV.)

Condition 3 is our two-weight example in general form. SP with a constant learning rate has
$2a_{L+1} + c = 0$, so the output change grows like $n^{1}$, the slope $1.02$ we measured.

### Nontrivial, feature learning or kernel: the Dynamical Dichotomy

A stable parametrization can still be useless: the output might not move at all in the limit. It
is **nontrivial** if it does.

**Theorem 3.4.** A stable abc-parametrization is nontrivial if and only if
$a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} + r = 1$ or $2a_{L+1} + c = 1$.

That is, one of the two terms of condition 3 must sit exactly at $\Theta(1)$.

A parametrization **admits feature learning** if $\Delta x^L_t(\xi)$ has $\Omega(1)$-coordinates for
some training routine, time $t \ge 1$ and input $\xi$: the representation itself changes. It is in
the **kernel regime** if there is a fixed kernel $K$ such that in the limit, for every training
routine,

$$
f_{t+1}(\xi) = f_t(\xi) - \eta\, K(\xi, \xi_t)\, \mathcal L'(f_t(\xi_t), y_t),
$$

that is, training reduces to kernel gradient descent with a kernel fixed at initialization (the
**neural tangent kernel**, NTK, in the case of NTP).

**Theorem 3.6.** A nontrivial stable abc-parametrization admits feature learning iff $r = 0$
(iff it evolves the feature kernel $x^\top x'/n$).

**Theorem 3.8.** A nontrivial stable abc-parametrization is in the kernel regime iff $r > 0$.

Since stability forces $r \ge 0$, one of the two always holds, and they cannot both hold:

**Corollary 3.9 (Dynamical Dichotomy).** A nontrivial stable abc-parametrization either admits
feature learning or is in the kernel regime, but not both.

This is not obvious, because "kernel regime" is not *defined* as "no feature learning". The
intuition: if $r > 0$ the features move by $n^{-r} \to 0$, so the output is a first-order Taylor
expansion around initialization, linear in the gradient, which is kernel gradient descent. If
$r = 0$ the features move by $\Theta(1)$ and pass through a nonlinearity, so the change of $f$ is no
longer linear in the loss derivative and no kernel can describe it.<Sidenote>The theorems assume $\phi$ is tanh or a smooth approximation of relu (Assumption 3.1). For a linear network the dichotomy fails: a one-hidden-layer linear net where only the first layer is trained both learns features and is in the kernel regime.</Sidenote>

**Corollary 3.10.** In any nontrivial stable feature-learning parametrization, $f_0(\xi) \to 0$.
*Proof.* Feature learning means $r = 0$. Stability condition 3 then gives
$a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} \ge 1$, so the CLT size of the initial output is
$n^{1/2 - (a_{L+1} + b_{L+1})} \le n^{-1/2} \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

This is the vanishing $V^\top U$ we saw in the two-weight example, and the reason for Appendix
D.2's advice to initialize the output layer at zero: the limit has $f_0 = 0$, so a small proxy
model gets closer to the limit if its $f_0$ is exactly 0.

### Why μP is "maximal": Theorem 5.6

**Proposition 5.3.** In a stable parametrization, hidden or input layer $l$ is **updated
maximally** ($\Delta W^l_t x^{l-1}_t$ has $\Theta(1)$-coordinates) iff $r_l = 0$.

**Proposition 5.5.** The output layer is updated maximally ($\Delta W^{L+1}_t x^L_t = \Theta(1)$)
iff $2a_{L+1} + c = 1$, and initialized maximally ($W^{L+1}_0\Delta x^L_t = \Theta(1)$) iff
$a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} + r = 1$.

**Theorem 5.6.** In μP every $W^l$ is updated maximally and $W^{L+1}$ is also initialized
maximally. μP is the unique stable abc-parametrization with this property.

*Proof of uniqueness* (up to the symmetry). Use the symmetry to set $c = 0$. Output updated
maximally: $2a_{L+1} = 1$, so $a_{L+1} = \tfrac12$. All $r_l = 0$ means $r = 0$, and output
initialized maximally means $a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} = 1$, so $b_{L+1} = \tfrac12$. Then the min in $r_l$
is $\min(1, 1) = 1$ and $r_l = 1 + 0 - 1 + 2a_l + \mathbb 1(l=1) = 2a_l + \mathbb 1(l=1)$. Setting
$r_l = 0$ gives $a_1 = -\tfrac12$ and $a_l = 0$ for $2 \le l \le L$. Stability condition 1 then fixes
$b_1 = \tfrac12$ and $b_l = \tfrac12$. That is μP. Conversely, plugging these values in gives
$r_l = 0$ for all $l$, $2a_{L+1} + c = 1$ and $a_{L+1} + b_{L+1} + r = 1$. $\blacksquare$

### What the infinite-width limit looks like

The theory doesn't just classify; it computes the limit. For a one-hidden-layer μP network
$f(\xi) = V\phi(U\xi)$ (Section 6.1 of Tensor Programs IV) the initial coordinates of $U$ and of
$nV$ are independent standard Gaussians $Z^{U_0}, Z^{nV_0}$. Each SGD step updates *every*
coordinate by the same deterministic formula applied to that coordinate's own random variables,
so the whole width-$n$ network becomes, in the limit, a pair of random variables evolving in
time:

$$
\mathring f_t(\xi) = \mathbb E\big[Z^{nV_t}\, \phi(\xi Z^{U_t})\big],\qquad
\mathring\chi_t = \mathcal L'(\mathring f_t(\xi_t), y_t),
$$

$$
Z^{nV_{t+1}} = Z^{nV_t} - \eta\mathring\chi_t\,\phi(\xi_t Z^{U_t}),\qquad
Z^{U_{t+1}} = Z^{U_t} - \eta\mathring\chi_t\,\xi_t\, Z^{nV_t}\phi'(\xi_t Z^{U_t}).
$$

This is **Theorem 6.1**: $f_t(\xi) \to \mathring f_t(\xi)$ almost surely. The expectation comes from
the LLN in $f = \frac1n\sum_\alpha (nV)_\alpha x_\alpha$, and the loss derivative becomes deterministic
because $f$ does. Note $\mathring f_0 = \mathbb E[Z^{nV_0}]\,\mathbb E[\phi(\xi Z^{U_0})] = 0$, as Corollary 3.10
requires.

For a linear network ($\phi = \text{id}$) the random variables stay linear combinations of the
two initial Gaussians: $Z^{nV_t} = A_t Z^{nV_0} + B_t Z^{U_0}$ and $Z^{U_t} = C_t Z^{nV_0} + D_t Z^{U_0}$.
Since $\mathbb E[Z^{nV_0}Z^{U_0}] = 0$ and both have variance 1, the whole infinite network reduces to
four numbers (**Corollary 6.2**):

$$
\mathring f_t(\xi) = (A_tC_t + B_tD_t)\,\xi,\quad
(A,B)_{t+1} = (A,B)_t - \eta\mathring\chi_t\xi_t\,(C,D)_t,\quad
(C,D)_{t+1} = (C,D)_t - \eta\mathring\chi_t\xi_t\,(A,B)_t,
$$

starting from $A_0 = D_0 = 1$, $B_0 = C_0 = 0$.

A worked example. Train on alternating examples $(\xi, y) = (1, 2)$ and $(-1, -1)$ (no linear
function fits both) with $\eta = 0.1$ and squared loss, so $\mathring\chi_t = \mathring f_t(\xi_t) - y_t$. At
$t = 0$: $\mathring f_0 = 0$, $\mathring\chi_0 = 0 - 2 = -2$, so $\eta\mathring\chi_0\xi_0 = -0.2$ and
$(A, B)_1 = (1, 0) + 0.2\,(0, 1) = (1, 0.2)$, $(C, D)_1 = (0, 1) + 0.2\,(1, 0) = (0.2, 1)$. Then
$\mathring f_1(1) = 1 \cdot 0.2 + 0.2 \cdot 1 = 0.4$. The chart compares the limit with actual finite
networks in μP (one random draw each, SGD with $\eta_U = \eta n$, $\eta_V = \eta/n$), evaluated at
$\xi = 1$:

### The rigorous machinery: Tensor Programs and the Master Theorem

Every heuristic so far ("coordinates look iid", "replace $u^\top v/n$ by $\mathbb E[Z^uZ^v]$") is made
rigorous by one theorem. Section 7 of Tensor Programs IV observes that the computation of SGD
training, forward and backward passes included, uses only three instructions on width-$n$ vectors:

- **MatMul**: multiply a vector by a Gaussian matrix $W$ or by its transpose, $Wx$ or $W^\top x$;
- **Nonlin**: apply a function coordinatewise, $\phi(x^1, \dots, x^k; \theta_1, \dots, \theta_l)$, possibly
  using earlier scalars $\theta_i$ (such as the loss derivative $\chi_t$);
- **Moment**: average over coordinates to get a scalar, $\frac1n\sum_\alpha \phi(x^1_\alpha, \dots, x^k_\alpha)$
  (such as the output $f = \frac1n\sum_\alpha (nV)_\alpha x_\alpha$).

A sequence of such instructions is a **Tensor Program**. Each weight matrix starts with
$W_{\alpha\beta} \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_W^2/n)$ and each initial vector with iid coordinates. The
framework then assigns to every vector $h$ a random variable $Z^h$ and to every scalar $\theta$ a
number $\mathring\theta$, by rules that mirror the instructions:

- **ZNonlin**: $Z^{\phi(x^1, \dots, x^k; \theta)} = \phi(Z^{x^1}, \dots, Z^{x^k}; \mathring\theta)$.
- **ZMoment**: $\mathring\theta = \mathbb E\, \phi(Z^{x^1}, \dots, Z^{x^k}; \mathring\theta_1, \dots)$.
- **ZMatMul**: $Z^{Wx} = \hat Z^{Wx} + \dot Z^{Wx}$, a Gaussian part plus a correction.
  - $\hat Z^{Wx}$ is a zero-mean Gaussian, jointly Gaussian over all vectors multiplied by the
    same $W$, with $\text{Cov}(\hat Z^{Wx}, \hat Z^{Wy}) = \sigma_W^2\,\mathbb E[Z^xZ^y]$. This is the CLT.
  - $\dot Z^{Wx}$ accounts for $x$ having been computed using $W^\top$. Write $Z^x$ as a function
    of the Gaussians $\hat Z^{W^\top y_i}$ it was built from; then
    $\dot Z^{Wx} = \sigma_W^2 \sum_i Z^{y_i}\, \mathbb E\big[\partial Z^x / \partial \hat Z^{W^\top y_i}\big]$.
    This is the correlation effect.

**Theorem 7.4 (Master Theorem).** Under mild regularity (pseudo-Lipschitz nonlinearities), for any
vectors $h^1, \dots, h^k$ in the program and any pseudo-Lipschitz $\psi$,

$$
\frac1n \sum_{\alpha=1}^n \psi(h^1_\alpha, \dots, h^k_\alpha) \;\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\; \mathbb E\,\psi(Z^{h^1}, \dots, Z^{h^k}),
$$

and every scalar $\theta$ converges almost surely to $\mathring\theta$.

In words: the coordinates of all vectors in the program behave like iid copies of the
$Z$'s, and all scalars, including the network output and the loss, become deterministic.
Theorem 6.1 above is this theorem applied to the program of a one-hidden-layer network.

The ZMatMul rule explains the $AA^\top\mathbf 1$ example of Appendix J exactly. With $\sigma_A^2 = 1$,
let $x = A^\top\mathbf 1$; then $Z^x = \hat Z^{A^\top \mathbf 1}$ is a standard Gaussian (its variance is
$\mathbb E[(Z^{\mathbf 1})^2] = 1$). For $Ax$: the Gaussian part $\hat Z^{Ax}$ has variance
$\mathbb E[(Z^x)^2] = 1$, and the correction is
$\dot Z^{Ax} = Z^{\mathbf 1}\,\mathbb E[\partial Z^x/\partial \hat Z^{A^\top\mathbf 1}] = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. So
$Z^{AA^\top\mathbf 1} = \mathcal N(0, 1) + 1$, the $\mathcal N(1, 1)$ we derived by hand and simulated.
The $\hat Z$ part is the CLT term; the $\dot Z$ part is the LLN term.

### Why no other parametrization can transfer

Appendix J.3 now argues in two steps.

**Kernel-regime parametrizations can't.** This covers NTP and SP with learning rate
$\Theta(1/\text{width})$ (with a $\Theta(1)$ learning rate, SP is not even stable). Their limit
does not learn features, and on tasks where features matter it performs trivially. Tensor
Programs IV's Word2Vec experiment is the example: the NTK limit gives trivial embeddings, so HPs
have no effect in the limit, while at finite width they do. The landscapes don't converge to
anything useful, and "wider is better" fails.

**Feature-learning parametrizations other than μP can't.** Any other feature-learning
parametrization differs from μP only in that some layers are *not* updated maximally. In the limit
those layers are frozen at initialization, so their learning rate has no effect at infinite width
but does at finite width. The finite-width HP landscape cannot converge to the limit's landscape.

So μP is the unique abc-parametrization in which every layer's learning rate plays the same role
at every width, which is what transfer needs. The paper is careful to call this an argument, not a
theorem: it explains why the optimum *can* converge, not how fast.

## Which hyperparameters transfer

Table 1 of the paper sorts HPs into three groups.

| Transfer | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **μTransferable** | optimization (learning rate, momentum, Adam $\beta$, LR schedule), initialization (per-layer init variance), parameter multipliers (output, attention, embedding) |
| **Not μTransferable** | regularization: dropout, weight decay |
| **Transferred across** | width; depth\*, batch size\*, training time\*, sequence length\* |

\* validated empirically on Transformers only. Transfer across width is justified by the theory.

Regularization doesn't transfer because how much to regularize depends on how large the model is
*relative to the data*, and μP knows nothing about the data size. The paper therefore targets the
training loss, which is the right target for large pretraining runs where overfitting is not the
bottleneck. Fine-tuning on a small dataset is a case where μTransfer may not be enough.

Transfer across depth, batch size, sequence length and training time is an empirical finding
(Figs. 4 and 19): HPs transferred well once the proxy had at least width 256, depth 4, batch size
32, sequence length 128 and 5000 steps. There are caveats. The optimal *initialization scale* does
not transfer across depth, and depth transfer works only for **pre-layernorm** Transformers, not
post-layernorm ones (which are much more sensitive to HPs in general).

## μTransfer in practice

#### The procedure

Algorithm 1 of the paper is three lines:

1. Parametrize the target model in μP.
2. Tune a smaller version (in width and/or depth) of the target model.
3. Copy the tuned hyperparameters to the target model.

### The `mup` package

The authors released [`mup`](https://github.com/microsoft/mup) for PyTorch (Appendix H). It
implements Table 8 and is designed so that, at a chosen **base shape**, the model is bit-for-bit
the SP model. There are two ways to choose the base: set it to the *target* model's shape (so the
target trains exactly as it would in SP, and you tune shrunk copies), or set it to the small
model you explored with (so its HPs carry over as you scale up). Usage:

```python
from mup import MuReadout, MuAdam, set_base_shapes

class Model(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, width):
        ...
        self.readout = MuReadout(width, vocab_size)  # replaces the output nn.Linear

model = Model(width=4096)
base = Model(width=256)    # widths here define "1x"
delta = Model(width=512)   # tells mup which dimensions grow with width
set_base_shapes(model, base, delta=delta)  # attaches p.infshape to every parameter
opt = MuAdam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)  # per-parameter-group LR scaling
```

`set_base_shapes` gives each parameter an `infshape` that records which dimensions are
"infinite" (they differ between `base` and `delta`) and their base sizes. The optimizer then
applies Table 8. From [`mup/optim.py`](https://github.com/microsoft/mup/blob/19814971934ef91dd546f88e913fc963e096d11c/mup/optim.py):

```python
# MuAdam: only matrix-like parameters (2 infinite dims) are rescaled
for p in param_group['params']:
    if p.infshape.ninf() == 2:
        matrix_like_p[p.infshape.width_mult()]['params'].append(p)
    ...
for width_mult, group in matrix_like_p.items():
    group['lr'] /= width_mult          # Adam LR 1/fan_in for hidden weights
```

where `width_mult()` is `fan_in / base_fan_in`. The output layer's $1/\text{fan\_in}$ multiplier lives
in [`MuReadout.forward`](https://github.com/microsoft/mup/blob/19814971934ef91dd546f88e913fc963e096d11c/mup/layer.py),
which computes `super().forward(self.output_mult * x / self.width_mult())`. `MuSGD` multiplies
the learning rate of vector-like parameters by `width_mult` (Table 8's $\text{fan\_out}$ and
$\text{fan\_in}$) and divides matrix-like ones by their fan-in/fan-out ratio. Attention has to be
changed by hand; the README's example keeps compatibility with $1/\sqrt d$ at the common $d = 64$:

```python
# attention_scores = query @ key.T / d**0.5
attention_scores = query @ key.T * 8 / d   # 8/d == 1/sqrt(d) at d = 64
```

#### Practical advice from Appendix D

- **Coordinate check** every μP implementation (as above); the package's
  `mup.coord_check.get_coord_data` records each activation's size over a few steps at many widths.
- **Zero-initialize the output layer and the query projection.** The μP network at initialization
  is approximately a Gaussian process with variance $\Theta(1/\text{width})$, which is $0$ in the
  limit but not in a small proxy. Starting from exactly zero removes this mismatch
  (`MuReadout(..., readout_zero_init=True)`). The same goes
  for attention logits, which are $\Theta(1/\sqrt{d_\text{head}})$ at initialization; $W_q = 0$ makes
  them exactly 0.
- **Keep $d_\text{head}$ (in particular $d_k$) large in the proxy**, for example 32 or more. A small
  $d_k$ makes the HP landscape noisy (Fig. 10).
- **Prefer relu-like activations and Gaussian initialization.** A narrow network's preactivations
  fluctuate more, and squashing functions such as tanh saturate more often in narrow networks than
  in wide ones, which biases the landscape. Non-Gaussian initialization converges to the limit more
  slowly.
- **Optimizers** (Appendix B.3): AdamW, Adagrad and RMSProp follow the Adam column. Weight decay in
  AdamW needs no scaling. Gradient clipping is fine with a width-independent threshold. Adam's
  $\epsilon$ must scale like $1/\text{fan\_in}$ (added after the square root) if it is not negligible.
  Optimizers that normalize the update to the weight's Frobenius norm (LARS, LAMB, Adafactor) give
  hidden updates with $\Theta(1/\sqrt n)$ entries, between Adam's $\Theta(1)$ and the needed
  $\Theta(1/n)$; the paper suggests this partly explains why T5 trained 220M to 11B models with one
  set of HPs while GPT-3 had to lower its learning rate with size.

#### Reverse μTransfer

Appendix I turns the idea around. If a large SP model diverges with some HPs, transfer those HPs
*down* to a small μP model (with the large model's width as base width) and the small model
diverges too. The authors used this to debug a width-8192 Transformer that diverged mid-training:
the width-256 copy diverged the same way, and its activations showed exploding attention logits.
A separate 6B-parameter SP model that kept blowing up was reproduced on a 100M-parameter model and
fixed by retuning it.

## An open puzzle

Section 9 points out something strange. For μTransfer to be useful, the proxy width $N$ must be
large enough that the HP *optimum* has converged, but small enough that the network *itself* has
not converged to the infinite-width limit, or the large model would be pointless. Experiments show
such $N$ exists. So the optimum is a "coarse" quantity that converges quickly with width, while the
function and its loss are "fine" quantities that converge slowly. Why this should be so is not
explained by the theory, and the authors leave it open, along with finite-width corrections to the
small remaining drift of the optimum, a principled parametrization for depth, and the transfer of
regularization HPs.

## Summary

- A **parametrization** is a rule for scaling initialization, multipliers and learning rates with
  width. Hyperparameters transfer across width only if the HP landscape converges as width grows,
  which depends on the parametrization, and *every* HP must be scaled correctly.
- In SP, training makes weight updates **correlated** with the activations they multiply, so their
  products grow by the **law of large numbers** ($\Theta(n)$), not the CLT ($\Theta(\sqrt n)$). Output
  and attention logits blow up while input layers barely learn, so no single learning rate works.
- **μP** fixes this: output weights $\Theta(1/n)$, hidden updates $\Theta(1/n)$, input updates
  $\Theta(1)$, attention $1/d$. In Adam terms: hidden and output learning rates $\propto 1/\text{fan\_in}$,
  output multiplier $1/\text{fan\_in}$, everything else constant.
- By Tensor Programs IV, stable parametrizations either **learn features** ($r = 0$) or are in the
  **kernel regime** ($r > 0$), never both; μP is the unique one where **every layer updates
  maximally**, so every learning rate keeps its meaning at every width.
- **μTransfer**: write the model in μP, tune a narrow (and shallower, shorter-trained) proxy, copy
  the HPs. It worked from 13M to 350M (BERT) and 40M to 6.7B (GPT-3); regularization HPs do not
  transfer.
