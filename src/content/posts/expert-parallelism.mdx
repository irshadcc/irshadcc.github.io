## Background

In a dense transformer, every token goes through every weight. The MLP in each layer holds about
two-thirds of those weights (with the usual hidden layer $4\times$ the model width, it has $8d^2$
of the layer's $12d^2$), so a model with twice the parameters costs twice as much per token. A
**mixture-of-experts** (MoE) layer breaks that link. It replaces the one MLP with many smaller
ones, the **experts**, and adds a **router** that sends each token to only a few of them. The
model has the parameters of all the experts, but each token pays for the compute of only a few.
Mixtral 8x7B has 8 experts per layer and uses 2 per token: 47B parameters, of which 13B are
active for any one token. DeepSeek-V3 has 256 routed experts per layer plus one shared expert,
and uses 8 of the routed ones per token: 671B parameters, 37B active.

The saving comes with two problems. The first is balance. Nothing makes a learned router spread
tokens evenly, and an expert that gets twice its share takes twice as long, while the others
wait. Left alone, a router can also **collapse**: the few experts it favours early get the most
gradient, get better, and get chosen even more, while the rest never learn. The second is size.
All those experts don't fit on one GPU, so **expert parallelism** (EP) gives each GPU a few whole
experts and moves every token to the GPUs that hold its experts, with an **all-to-all**, the one
collective where every rank sends something different to every other rank.

This post covers both. It starts with the MoE layer on a single GPU: the equations, the
different kinds of router, how the tokens are dispatched to the experts and combined back, and
the two ways training keeps the load balanced, Switch Transformer's auxiliary loss and
DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free bias. It then builds an expert-parallel layer in PyTorch, looks
at what happens when the load is uneven, works out what the all-to-alls cost, and ends with how
Megatron-LM, DeepSpeed and DeepSeek implement it.

EP is the last of the four parallel dimensions in
[Distributed Topology](/blog/distributed-topology/), after
[tensor](/blog/tensor-parallelism/), [data](/blog/zero-and-fsdp/) and
[pipeline](/blog/pipeline-parallelism/) parallelism. The all-to-all and its cost are covered in
[How GPUs Talk](/blog/collective-communication/).

## Mixture of experts

An MoE layer has $E$ experts $\text{FFN}_1, \ldots, \text{FFN}_E$, each an ordinary MLP, and a
**gating function** $G$ that gives every expert a weight for the token $x$. The output is the
weighted sum of the experts' outputs (Shazeer et al., 2017):

$$
y = \sum_{e=1}^{E} G(x)_e \, \text{FFN}_e(x)
$$

The trick is that $G(x)$ is **sparse**: only $k$ of its $E$ entries are non-zero, and the experts
with weight zero are never run. The cost per token is $k$ experts, whatever $E$ is.

In most models today the experts are gated MLPs, as in the dense Llama-style layer:
$\text{FFN}_e(x) = W^{(e)}_2 \big(\text{SiLU}(W^{(e)}_1 x) \odot W^{(e)}_3 x\big)$. The gating
function is built from a **router**, a linear layer $W_r$ with one row per expert, whose output
$h = W_r x$ holds one **logit** per expert. The most common router turns the logits into
probabilities, keeps the top $k$ and renormalizes them into **gate weights**:

$$
p = \text{softmax}(h), \qquad \mathcal{T} = \text{top-}k(p), \qquad
g_e = \frac{p_e}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{T}} p_j} \text{ for } e \in \mathcal{T}, \qquad
y = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{T}} g_e \, \text{FFN}_e(x)
$$

Take a layer with $E = 4$ experts, $k = 2$, and the token "cat", whose router logits are
$h = (0.2, 1.1, 1.9, 0.0)$. The softmax gives $p = (0.103, 0.252, 0.561, 0.084)$. The top two are
experts 2 and 1, so $\mathcal{T} = \{2, 1\}$, and renormalizing gives
$g_2 = 0.561 / (0.561 + 0.252) = 0.690$ and $g_1 = 0.310$. The layer's output is
$y = 0.690\,\text{FFN}_2(x) + 0.310\,\text{FFN}_1(x)$; experts 0 and 3 never see the token.

The router learns through the gate weights. If expert 2's output lowered the loss, the gradient
raises $g_2$, which raises $h_2$. The top-$k$ choice itself has no gradient: an expert that wasn't
chosen gets no signal from this token, which is the root of the collapse described above.

Some models add **shared experts** that every token goes through, outside the router. DeepSeek-V3
has one per layer, so with the residual connection its MoE layer computes
$h' = u + \text{FFN}^{(s)}(u) + \sum_{e \in \mathcal{T}} g_e \, \text{FFN}^{(r)}_e(u)$ for a token
$u$. The shared expert learns what every token needs, which leaves the routed experts free to
specialize.<Sidenote>DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024) introduced shared experts together with
**fine-grained** experts: many small experts instead of a few large ones, so that each token's
$k$ experts can be combined in many more ways. DeepSeek-V3's routed experts are 2048 wide, against
18432 for its dense layers.</Sidenote>

The rest of this section follows a batch of 8 tokens, "The cat sat on the mat. It", through a
4-expert layer: first the router decides where each token goes, then dispatch lays the tokens
out for the experts, then combine adds the results back up.

### Routers

All routers produce the same thing, a set of experts and a gate weight for each token, but they
differ in who chooses and how. The figure runs six of them on the same 8 × 4 matrix of logits.
Each cell shows the router's score; the highlighted cells are the chosen experts, with their gate
weights in bold, and the bottom row counts how many token copies each expert receives. With 8
tokens and 2 experts each, a balanced router gives every expert 4.

<MoeRouters title="Six routers on the same 8 tokens and 4 experts" />

**Token choice: softmax top-$k$.** Each token picks its $k$ best experts, as above. This is the
router in Mixtral, Qwen-MoE and most other open models. On the example batch, expert 2 is a
favourite: it is in the top 2 of 7 tokens, while expert 1 gets 2. The original MoE layer
(Shazeer et al., 2017) added trainable noise to the logits before the top-$k$,
$H(x)_i = (W_g x)_i + \varepsilon \cdot \text{Softplus}((W_\text{noise} x)_i)$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, so that experts just outside the top $k$ sometimes get chosen
and get a gradient. Models differ in whether they renormalize: Mixtral does (taking the softmax
over only the top $k$ logits gives the same $g$), DeepSeek-V2 keeps the raw $p_e$.

**Top-2 with a random second expert.** GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) always sends a token to its
best expert, but to its second only if $2 g_2 > u$ for a uniform random $u$, where $g_2$ is the
second expert's renormalized gate. A second expert with a small gate adds little to the output, so
skipping it some of the time saves expert capacity.

**Switch: top-1.** Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2021) showed that $k = 1$ works, halving the
expert compute and communication. The gate is the raw probability $p_e$, not renormalized:
renormalizing a single number would make it 1, and the router would get no gradient at all.

**Sigmoid with a bias.** DeepSeek-V3 scores each expert independently with a sigmoid,
$s_e = \sigma(h_e)$, so one expert's score doesn't push down the others'. It picks the top $k$ of
$s_e + b_e$, where $b_e$ is a per-expert balancing bias, but takes the gates from $s_e$ alone,
normalized over the chosen experts. The bias is the subject of
[balancing without a loss](#balancing-without-a-loss) below. Its tab in the figure has a slider
that runs the bias update: after 4 steps the busiest expert drops from 7 copies to 5.<Sidenote>Two
details from DeepSeek-V3's [inference code](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/blob/9b4e9788e4a3a731f7567338ed15d3ec549ce03b/inference/model.py#L535-L598):
the normalized gates are multiplied by a `route_scale` of 2.5, and the router is **group-limited**.
The 256 experts form 8 groups, and each token first keeps the 4 groups whose two best $s_e + b_e$
add up highest, then picks its 8 experts from those. In training, a group is a node, so no token's
experts span more than 4 nodes.</Sidenote>

**Expert choice.** Zhou et al. (2022) turn the choice around: each expert picks the $c$ tokens
that score it highest, with $c = n \times \text{capacity factor} / E$ for $n$ tokens. The load is
perfectly balanced by construction; in the example each expert takes exactly 4. But a token can
now get any number of experts: "The" and "." get one, "sat" and "It" three, and a token might
get none. It also needs the whole batch to decide, so in a language model a token's routing
depends on tokens that come after it, which leaks the future during training and doesn't work
when generating one token at a time.

**Balanced assignment.** BASE layers (Lewis et al., 2021) route each training batch by solving a
linear assignment problem: maximize the total token-expert score subject to every expert getting
the same number of tokens. Sinkhorn routing (Clark et al., 2022) approximates that: it rescales
the rows and columns of $\exp(h)$ alternately until every expert's column has the same sum, then
takes each token's top $k$ in the rescaled matrix. In the figure, expert 2's column is scaled
down, and its load falls from 7 to 5. At inference, both fall back to plain top-$k$ on the
router's scores.

**Hash routing.** Hash layers (Roller et al., 2021) don't learn a router at all: the expert is a
fixed function of the token's id. The paper uses a random lookup table built at initialization;
the figure uses the GPT-2 token id modulo 4. The load depends only on how often each token
appears, and the routing can't adapt to context: a token always goes to the same expert.

| Router | Chosen by | Balanced by | Used in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Softmax top-$k$ | token | aux loss | Mixtral, Qwen-MoE, DeepSeek-V2 |
| Top-1 | token | aux loss, capacity | Switch Transformer |
| Sigmoid + bias | token | bias, tiny loss | DeepSeek-V3 |
| Expert choice | expert | construction | Zhou et al. (2022) |
| BASE, Sinkhorn | solver | construction (training) | BASE layers, Megatron-LM |
| Hash | fixed function | token frequencies | Hash layers |

### Dispatch

Once the router has spoken, every token has $k$ **copies** to deliver, one per chosen expert. The
16 copies in the example are 4, 2, 7 and 3 for experts 0 to 3. **Dispatch** is the step that
gathers each expert's copies into a contiguous input so that it can run as one matrix multiply.
There are three ways to do it.

**Loop over the experts.** The simplest: for each expert, find the tokens that chose it, run them
through it and add the weighted result back. This is the reference implementation the rest of this
post builds on:

```python
import copy

import torch
import torch.distributed as dist
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

D, FFN, EXPERTS, TOP_K, TOKENS = 32, 64, 8, 2, 64  # TOKENS per rank


class Expert(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.up, self.down = nn.Linear(D, FFN), nn.Linear(FFN, D)

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.down(F.gelu(self.up(x)))


class MoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.router = nn.Linear(D, EXPERTS, bias=False)
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList(Expert() for _ in range(EXPERTS))

    def route(self, x):
        probs = self.router(x).softmax(dim=-1)
        weights, ids = probs.topk(TOP_K, dim=-1)  # (tokens, k) each
        return weights / weights.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True), ids

    def forward(self, x):
        weights, ids = self.route(x)
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for e, expert in enumerate(self.experts):
            # nonzero's output size depends on the data, so torch.compile breaks the graph here
            token, slot = (ids == e).nonzero(as_tuple=True)
            out = out.index_add(0, token, weights[token, slot, None] * expert(x[token]))
        return out
```

It is correct and easy to read, but it launches separate kernels for every expert, which is slow
with hundreds of small experts.

**Fixed capacity.** GShard and Switch Transformer ran on TPUs, which need every shape known at
compile time. So each expert gets a buffer with a fixed number of slots, its **capacity**:

$$
C = \left\lceil \frac{T k}{E} \times \text{capacity factor} \right\rceil
$$

for $T$ tokens. A capacity factor of 1 is exactly the balanced share. Each copy's slot is a
cumulative sum over a one-hot **dispatch mask**: the $n$-th copy bound for expert $e$ takes slot
$n$. GShard numbers all the first choices before any second choices, so that when an expert is
full, the copies that get **dropped** are the less important ones. The buffers then form one
$E \times C \times d$ tensor, built with a single einsum of the mask and the tokens, and the
experts run as one batched matrix multiply.<MarginNote>Megatron-LM's capacity mode
(`--moe-expert-capacity-factor`) keeps the copies with the highest probability by default
(`--moe-token-drop-policy probs`), or the earliest (`position`).</MarginNote>

**Sort, then grouped GEMM.** Modern systems are **dropless**. They sort the copies by expert id,
gather them into one tensor, and count how many each expert got. A **grouped GEMM**, one kernel
that multiplies each expert's weights by its own, differently sized group of rows, then runs all
the experts at once. MegaBlocks (Gale et al., 2022) showed this could be done efficiently with
block-sparse kernels; Megatron-LM's `permute` is the sort and gather, and its `--moe-grouped-gemm`
the grouped GEMM. Nothing is padded or dropped, but the shapes change from batch to batch.

### Combine

**Combine** is dispatch run backwards. Each expert output row goes back to the token it came
from, is multiplied by its gate weight, and each token sums its $k$ rows. With fixed capacity it is
another einsum, with a **combine tensor** that holds the gate weights where the dispatch mask had
ones. In the sorted, dropless layout it is a scatter-add over the same indices the sort produced,
as in Megatron-LM's `unpermute`. Gradients follow the same path in reverse: the backward of a
gather is a scatter-add, and vice versa.

A few choices that look minor change the result:

- **Dropped copies.** A dropped copy contributes zero, and the remaining gates are not
  renormalized. In the example, "The" keeps its first expert's output scaled by 0.622 and loses
  the other 0.378. If all of a token's copies are dropped, its output is zero, and only the
  residual connection carries it through the layer.
- **Normalization.** Whether the gates sum to 1 (Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3) or not (Switch,
  DeepSeek-V2) sets the scale of the layer's output relative to the residual stream.
- **Where the gate is applied.** It can multiply the expert's output, as above, or be applied
  inside the expert. Megatron-LM's dispatcher sends the probabilities along with the tokens and
  multiplies them in inside the activation function, a change its release notes list as a
  memory saving.
- **Shared experts** are added on top: their output doesn't depend on the router, so they can run
  while the routed tokens are still in flight.

### Balancing with an auxiliary loss

Back to the first problem. The router's training signal only cares about the language-modelling
loss, so nothing stops it from overusing a few experts. The standard fix is an **auxiliary
load-balancing loss** added to the training loss. Switch Transformer's version, for a batch of $T$
tokens, is

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{aux} = \alpha \cdot E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \, P_i, \qquad
f_i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{x} \mathbb{1}\{\text{expert } i \text{ is chosen for } x\}, \qquad
P_i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{x} p_i(x)
$$

$f_i$ is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$, and $P_i$ is the average probability the
router gives it. Under perfect balance both are $1/E$, the sum is $E \cdot 1/E^2 = 1/E$, and the
loss is $\alpha$. The factor $E$ keeps that value the same for any number of experts. Switch used
$\alpha = 10^{-2}$, after sweeping $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-5}$.

The trick is in which part has a gradient. $f_i$ comes from the top-$k$ choice and has none.
$P_i$ does, and $\partial \mathcal{L}_\text{aux} / \partial P_i = \alpha E f_i$: each expert's
average probability is pushed down in proportion to how much traffic it already gets. The busiest
experts are pushed hardest, so tokens drift to the quiet ones.<Sidenote>For $k > 1$, Megatron-LM
counts all $k$ choices and divides by $Tk$, so that $f$ still sums to 1; the code below does the
same. GShard's earlier version is $\frac{1}{E}\sum_e \frac{c_e}{S} m_e$, the same product up to a
constant: it explains $f_i P_i$ as a differentiable stand-in for $f_i^2$, whose sum is smallest
when the load is even.</Sidenote>

In PyTorch:

```python
def switch_aux_loss(logits, ids, alpha=1e-2):
    """Switch Transformer's load-balancing loss, counted over all k choices as in Megatron-LM."""
    tokens, num_experts = logits.shape
    k = ids.shape[1]
    probs = logits.softmax(dim=-1)
    counts = torch.bincount(ids.flatten(), minlength=num_experts)
    f = counts / (tokens * k)  # fraction of token copies per expert: no gradient
    P = probs.mean(dim=0)  # mean router probability per expert: has a gradient
    return alpha * num_experts * (f * P).sum()

# in the training step, for each MoE layer
loss = lm_loss + switch_aux_loss(router_logits, ids)
```

On the example batch with top-2, the loads 4, 2, 7, 3 give $f = (0.250, 0.125, 0.438, 0.188)$,
and the average probabilities are $P = (0.239, 0.203, 0.399, 0.159)$. So
$\mathcal{L}_\text{aux} / \alpha = 4 \times (0.250 \cdot 0.239 + 0.125 \cdot 0.203 + 0.438 \cdot 0.399 + 0.188 \cdot 0.159) = 1.158$,
against 1 for a balanced batch. The gradients on the $P_i$ are $\alpha E f = \alpha\,(1.00, 0.50, 1.75, 0.75)$:
expert 2's probability is pushed down 3.5 times as hard as expert 1's. Through the softmax, that
lowers expert 2's logits and raises the others'; summed over the batch, the gradient on expert 2's
logits is $+0.210\alpha$ and on expert 1's $-0.109\alpha$.

The catch is that this loss pulls against the language-modelling loss. To balance, it must make
the router send some tokens to experts that aren't their best, and it adds a gradient to every
router weight that has nothing to do with the task. Set $\alpha$ too small and the load drifts out
of balance; set it large and the model gets worse. Wang et al. (2024) swept $\alpha$ from $10^{-4}$ to
$10^{-2}$ on 1B-parameter MoE models: the small values led to routing collapse, and the large
ones balanced the load but notably raised the perplexity. The [simulation below](#balancing-without-a-loss) shows the same trade-off.

### Balancing without a loss

DeepSeek-V3 avoids the trade-off with the **auxiliary-loss-free** strategy of Wang et al.
(2024). Each expert gets a bias $b_i$ that is added to its score only when choosing the top $k$,

$$
g'_{i,t} = \begin{cases} s_{i,t}, & s_{i,t} + b_i \in \text{Topk}(\{ s_{j,t} + b_j \}, K) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

while the gate weights still come from the unbiased $s_{i,t}$. After every training step, each
expert's bias moves by a fixed amount $\gamma$ against its load:

$$
b_i \leftarrow b_i + \gamma \cdot \text{sign}(\bar{c} - c_i)
$$

where $c_i$ is the number of tokens expert $i$ received in the step and $\bar c$ the mean. An
overloaded expert's bias goes down, so it wins fewer top-$k$ contests next step; an underloaded
expert's goes up. Because the bias never touches the gates or the loss, it adds no gradient: the
router's weights are trained by the language-modelling loss alone. It acts like a price that
rises on busy experts until demand evens out.

```python
class AuxFreeRouter(nn.Module):
    """DeepSeek-V3's router: sigmoid affinities, top-k chosen with a per-expert bias."""

    def __init__(self, dim, num_experts, k, gamma=1e-3):
        super().__init__()
        self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(num_experts, dim) / dim**0.5)
        self.register_buffer("bias", torch.zeros(num_experts))
        self.register_buffer("counts", torch.zeros(num_experts))
        self.k, self.gamma = k, gamma

    def forward(self, x):
        s = torch.sigmoid(x @ self.weight.T)  # affinities, (tokens, experts)
        ids = (s + self.bias).topk(self.k, dim=-1).indices  # the bias steers the choice...
        gates = s.gather(1, ids)  # ...but the gates use s alone
        gates = gates / gates.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        if self.training:
            self.counts += torch.bincount(ids.flatten(), minlength=len(self.bias))
        return gates, ids

    @torch.no_grad()
    def update_bias(self):
        """Call once per training step, after the optimizer step."""
        # With data parallelism, all-reduce self.counts here first.
        self.bias += self.gamma * torch.sign(self.counts.mean() - self.counts)
        self.counts.zero_()
```

The bias is a buffer, not a parameter: the optimizer never sees it. The counts accumulate over
every micro-batch of the step and, under data parallelism, have to be summed over all ranks first,
so that every rank applies the same update. Megatron-LM's `get_updated_expert_bias` does exactly
this, with an all-reduce over the tensor, data and context-parallel group and
`--moe-router-bias-update-rate` as $\gamma$ (default $10^{-3}$). The loads also come from the step
just finished, not the current batch: using the current batch's own loads would let a token's
routing depend on tokens after it.

moves from expert 2 to expert 1. Expert 0's load is exactly the mean, $\text{sign}(0) = 0$, and its
bias never moves. From step 4 on, the busiest expert has 5 copies and the bias oscillates around
the balance point, one token flipping back and forth. With real batches of millions of tokens and
$\gamma = 10^{-3}$, those flips are a tiny fraction of the load.

enough to fix that ($\alpha = 0.1$) makes the task loss 19% worse. The bias leaves the task loss where it

0.32 by the end, while the strong auxiliary loss keeps improving.<Sidenote>Averages over 3 seeds, 2000 Adam
steps at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, softmax scores for all four runs so that only the
balancing differs. This is a toy, not a reproduction of the papers' numbers: here $\alpha = 0.01$
barely helps, and a larger $\gamma = 0.003$ oscillated and did worse (MaxVio
0.63), the effect Wang et al. report for $\gamma = 0.01$.</Sidenote>

DeepSeek-V3 keeps one small loss as a safety net. The bias balances each expert's load over the
whole batch, but a single sequence could still send all its tokens to the same few experts. So it
adds a **sequence-wise balance loss** of the same form, computed per sequence:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{Bal} = \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i P_i, \qquad
f_i = \frac{E}{K T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{1}\{i \text{ chosen for } t\}, \qquad
P_i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{s_{i,t}}{\sum_j s_{j,t}}
$$

with $T$ the sequence length. Here $f_i$ is scaled to be 1 for every expert under balance, and
the $s$ are normalized per token because sigmoid scores don't sum to 1. DeepSeek-V3 sets
$\alpha = 10^{-4}$, "just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence", and $\gamma =
10^{-3}$ for the first 14.3T training tokens, then $\gamma = 0$ for the last 500B, freezing the
biases.

```python
def sequence_balance_loss(s, ids, alpha=1e-4):
    """DeepSeek-V3's complementary loss on one sequence: s is (T, E) affinities, ids (T, k)."""
    T, E = s.shape
    k = ids.shape[1]
    f = torch.bincount(ids.flatten(), minlength=E) * E / (k * T)  # 1 for every expert when balanced
    P = (s / s.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)).mean(dim=0)
    return alpha * (f * P).sum()
```

The other half of balance is the hardware: even a balanced router leaves some batches uneven, and
with experts spread over GPUs, the busiest GPU sets the pace, as the next section shows.

## The distributed MoE layer

With **expert parallelism** over $N$ ranks, the **EP group**, each rank holds $E/N$ whole experts.
Everything else in the layer, including the router, is replicated as in data parallelism, and each
rank runs its own tokens. One MoE layer then has three steps:

1. **Dispatch.** Each rank routes its tokens and sends each copy to the rank that holds its
   expert.
2. **Compute.** Each rank runs its experts on the copies it received, from every rank.
3. **Combine.** Each rank sends the results back to where the tokens came from, and each token
   adds up its $k$ results.

Dispatch and combine are both **all-to-alls**: rank $i$'s buffer holds one chunk for every rank
$j$, the copies bound for rank $j$'s experts, and chunk $j$ must end up on rank $j$. Combine is the
same exchange in reverse. Unlike the textbook all-to-all in
[How GPUs Talk](/blog/collective-communication/), the chunks are uneven: rank $i$ sends rank $j$
as many copies as chose $j$'s experts, which changes with every batch.

### Megatron-LM's MoE layer

Megatron-LM's `MoELayer.forward` runs the three steps through a **token dispatcher**. With
`--moe-token-dispatcher-type alltoall` it is `MoEAlltoAllTokenDispatcher`, which adds the
bookkeeping around the two all-to-alls: counting the copies, sorting them so that each
destination's chunk is contiguous, and undoing both sorts on the way back.

The figure runs it on a scaled-down configuration: EP = 4, 8 experts (2 per rank, so rank $r$
holds experts $2r$ and $2r + 1$), top-2 routing and 8 tokens, 2 per rank. Each step highlights

along it, as a matrix with its shape, and over a node to see the equation it computes. Compare the
edge into "All-to-all: dispatch" with the edge out of it: before the dispatch each rank holds
copies for every rank's experts, after it only copies for its own.

A few things to look for:

- **The counts move first.** Steps 3 to 6 move no tokens. The ranks exchange how many copies they
  will send each other with an all-gather of the per-expert counts, because `all_to_all` needs the
  split sizes on the CPU to size its buffers. That copy to the host is a synchronization point.
  Megatron starts it on a side stream and, depending on the MoE and parallel settings, waits for
  it as late as it can, at the latest just before the dispatch all-to-all.
- **Two sorts on each side.** `permute` sorts the local copies by expert, so the chunk for each
  rank is contiguous. After the exchange, the received rows are ordered by sender, then by expert,
  and `sort_chunks_by_idxs` regroups them by local expert for the grouped GEMM. On the way back,
  the same two sorts run in reverse.
- **The gates travel with the tokens.** The gate weights go through their own all-to-all, and
  the experts multiply them into the activation. `unpermute` then only has to add each token's
  rows together.
- **The busiest rank sets the pace.** Rank 1, which holds the popular experts 2 and 3, receives 8
  of the 16 copies; rank 3 receives 2. The grouped GEMM on rank 1 does four times the work of rank
  3's, and the combine can't finish until rank 1 is done. Hover over an edge leaving "Count copies,
  all-gather counts" to see the whole table of counts.

### Megatron Core at scale

The figure shows the algorithm at its simplest. NVIDIA's paper on Megatron Core's MoE support,
[Scalable Training of Mixture-of-Experts Models with Megatron Core](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07685)
(Yan et al., 2026), describes what it takes to run the same layer on thousands of GPUs. It
organizes the work around three walls, memory, communication and compute efficiency, and several of
its answers explain details visible in the figure.

**Parallel Folding.** Attention and MoE layers want different parallelism. Attention benefits from
tensor and context parallelism; experts are small and want to stay whole, spread by EP. Older
frameworks carved the EP group out of the data-parallel group, so EP could be at most DP. Megatron
Core gives the two kinds of layer separate decompositions of the same GPUs: attention uses
TP × CP × DP × PP, the MoE layers use expert TP × EP × expert DP × PP, and only PP must match. With
attention at TP 4, CP 2, DP 8 and PP 4 on 256 GPUs, the old rule caps EP at 8; folding allows EP 64
with expert TP 1. Folding also lets CP and EP share the same 8 GPUs of an NVLink domain, so both of
their all-to-alls stay off the slower network.

**Why the gate goes inside the expert.** In the figure, the experts' line of code multiplies the gate
weights into the activation rather than into the expert output. Without biases, the two are the same:

$$
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}} p_i \, W_2^{(i)} \phi\big(W_1^{(i)} x\big)
= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}} W_2^{(i)} \big(p_i \, \phi(W_1^{(i)} x)\big)
$$

because a scalar commutes with a linear map. But the gradient with respect to $p_i$ differs in what
it needs. On the left it needs each expert's output, which would have to be kept for the backward
pass; on the right it needs only $\phi(W_1^{(i)} x)$, which a fused kernel recomputes from the
pre-activation $W_1^{(i)} x$ that SwiGLU's own backward keeps anyway. The paper calls this
memory-efficient permutation and measures about 26.3 GB of activation memory saved per GPU on
DeepSeek-V3, at no extra compute.

**Token-based dispatch.** The `alltoall` dispatcher in the figure permutes first, so a token with
two experts on the same rank is sent twice. The `flex` dispatcher instead hands the tokens to DeepEP
or to NVIDIA's **HybridEP**, which send each token once per destination and fuse the reduction into
the combine. Like DeepSeek's kernels, HybridEP crosses the network once per target node, to the
GPU with the same local index, and forwards inside the node. Measured with hidden size 7168 and 256
experts:

| Dispatch latency (µs) | EP 8 | EP 16 | EP 32 | EP 64 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| H100, `alltoall` | 1265 | 5774 | 8059 | 9164 |
| H100, HybridEP | 661 | 1485 | 3064 | 4626 |
| GB200, `alltoall` | 735 | 743 | 769 | 930 |
| GB200, HybridEP | 391 | 578 | 612 | 675 |

On H100 the gap is largest from EP 16 on, once the all-to-all leaves an 8-GPU node; on a GB200
NVL72 system, where up to 72 GPUs share one NVLink domain, both stay nearly flat.

**Overlap.** Even with faster dispatch, the paper reports that the all-to-alls take 30 to 40% of
DeepSeek-V3's iteration time at EP 64. Megatron Core hides them by merging the forward pass of one
micro-batch with the backward pass of another inside the usual 1F1B schedule, with computation and
communication on separate CUDA streams. It is the same pairing as DualPipe without its
bidirectional pipeline, and it needs no extra activation memory; only the first forward and last
backward of a step stay exposed.

**Sync-free dropless MoE.** The count exchange in the figure hides a problem: the CPU needs the
per-expert counts to size the buffers and launch the grouped GEMM, so every MoE layer waits for a
device-to-host copy, and the layer can't be captured in a CUDA graph. The paper removes the wait
with kernels that read the counts from GPU memory: a device-initiated grouped GEMM (cuBLASLt from
CUDA 13.1, or a CuTe DSL kernel with the activation fused in) and a HybridEP dispatch into buffers
sized by an upper bound. Upper bounds waste memory, up to the EP size times the actual need if
every token could go to one rank, so two more pieces shrink the waste. **ECHO** clones the hottest
experts onto ranks with spare capacity for the current batch, matching each expert's overflow to
spare slots with bin packing, and reduces the clones' gradients back into the original in the
backward pass. **Paged stashing** reuses the unused parts of the preallocated buffers inside the
CUDA graph. Together they let the whole dropless layer run in one graph.

With all of this, the paper reports, for DeepSeek-V3 at sequence length 4096 with routing forced to
be balanced:

| GPUs | TP/PP/EP | Precision | TFLOPS/GPU |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 256 GB200 | 1/4/64 | MXFP8 | 1048 |
| 1024 H100 | 2/8/64 | FP8, blockwise | 368 |

The GB200 run dispatches with HybridEP, the H100 run with DeepEP.

The two runs needed different fixes. On H100, EP 64 spans 8 nodes, and the paper estimates the
cross-node all-to-all would take nearly half of each step with the standard dispatcher, so DeepEP
and the overlap matter most. On GB200, EP 64 stays inside the NVL72 NVLink domain, the
communication wall mostly disappears, and the CPU can't launch kernels fast enough, so partial
CUDA graphs and kernel fusion matter most. The larger memory (192 GB against 80 GB) also lets the
GB200 run use half the pipeline depth.

## DeepSpeed's MoE layer

DeepSpeed's `deepspeed.moe.layer.MoE` came out of DeepSpeed-MoE (Rajbhandari et al., 2022). It
wraps any expert module you give it, and its forward pass follows GShard's algorithm closely:

```python
moe = MoE(hidden_size=h, expert=mlp, num_experts=64, ep_size=8, k=2,
          capacity_factor=1.0, min_capacity=4, drop_tokens=True, use_rts=True)
output, l_aux, exp_counts = moe(hidden_states)
loss = lm_loss + aux_coeff * l_aux  # the caller adds the balancing loss
```

The arguments shown are the defaults except `num_experts`, `ep_size` and `k`, which default to 1.
The design choice that shapes everything else is a **fixed capacity**.

### Fixed capacity, equal all-to-alls

Every rank gives every expert the same number of slots,
$C = \lceil S / E \times \text{capacity factor} \rceil$ for $S$ local tokens, doubled for top-2,
and at least `min_capacity`. The tokens are scattered into an `[E, C, h]` buffer; slots nobody
fills are zero padding, and copies past an expert's capacity are dropped. Because every buffer has
the same shape, both all-to-alls send equal chunks, and no counts have to be exchanged first.

The figure runs it on the same example. With a capacity factor of 1, each expert gets
$C = \lceil 2 \times 2/8 \rceil = 1$ slot per rank. The `min_capacity` default of 4 is set to 0
here; it exists so that small batches don't drop everything, and on 2 tokens per rank it would
make the buffers mostly padding.

<MoeEpSteps flow="deepspeed" title="DeepSpeed's MoE layer: EP = 4, 8 experts, top-2, capacity 1" />

Every rank sends 6 rows in each all-to-all (2 to each of the 3 others) whatever the routing,
against 5 to 10 for Megatron's dropless dispatch, and most of those rows are padding: each rank's
buffer holds 8 slots for its 4 copies. In exchange the shapes never change, which suited the
TPU-era kernels this design comes from and still makes the layer easy to capture in a CUDA graph.
One copy is dropped: on rank 0, `t1` claims expert 2's only slot with its first choice, so `t0`'s
second choice is cut and `t0` keeps only expert 1's output, still scaled by its original gate
weight of 0.61. With `drop_tokens=False`, the capacity becomes the largest count any expert has on
any rank, agreed with an all-reduce (MAX) over the EP group: 2 here, which keeps every copy and
doubles the buffers.

### Gating details

DeepSpeed's gates differ from the textbook versions in a few places worth knowing:

- **Top-1** uses Switch's loss, $E \sum_e m_e c_e$, with $m_e$ the mean gate and $c_e$ the fraction
  of tokens sent to expert $e$. When an expert is over capacity, **Random Token Selection**
  (`use_rts=True`, the default) keeps a random subset of its tokens instead of the first ones in
  the batch, so that tokens late in a sequence aren't always the ones dropped.
- **Top-2** picks the second expert by adding Gumbel noise to the logits before the second argmax
  (`top2_2nd_expert_sampling=True`, the default), a softer version of GShard's rule of keeping the
  second expert with probability $2g_2$. Its loss, $E^2 \cdot \text{mean}_e(m_e c_e)$, is the same
  scale as top-1's but counts only first choices in $c_e$. Slots are assigned to all first choices
  before any second choice, as in GShard, and the two gates are renormalized to sum to 1.
- **Noise.** `noisy_gate_policy="Jitter"` multiplies the gate's input by a random factor in
  $[0.99, 1.01]$ during training; `"RSample"` adds Gumbel noise to the logits used to choose the
  expert, but not to the gate weights.

### Tensor and expert-data parallelism

When the dense layers are tensor-parallel, every TP rank holds the same tokens, so naive dispatch
would send each token TP times. DeepSpeed's `drop_tokens` keeps only this TP rank's share of the
capacity slots before the all-to-all and `gather_tokens` all-gathers them afterwards, dividing the
all-to-all volume by the TP size.

Expert parameters are tagged `allreduce=False` with a group name such as `ep_size_8`, so the engine
all-reduces their gradients only over the **expert-data-parallel** group, the ranks that hold
copies of the same experts, and the dense parameters over the full data-parallel group: an
expert's gradient is already complete on each copy's rank, since the backward all-to-all brought it
the gradients of every token that used it. The DeepSpeed-MoE paper uses this freedom
for **PR-MoE** (pyramid-residual MoE): more experts in the last layers, where they help most, and a
**residual MoE** in every layer, which sends each token through a dense MLP plus one routed expert
and mixes the two with a learned per-token softmax weight (`use_residual=True`). On 128 GPUs, a
PR-MoE with 32, 64 and 128 experts in different layers runs with 128-way data parallelism for the
dense layers and 32-, 64- and 128-way expert parallelism (times 4-, 2- and 1-way expert-data
parallelism) for the MoE layers, so that every GPU holds exactly one expert per layer.

### Inference: slicing and hierarchical all-to-all

The paper's inference system adds two ideas that later systems took up. **Expert-slicing**
splits a single expert's matrices across GPUs, like tensor parallelism for experts, when there
are more GPUs than experts. And a **hierarchical all-to-all** first exchanges within each node,
then between nodes, which cuts the number of messages per GPU from $O(p)$ to $O(G + p/G)$ for $p$
GPUs with $G$ per node, at the cost of moving the data twice; for the small, latency-bound
messages of inference that trade is worth it. DeepSeek's dispatch, below, is a refined version of
the same idea. Recent DeepSpeed versions also add AutoEP, a dropless path ported from TorchTitan:
a token-choice top-$k$ router and grouped-GEMM experts, applied to Hugging Face checkpoints.

## DeepSeek's MoE

DeepSeek's models are the clearest example of MoE architecture and systems designed together.
Most of the choices this post has covered, fine-grained experts, shared experts, auxiliary-loss-free
balancing, node-limited routing and DeepEP, come from this line of work.

### From DeepSeekMoE to DeepSeek-V3

**DeepSeekMoE** (Dai et al., 2024) made two changes to the standard MoE layer. **Fine-grained
expert segmentation** splits each expert into $m$ smaller ones (cutting the hidden width by $m$)
and activates $m$ times as many, so compute and parameters stay the same but the number of
possible expert combinations explodes: top-2 of 16 experts has $\binom{16}{2} = 120$ combinations,
top-8 of 64 quarter-size experts has $\binom{64}{8} = 4{,}426{,}165{,}368$. **Shared expert
isolation** sets aside a few experts that every token uses, to hold common knowledge, so that the
routed experts don't each have to relearn it. The later models scaled both up and changed how
balance is enforced:

| | DeepSeekMoE 16B | V2 | V3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Params (active) | 16.4B (2.8B) | 236B (21B) | 671B (37B) |
| Shared + routed | 2 + 64 | 2 + 160 | 1 + 256 |
| Routed per token | 6 | 6 | 8 |
| Scores | softmax | softmax | sigmoid |
| Routing limit | none | 3 devices | 4 nodes |
| Balancing | expert loss | 3 losses | bias, small loss |
| Dropping | no | training | no |

DeepSeek-V2 balanced with three losses: an expert-level one ($\alpha_1 = 0.003$), a device-level
one that balances the total load of each device's experts ($\alpha_2 = 0.05$), and a communication
one that balances the tokens each device receives ($\alpha_3 = 0.02$). It also dropped the
lowest-scoring tokens on any device over its average budget, a capacity factor of 1.0 per device,
while never dropping tokens from about 10% of the training sequences, so that inference could run
without dropping and stay consistent with training. DeepSeek-V3 replaced all of this with the
[bias](#balancing-without-a-loss) and a sequence-wise loss of $\alpha = 10^{-4}$, and drops nothing,
in training or in inference.

### Node-limited routing and the two-hop dispatch

On DeepSeek's H800 cluster, NVLink inside a node gives 160 GB/s and InfiniBand between nodes
50 GB/s, 3.2 times less. So DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to experts on at most $M = 4$ of the 8
nodes its EP group spans. The router scores each node by the sum of its best $K_r / M = 2$
expert affinities, keeps the best 4 nodes and picks the top 8 experts among them. A token then
crosses InfiniBand at most 4 times, once per target node, to the GPU with the same local index as
its own; NVLink forwards it from there to every GPU on that node that holds one of its experts.
The figure runs this on the example, as 2 nodes of 2 GPUs:

<MoeEpSteps flow="deepseek" title="DeepSeek-V3's two-hop dispatch: 2 nodes × 2 GPUs, 8 experts, top-2" />

Seven copies need the other node but only six transfers cross InfiniBand: `t6` sends one row to
node 0 that serves both its experts there. The saving grows with the number of experts per node a
token uses: the report notes that with IB and NVLink fully overlapped, a token can reach about 3.2
experts per node at no extra cost, so DeepSeek-V3 could use up to 13 experts per token ($4 \times
3.2$) for the communication cost of 8. The combine is the mirror image, and it sums on the way:
each forwarding GPU adds up a token's results from its node before sending one row back over
InfiniBand.

The kernels that do this use 20 of the H800's 132 SMs, split into 10 channels, with **warp
specialization**: separate warps send over InfiniBand, forward to NVLink and receive, and the
number of warps per task adapts to the load. For the combine, the stages are NVLink sending,
forwarding with accumulation, and InfiniBand receiving with accumulation. Custom PTX instructions
and auto-tuned chunk sizes keep the kernels from polluting the L2 cache that the computation shares.

### Hiding the communication: DualPipe

Even with these kernels, cross-node EP puts DeepSeek-V3's training at about one unit of
communication per unit of computation. **DualPipe** hides it by pairing a forward chunk of one
micro-batch with a backward chunk of another, and splitting both into attention, dispatch, MLP and
combine (with the backward split further into input and weight gradients, as in ZeroBubble), so
that one chunk's all-to-all always runs during the other's computation. It feeds micro-batches from
both ends of the [pipeline](/blog/pipeline-parallelism/) at once, which also shrinks the pipeline
bubble, at the cost of two copies of the parameters: affordable because with EP 64 each GPU holds
few experts. The training layout is PP 16 with DualPipe, EP 64 across 8 nodes, ZeRO-1 data
parallelism, and no tensor parallelism at all.

### DeepEP

**DeepEP** is the open-source form of these kernels, now at version 2.5. A single `EPBuffer` serves
training, prefill and decoding through the same `dispatch` and `combine` calls:

- **Hybrid or direct.** In hybrid mode, the default, multi-node traffic takes the two-hop RDMA +
  NVLink path above; direct mode sends straight to the destination GPU.
- **Expanded layout.** `dispatch` returns the received rows already grouped by expert and aligned
  for the grouped GEMM (DeepGEMM's alignment, for example), one gate weight per row; the experts
  apply the weights, and `combine` only sums. Inputs to `dispatch` can be FP8 with their scale
  factors; inputs to `combine` must be BF16.
- **CPU sync or not.** With `do_cpu_sync=True` (training, prefill) the receive counts are brought
  to the CPU and the outputs have exact sizes. With `False` (decoding) the outputs are allocated
  to a fixed capacity and the GEMMs read the valid ranges from GPU-side counts, which keeps the
  CPU out of the loop and lets a decode step be captured in a CUDA graph.
- **Overlap.** Calls can return before the data has arrived, with the final step deferred until
  `wait()`, so independent work runs in between; the number of SMs used is computed from the
  topology or set by hand. The backward of dispatch is a combine, and vice versa, reusing the
  routing handle saved in the forward pass.

Version 2.5 also adds primitives for redundant experts in training: prefetching a redundant
expert's weights over NVLink before it computes, and reducing its FP32 gradients back into the
original expert afterwards.

### Serving DeepSeek-V3

DeepSeek serves prefill and decoding on separate machines. **Prefill** runs on 4 nodes, 32 GPUs:
attention with TP 4, sequence parallelism and DP 8, the MoE layers with EP 32, the same two-hop
all-to-all as in training, and 32 redundant experts, one extra per GPU, chosen every 10 minutes or
so from the observed load ([EPLB](#expert-parallel-load-balancing-eplb) below). Two micro-batches
of similar size are processed together, so that one's attention and experts run during the
other's dispatch and combine. **Decoding** runs on 40 nodes, 320 GPUs: attention with TP 4 and
DP 80, and EP 320, with one expert per GPU and 64 GPUs holding the shared expert and the redundant
ones. The shared expert is treated as a ninth routed expert that every token picks. The all-to-alls
go point to point over InfiniBand, using IBGDA so the GPU drives the network directly, because at
a few hundred tokens per expert decoding is limited by latency and memory reads, not by compute.

## The MoE megakernel

Every implementation so far runs one MoE layer as a chain of kernels. The baseline DeepGEMM
measures against has five: a DeepEP dispatch, a grouped GEMM for the first linear layer, a SwiGLU
kernel that also applies the gate weights, a grouped GEMM for the second, and a DeepEP combine.
Each one writes its result to HBM for the next to read back, each launch waits for the previous one
to drain, and communication only overlaps computation if the framework splits the batch and
interleaves the pieces (DualPipe, TBO, DBO).

DeepSeek's **Mega MoE**, in [DeepGEMM](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM), fuses all five
into a single persistent kernel. Its README describes it as fusing and overlapping "EP dispatch,
linear 1 and linear 2 (FP8xFP4 or FP8xFP8), SwiGLU, and EP combine into a single mega-kernel,
overlapping NVLink communication and tensor core computation". It runs on Blackwell GPUs within
one NVLink domain, and needs every rank's buffers in **symmetric memory**, allocated at the same
offset on every GPU so that a rank can read and write a peer's buffer with ordinary loads and
stores:

```python
buffer = deep_gemm.get_symm_buffer_for_mega_moe(
    group, num_experts, num_max_tokens_per_rank, num_topk, hidden, intermediate_hidden,
    mma_type='fp8xfp4')
l1, l2 = deep_gemm.transform_weights_for_mega_moe(l1_weights, l2_weights)

buffer.x[:num_tokens].copy_(x_fp8)            # inputs go into the symmetric buffer
buffer.x_sf[:num_tokens].copy_(x_sf)
buffer.topk_idx[:num_tokens].copy_(topk_idx)
buffer.topk_weights[:num_tokens].copy_(topk_weights)

y = torch.empty((num_tokens, hidden), dtype=torch.bfloat16, device='cuda')
deep_gemm.fp8_fp4_mega_moe(y, l1, l2, buffer)  # dispatch, both GEMMs, SwiGLU, combine
```

The pull request that introduced it reports, on Blackwell with EP 8 and 512 tokens per rank,
146.5 µs per layer for DeepSeek-V4-Flash's shapes (256 experts, top-6, hidden 4096) and 369.6 µs
for DeepSeek-V4-Pro's (384 experts, top-6, hidden 7168), 1.73 and 1.54 times faster than the
five-kernel baseline, and up to 1.96 times faster at 1 token per rank, where launch and
synchronization overheads dominate.<Sidenote>From the benchmark table in
[DeepGEMM PR #316](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepGEMM/pull/316), FP8 activations and FP4 weights,
averaged over the 8 ranks. The baseline is the five-kernel chain above, built from DeepEP, DeepGEMM
grouped GEMMs and a TileLang SwiGLU kernel, and the test checks the fused kernel's results against
it. I could not run either: both need Blackwell GPUs.</Sidenote> The serving frameworks call it
directly: SGLang with `--moe-a2a-backend megamoe`, falling back to its usual path when a batch has

#### One persistent kernel, six warp roles

The kernel is launched once per layer with one CTA per SM, in clusters of two SMs, and every CTA
stays resident for the whole layer. Instead of giving all warps the same job, it splits them by
role:

```cpp
if (warp_idx < kNumDispatchWarps) {
    cutlass::arch::warpgroup_reg_dealloc<kNumDispatchRegisters>();
    // dispatch: count, publish, pull tokens over NVLink, then clean up
} else if (warp_idx == kNumDispatchWarps) {
    // TMA loads of token tiles (operand A)
} else if (warp_idx == kNumDispatchWarps + 1) {
    // TMA loads of weight tiles (operand B)
} else if (warp_idx == kNumDispatchWarps + 2) {
    // issues the tensor-core MMAs (leader CTA of the pair only)
} else if (warp_idx == kNumDispatchWarps + 3) {
    if (is_leader_cta)
        scheduler.mainloop(num_tokens, sm_locality_domains);   // hands out GEMM tasks
} else {
    cutlass::arch::warpgroup_reg_alloc<kNumEpilogueRegisters>();
    // epilogue: SwiGLU, NVLink writes of results, then the combine reduction
}
```

The roles run concurrently and talk through barriers in shared memory, so one SM can pull tokens
for a later block, load tiles, multiply and write results back at the same time. Their needs
differ, so `warpgroup_reg_dealloc` and `warpgroup_reg_alloc` move registers between them at run
time: with up to 64 experts per rank, dispatch threads keep 48 registers, the load, MMA and
scheduler threads 40, and the epilogue threads get 208, enough to hold a tile row and the combine's
FP32 sums.

#### The symmetric buffer

Everything the ranks share lives in one symmetric allocation, laid out by `MegaMoEBuffer`. It
starts with control words, then the data:

```cpp
struct alignas(128) MegaMoESignals {
    uint32_t grid_sync_count[4];            // SM-to-SM barriers within this GPU
    uint32_t nvl_barrier_counter;           // GPU-to-GPU barrier
    int nvl_barrier_signals[2];
    uint32_t l1_task_count[...], l2_task_count[...];   // the scheduler's queues
    uint64_t combine_ready_grid_idx[kNumMaxRanks];     // "my writes into you are done"
    uint64_t expert_send_count[2048];       // tokens this rank sends to each expert
    uint64_t expert_recv_count[2048];       // [source rank][local expert]
    uint64_t expert_recv_count_sum[512];    // per local expert, over all sources
    uint32_t l1_full_count[1 << 20];        // per ring block: tokens arrived
    uint32_t l1_empty_count[1 << 20];       // per ring block: GEMM-1 N blocks done
    uint64_t l2_full_mask[1 << 20];         // per ring block: one bit per GEMM-1 N block
    uint32_t l2_empty_count[1 << 20];
    ...
};
// then: input tokens / top-k ids / top-k weights   (read by peers during dispatch)
//       L1 ring buffer, L2 ring buffer             (this rank's experts' working set)
//       combine buffer [top-k (+1 shared), tokens, hidden]   (written by peers)
```

`sym_buffer.map(ptr, rank)` turns a pointer into this rank's buffer into the same pointer in
`rank`'s buffer, which is all the communication the kernel needs. The L1 and L2 buffers are
**rings** sized below the worst case: blocks are reused once their consumers have signalled that
they are finished, which bounds the memory while letting many blocks be in flight.

#### Dispatch: count, publish, pull

Dispatch has three phases. First every SM counts, in shared memory, how many of its tokens go to
each expert, then reserves a range of slots for them with one global atomic per expert:

```cpp
read_topk_idx([&](uint32_t token_topk_idx, int expert_idx) {
    atomicAdd_block(shared_storage.expert_token_count + expert_idx, 1);
});
for (uint32_t i = thread_idx; i < kNumExperts; i += kNumDispatchThreads) {
    const uint64_t send_value = (1ull << 32) | shared_storage.expert_token_count[i];
    shared_storage.expert_token_count[i] = static_cast<uint32_t>(
        ptx::atomic_add(workspace.get_expert_send_count_ptr(i), send_value));
}
```

The low 32 bits of the counter add up token counts, so the value an SM gets back is the first slot
it owns; the high 32 bits count how many SMs have added. Second, each SM writes, for each of its
token copies, the copy's index into the *destination* rank's buffer, at the slot it reserved:

```cpp
read_topk_idx([&](uint32_t token_topk_idx, int expert_idx) {
    const auto dst_rank_idx = expert_idx / kNumExpertsPerRank;
    const auto dst_slot_idx = atomicAdd_block(shared_storage.expert_token_count + expert_idx, 1);
    const auto dst_ptr = workspace.get_src_token_topk_idx_ptr(
        expert_idx % kNumExpertsPerRank, sym_buffer.rank_idx, dst_slot_idx);
    *sym_buffer.map(dst_ptr, dst_rank_idx) = token_topk_idx;    // a store into a peer's memory
});
```

After a grid-wide barrier, SM 0 publishes the per-expert totals to every destination rank, and an
NVLink barrier makes sure all ranks have published before anyone reads. These are the counts the
earlier dispatchers sent with an all-gather and copied to the CPU; here they stay on the GPU.

Third, each rank **pulls** the tokens its experts need. Every dispatch warp takes one token slot at
a time, finds out which source rank and token it refers to, and copies the row over NVLink into the
L1 ring in chunks, with a TMA load from the peer into shared memory and a TMA store into the ring:

```cpp
const auto src_base_ptr = sym_buffer.map(
    buffer.input_token_buffer.get_data_buffer(src_token_idx).get_base_ptr(), current_rank_in_expert_idx);
const auto dst_base_ptr = buffer.l1_token_buffer.get_data_buffer(pool_token_idx % kNumRingTokens).get_base_ptr();
...
for (uint32_t i = 0; i < kNumChunks; ++ i) {
    ptx::tma_load_1d(pull_buffer.get_base_ptr(), math::advance_ptr(src_base_ptr, i * kNumBytesPerPull),
                     pull_mbarrier, kNumBytesPerPull);       // remote HBM -> shared memory
    ...
    issue_and_wait_pull_store(i);                           // shared memory -> local L1 ring
}
...
*workspace.get_token_src_metadata_ptr(pool_token_idx) = {current_rank_in_expert_idx, src_token_idx, src_topk_idx};
ptx::red_add_rel(workspace.get_l1_full_count_ptr(pool_block_idx % kNumRingBlocks), 1u);
```

Three details matter. Slots within an expert are assigned to source ranks round-robin (the
"min-peeling" loop in the code), so consecutive pulls go to different peers and spread the load on
the NVLink ports. Before writing into a ring slot, the warp waits on `l1_empty_count` until the
previous user of that slot is done. And the last line, a release-ordered atomic add, is the
signal the GEMM side waits on: when a block's `l1_full_count` reaches `BLOCK_M`, its tokens are
all in place. The metadata written just before it, which rank, token and top-k slot the row came
from, is what the second GEMM uses to send the result home. Pulling rather than pushing means each
rank decides the order in which its own experts' tokens arrive, so it can fill complete blocks for
the GEMM early.

#### The scheduler

The work is split into **tasks**: one task is one block of `BLOCK_M` tokens of one expert times one
pair of `BLOCK_N` output columns, for either the first GEMM (L1, $N$ = twice the expert width, gate
and up projections together) or the second (L2, $N$ = hidden size). A scheduler warp on each leader
CTA claims tasks from global counters and passes them through a two-slot queue in shared memory to
the load, MMA and epilogue warps of both CTAs.

L2 tasks depend on L1 tasks: the second GEMM's input for a block is the first GEMM's output. Rather
than wait for the whole first GEMM, each finished L1 N block sets one bit in the block's
`l2_full_mask`, and an L2 task waits only for the bits covering the K block it is about to load:

```cpp
// Each finished L1 N block toggles its bit in `l2_full_mask`, so L2 K blocks can start as soon as they are fed
L2KBlockDependency l2_k_block_dependency(workspace.get_l2_full_mask_ptr(ring_block_idx), pool_block_idx / kNumRingBlocks);
for (uint32_t k_block_idx = 0; k_block_idx < num_k_blocks; advance_pipeline(k_block_idx)) {
    if (task_info.block_phase == sched::BlockPhase::Linear2)
        l2_k_block_dependency.wait(k_block_idx);
    ...
}
```

The scheduler interleaves L1 and L2 tasks so that the two GEMMs of different blocks overlap, after
a warm-up phase of L1 tasks long enough that no L2 task can be claimed before the L1 tasks it
depends on, which would deadlock a persistent kernel. Shared-expert tasks don't depend on dispatch
at all, so they can be scheduled first and fill the time the pulls take. Tasks are also queued per
**locality domain** (two per GPU, matching Blackwell's two dies), and an SM steals work from the
other domain's queue only once its own is empty.

#### The GEMM pipeline

The GEMMs use Blackwell's 2-SM tensor-core instructions. Two TMA warps fill a ring of
`kNumStages` shared-memory stages, one with token tiles and one with weight tiles, and a single
thread on the leader CTA issues the MMAs:

```cpp
// Wait the token arrival
if (task_info.block_phase == sched::BlockPhase::Linear1) {
    const auto ptr = workspace.get_l1_full_count_ptr(block_idx);
    const auto num_expected_tokens = BLOCK_M * (pool_block_idx / kNumRingBlocks + 1);
    while (ptx::ld_acq(ptr) != num_expected_tokens);
}
...
shared_storage.full_barriers[stage_idx].wait(phase);              // TMA loads landed
ptx::SM100_MMA_F16BF16_2x1SM_SS::fma(b_desc, a_desc, accum_stage_idx * UMMA_N,
    k_block_idx > 0 or umma_k_block_idx > 0 or k > 0, ...);       // accumulate into tensor memory
...
empty_barrier_arrive(k_block_idx == num_k_blocks - 1);            // free the stage; last K: tile done
```

The load warp's spin on `l1_full_count` is the whole hand-off between dispatch and compute:
the GEMM for a block starts the moment its last token lands, while other blocks are still being
pulled. The MMA swaps A and B ("always swap A/B" in the code): the weights take the
tensor core's M dimension, which must be 64, 128 or 256 rows, and the tokens its N dimension, which
can be as small as 8 or 16. That is what lets the kernel use token blocks from 8 to 240 rows,
small enough for decode batch sizes, where an expert may see only a handful of tokens. The token tile is
multicast to both CTAs of the pair, which each load half of it, and the accumulators live in
Blackwell's per-SM **tensor memory** in two stages, so the epilogue can drain one tile while the
next accumulates.

#### The first epilogue: SwiGLU and the gate

When a first-GEMM tile is complete, the epilogue warps read it from tensor memory, apply SwiGLU,
multiply by the token's top-k weight, and store BF16 into the L2 ring with TMA:

```cpp
cute::SM100_TMEM_LOAD_16dp256b1x::copy(tmem_addr, values[0], values[1], values[2], values[3]);
...
// SwiGLU: silu(gate) * up, with the gate and up columns interleaved in groups of 8
auto gate = __bfloat1622float2(bf16_gate);
const auto denom = __fadd2_rn({1.0f, 1.0f}, make_float2(__expf(-gate.x), __expf(-gate.y)));
gate = __fmul2_rn(gate, {math::fast_rcp(denom.x), math::fast_rcp(denom.y)});
bf16x2_output[i * 2 + k] = __float22bfloat162_rn(__fmul2_rn(__fmul2_rn(gate, up), weights));
...
L2KBlockDependency::arrive(workspace.get_l2_full_mask_ptr(ring_block_idx), n_block_idx);
ptx::red_add(workspace.get_l1_empty_count_ptr(ring_block_idx), 1u);
```

The weight is applied here, between the two linear layers, exactly as in Megatron's
memory-efficient permutation, so the second GEMM's output is already weighted and the combine only
has to add. `transform_weights_for_mega_moe` interleaves the gate and up columns in groups of 8, so
that each thread holds matching pairs after one tensor-memory load. The last two lines are the
signals: the L2 bit for this N block, and one more finished user of the L1 ring slot. When the
model sets an activation clamp (`kActivationClamp`), the gate and up values are clamped before
SwiGLU.

#### The second epilogue: writing results home

The second GEMM's output for a token copy belongs on the rank the token came from. The epilogue
reads the metadata the dispatch stored, and writes each row straight into that rank's combine
buffer, at the copy's top-k slot:

```cpp
const auto& [dst_rank_idx, dst_token_idx, dst_topk_idx] = cached_src_metadata[j];
const auto packed = ptx::ld_shared(reinterpret_cast<float4*>(smem_ptr));
const auto dst_token = buffer.combine_token_buffer.get_rank_buffer(dst_topk_idx)
                       .get_data_buffer(dst_token_idx);
const auto dst_ptr = math::advance_ptr<float4>(dst_token.get_base_ptr(), n_idx * sizeof(nv_bfloat16) + ...);
*sym_buffer.map(dst_ptr, dst_rank_idx) = packed;     // a 16-byte store into a peer's memory
```

This replaces the combine all-to-all. The data moves as the tiles finish, spread over the whole
second GEMM, instead of in one burst at the end, and padding rows are skipped (`m_idx_in_block >=
valid_m`), so only real tokens cross NVLink.

#### Combine and cleanup

When all of a GPU's SMs have finished their second-GEMM writes (a grid-wide barrier), SM 0 tells
every peer so by writing the launch's grid index into the peer's `combine_ready_grid_idx`. Using the
grid index rather than a flag means the signal never has to be reset. Then each epilogue warp takes
tokens, waits until every rank holding one of the token's experts has signalled, and reduces:

```cpp
comm::wait_until([&]() { return __all_sync(0xffffffff,
    not is_routed or ptx::ld_acq_sys(peer_ready_ptr) == grid_idx); }, ...);
...
bool do_reduce = move_mask_and_load(load_stage_idx);          // TMA-load the first top-k row
float2 reduced[...] = {};
while (do_reduce) {
    do_reduce = move_mask_and_load(load_stage_idx ^ 1);        // prefetch the next one
    combine_load_barriers[load_stage_idx]->wait(combine_phase);
    for (...) ptx::accumulate(reduced[...], bf16_values[l]);   // FP32 sum
    load_stage_idx ^= 1;
}
... // cast to BF16, then TMA-store the token into y
```

Each token's rows come from local HBM (the peers already wrote them there), loaded two at a time
so that the next row's load overlaps the current sum, and are summed in FP32. A shared expert's
output sits in one extra slot and is added the same way. Meanwhile the dispatch warps, idle since
the pulls finished, reset the counters and ring signals for the next launch, and a final NVLink
barrier makes sure no rank starts the next layer while a peer is still reading its buffer.

#### What changes for FP8 × FP4

The FP8×FP4 kernel, the one benchmarked above, keeps the same structure with block scale factors
added everywhere: tokens travel as FP8 with one UE8M0 scale per 32 values, weights are FP4, the
scale factors are copied into tensor memory next to the operands for block-scaled MMAs, and the
first epilogue quantizes its SwiGLU output back to FP8, computing each block's absolute maximum
across the warps of a pair before writing values and scales into the L2 ring.

A fused kernel trades flexibility for speed. Mega MoE communicates only over NVLink, within one
NVLink domain (it has no RDMA path), runs only on Blackwell, and only with the layouts and data types it was written for; the
weights must be transformed into its layout once at load time. Inside those limits it does in one
launch, with no CPU in the loop, what the rest of this post did with five kernels and a
synchronization point.

## vLLM's MoE layer

Serving changes the problem. There is no backward pass, a decode step processes one token per
request, so batches are small and the all-to-alls are latency-bound, and every step is replayed
from a CUDA graph, which wants fixed shapes. vLLM's MoE layer is built around those constraints.

Expert parallelism is switched on with `--enable-expert-parallel`. The EP group is then every GPU
of the deployment, $\text{EP} = \text{TP} \times \text{DP}$. The usual setup for a model like
DeepSeek-V3 is TP = 1 with data-parallel attention: each rank serves its own requests with its own
copy of the attention weights, and the MoE layers of all ranks share the experts. Experts are
placed in contiguous blocks, rank $r$ holding experts $rE/N$ to $(r + 1)E/N - 1$
(`--expert-placement-strategy linear`, the default).

### The default: all-gather and reduce-scatter

vLLM's default `--all2all-backend` is `allgather_reducescatter`, and it doesn't do an all-to-all at
all. Each rank routes its own tokens, then **all-gathers** every rank's tokens together with their
top-$k$ ids and weights, so that every rank holds the whole batch. Each rank computes only the
copies routed to its own experts, which gives it a partial output for every token, and a
**reduce-scatter** adds the partial outputs and hands each rank back the rows of its own tokens.

The figure runs it on the same example as Megatron's: EP = 4, 8 experts, top-2, 8 tokens, 2 per
rank, with the same routing. After the all-gather every rank holds every token, but it only runs
the copies whose expert it owns; hover over the edge into a fused MoE kernel to see which.

<MoeEpSteps flow="vllm" title="vLLM's default EP path: EP = 4, 8 experts, top-2, 8 tokens" />

Compare the traffic. Megatron's all-to-all moves only the copies that leave a rank, so how much
each rank sends depends on the routing: 5, 10, 5 and 6 rows (dispatch and combine together) in
this example. The all-gather and reduce-scatter move every token to every rank whatever its
routing, 12 rows per rank. In general, with $T$ tokens per rank, top-$k$ routing, $N$ ranks and
balanced routing, each rank sends per layer

| Scheme | Rows sent per rank | DeepSeek-V3 ($k = 8$), EP = 8 | EP = 32 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| All-to-all (dispatch + combine) | $2Tk\,(N-1)/N$ | $14T$ | $15.5T$ |
| All-gather + reduce-scatter | $2T\,(N-1)$ | $14T$ | $62T$ |

The ratio is $N / k$. Within one 8-GPU node, with DeepSeek-V3's 8 experts per token, the two move
the same amount, and the simple collectives win: NCCL implements them well, their sizes are known
in advance, and the routing never has to be exchanged. Across nodes the all-gather sends each
token to many GPUs that don't need it, which is why multi-node deployments switch to an all-to-all
backend.<Sidenote>Both counts assume ring algorithms and ignore the top-$k$ ids and weights, which
are a few bytes per token next to a hidden vector of 7168 values. On 4 gloo processes, both this
scheme and SGLang's below reproduce the single-device MoE output exactly; gloo has no
reduce-scatter, so the check used an all-reduce followed by taking the rank's slice, which gives
the same result.</Sidenote>

### Inside the fused MoE kernel

Megatron permutes the tokens into a contiguous buffer before its grouped GEMM. vLLM's Triton
kernel doesn't move them at all. `moe_align_block_size` only builds an index: the copies sorted by
expert, each expert's group padded to a multiple of the kernel's block size `BLOCK_M`, and the
expert of every block. The kernel launches one program per block, which loads its expert's weights
and reads its token rows from the input by index.

With expert parallelism, `expert_map` maps each global expert to its local index, or to $-1$ if
the expert lives on another rank, and those copies are dropped from the index. Here it is for two
ranks of the example, with `BLOCK_M` = 4 (real values are tuned per GPU and shape, typically 16 to
128). The copies are numbered $2t + j$ for token $t$'s $j$-th choice, so there are 16 of them, and
16 is used as the padding value:

| Rank | Its experts | `sorted_token_ids` | Blocks and their experts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | E0, E1 | 4, 16, 16, 16 · 0, 12, 16, 16 | E0, E1 |
| 1 | E2, E3 | 1, 2, 8, 15 · 3, 7, 11, 13 | E2, E3 |

Check one entry: copy 12 is $t = 6$, $j = 0$, and token 6 chose expert 1 first, so it sits in rank
0's E1 block. Rank 0's two experts get one and two copies, so six of its eight slots are padding,
while rank 1's eight copies fill both blocks.

The rest of `fused_experts_impl` is two calls of the same kernel: the first GEMM with $W_1$ (the gate and up projections side by side) into a
`[tokens, top-k, N]` buffer, SiLU-and-multiply, then the second GEMM with $W_2$, which multiplies
each row by its gate weight and writes it at the copy's own `[token, j]` position. `moe_sum` then
adds each token's $k$ rows. Blocks of other ranks' experts are never launched, and their rows stay
zero, which is exactly what the reduce-scatter needs.

### All-to-all backends

For larger deployments, `--all2all-backend` selects a real all-to-all. vLLM's **modular kernel**
splits the layer into a `prepare` step (quantize, then dispatch), an `experts` step (the GEMMs) and
a `finalize` step (apply the gates, sum, combine), so any communication backend can be paired with
any expert kernel whose input format it produces:

| Backend | What the experts receive, and when to use it |
| --- | --- |
| `allgather_reducescatter` | the default: every token on every rank; one node |
| `deepep_high_throughput` | contiguous rows grouped by expert; multi-node prefill |
| `deepep_low_latency` | fixed slabs `[local experts, max tokens, h]`; decode, CUDA graphs |
| `flashinfer_nvlink_*` | contiguous rows; multi-node NVLink systems |

The two DeepEP modes differ in layout. High-throughput mode, like Megatron, returns exactly the
received rows, grouped by expert, with a count per expert; the counts change every step. Low-latency
mode returns a fixed-size buffer with one slab per local expert and a count of valid rows in each.
The shape never changes, so a decode step can be captured in a CUDA graph, at the price of padding
every expert's slab to the worst case.

Two more features matter at scale. `--enable-dbo` (**dual batch overlap**) splits a batch into two
micro-batches and runs one's all-to-all while the other computes, for batches of more than 32
tokens if they are all decodes, or more than 512 if they contain a prefill. `--enable-eplb` turns on the expert-parallel load balancer, covered
[below](#expert-parallel-load-balancing-eplb).

## SGLang's MoE layer

SGLang's MoE layer has the same shape, a router, a **dispatcher** and an **MoE runner**, with the
communication backend chosen by `--moe-a2a-backend` and the expert kernels by
`--moe-runner-backend`. What differs is the default.

### The default: no dispatch at all

With `--tp 4 --ep 4`, attention is tensor-parallel, and it ends with an all-reduce, so all four
ranks already hold the same batch when the MoE layer starts. There is nothing to send. The default
`--moe-a2a-backend none` uses a `StandardDispatcher` that only renames experts: its
`local_expert_mapping` turns this rank's experts into 0 and 1 and every other expert into $-1$.
Each rank runs its local experts on the whole batch, and the partial outputs are added by an
all-reduce over the TP group, exactly the all-reduce a tensor-parallel MLP would end with.

<MoeEpSteps flow="sglang" title="SGLang's default EP path: EP = 4, 8 experts, top-2, 8 tokens" />

Here every rank routes all 8 tokens, the same work four times, which is cheap next to the experts.
A ring all-reduce of the `[8, h]` output sends $2(N-1)/N \times 8 = 12$ rows per rank, the same as
vLLM's all-gather and reduce-scatter, and for the same reason: with replicated tokens, an
all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather. This is also the only mode in which
SGLang allows the EP group to be smaller than the TP group: with `--tp 8 --ep 2`, say, each expert
is split by tensor parallelism over 4 GPUs, and the same all-reduce adds both kinds of partial
sum.<Sidenote>With data-parallel attention (`--attn-dp-size`, which replaced
`--enable-dp-attention`), each attention-DP group holds its own requests, and SGLang first gathers
them into one global buffer, either with an all-gather or with an all-reduce over a zero-filled
buffer in which each group has written its own rows, and scatters the result back after the MoE.
That is the same pattern as vLLM's default path.</Sidenote>

### DeepEP and two-batch overlap

`--moe-a2a-backend deepep` replaces the dispatcher with DeepEP, which requires `ep_size` to equal
`tp_size`, and pairs naturally with `--moe-runner-backend deep_gemm` for FP8 models. DeepEP has the
same two modes as in vLLM, `normal` with a contiguous layout and a count per expert, and
`low_latency` with the fixed `[local experts, max tokens, h]` layout and a `masked_m` tensor of
valid rows. The difference is that SGLang can switch between them at run time: with
`--deepep-mode auto`, the default, a batch that contains any prefill (an "extend" batch) uses
normal mode, and a pure decode batch uses low-latency mode, so one server gets both.

To hide the all-to-alls, `--enable-two-batch-overlap` (**TBO**) splits each batch into two
micro-batches and interleaves them at yield points in the layer, so that one micro-batch's
attention or experts run while the other's dispatch or combine is in flight. Single-batch overlap
(`--enable-single-batch-overlap`) instead hooks into the dispatcher to run the shared expert during
the combine of the same batch. `--enable-eplb` re-places the experts from their recorded loads, as
[the next section](#expert-parallel-load-balancing-eplb) describes.

| | Megatron-LM | vLLM | SGLang |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Default comm. | all-to-all | all-gather, reduce-scatter | all-reduce |
| Who routes | own tokens | own tokens | every rank, all tokens |
| Expert kernel | grouped GEMM on permuted rows | Triton, rows read by index | Triton by default |
| At scale | DeepEP, HybridEP | DeepEP, one mode per deployment | DeepEP, mode per batch |
| Overlap | across micro-batches | dual batch | two-batch, single-batch |

## Expert-parallel load balancing (EPLB)

The auxiliary loss and DeepSeek's bias balance the experts during training, over the training
data. A deployed model sees whatever its users send: a service that mostly answers coding
questions leans on different experts than one that mostly chats, and nothing can be retrained
while it serves. In the figures above, rank 1 holds the two popular experts and does four times
the work of rank 3. The fix has to happen on the hardware side, and both vLLM and SGLang use the
same one, DeepSeek's **expert-parallel load balancer** (EPLB): record how many tokens each expert
gets, then periodically re-place the experts, giving the busiest ones **redundant** copies on other
GPUs so that their tokens can be split.

The vocabulary is the same in both. A **logical expert** is one of the model's experts. A
**physical expert** is one set of its weights on one GPU, a slot. With $E$ logical experts, $R$
redundant ones and $N$ GPUs, each GPU has $(E + R)/N$ slots. DeepSeek-V3's own deployment uses 32
redundant experts for prefill on 32 GPUs, so 9 slots per GPU, and recomputes the placement about
every ten minutes.

### The placement algorithm

EPLB uses two greedy building blocks. The first gives out copies, one at a time, always to the
expert with the highest load per copy:

```python
for i in range(num_log, num_phy):
    redundant_indices = (weight / logcnt).max(dim=-1).indices
    phy2log[:, i] = redundant_indices
    rank[:, i] = logcnt[arangen, redundant_indices]
    logcnt[arangen, redundant_indices] += 1
```

Each copy is assumed to take an equal share of its expert's tokens, so an expert with load 183
and two copies becomes two items of load 91.5. The second building block, `balanced_packing`,
packs items into bins that must each hold the same number of items: sort the items by load,
heaviest first, and put each one into the lightest bin that still has room. That is the classic
longest-processing-time heuristic, with an extra constraint, because every GPU has the same number
of expert slots.

The **hierarchical** policy chains them in three steps, to respect DeepSeek-V3's node-limited
routing:

1. **Pack groups onto nodes.** The router picks experts from a few *groups* of experts, and on
   DeepSeek-V3 the groups are the nodes. EPLB moves whole groups onto nodes, balancing the nodes'
   total load, so that a token's experts still sit on at most as many nodes as the router allows.
2. **Replicate within each node.** Each node gets its share of the copies, and gives them to its
   hottest experts.
3. **Pack the copies onto the node's GPUs**, balancing the GPUs within the node.

The **global** policy skips the grouping: it replicates over all experts and packs the copies onto
all GPUs at once. DeepSeek uses hierarchical placement for prefill, where the EP group is small and
spans few nodes, and global placement for decode, where EP is large enough that it spans many
nodes anyway.

Here is the example from EPLB's README: two MoE layers of 12 experts in 4 groups, with 4 redundant
copies per layer, so 16 physical experts on 2 nodes of 4 GPUs each. The figure runs a TypeScript
port of `eplb.py` that produces the same placements as the original:

<EplbPlacement title="EPLB on 12 experts, 16 copies, 2 nodes × 4 GPUs" />

Take layer 0. The GPUs' mean load is 129, but expert 10 alone has load 183, so without copies some
GPU would carry at least 1.42 times the mean, however the experts were arranged. EPLB gives
experts 10, 5, 1 and 4 a second copy, and the busiest GPU drops to 1.21 times the mean.

The hierarchical policy can't do better than that here, because groups can't be split across
nodes. The four groups' loads are 262, 330, 116 and 325, and the best split onto two nodes is
446 against 587. Switch to the global policy and the imbalance falls to 1.07, at the price of
letting each group's experts spread across both nodes, which means more traffic between nodes. On
layer 1, the global policy happens to put both copies of expert 8 on GPU 6. The greedy packing
doesn't forbid it, though two copies on one GPU balance nothing.

On this post's own example, the 8 experts of the EP = 4 figures got 1, 2, 4, 4, 1, 2, 1 and 1
copies, and the ranks did 3, 8, 3 and 2 copies' worth of work. With no extra slots, EPLB can only
move experts around: it pairs each popular expert with a quiet one and gets the ranks to 5, 5, 3
and 3. With 4 redundant slots, 3 per rank, it gives expert 2 three copies and experts 1 and 3 two
each, and the ranks' loads become 4.3, 4, 4 and 3.7, against a mean of 4.<Sidenote>Computed with
both vLLM's `DefaultEplbPolicy` and the TypeScript port, which agree: the four ranks hold experts
[3, 2, 1], [5, 0, 6], [3, 1, 7] and [2, 2, 4]. Rank 3 holds two of expert 2's three copies, the same
quirk as layer 1 above.</Sidenote>

### Picking a replica

A placement also changes routing. The router still picks logical experts, and something has to
turn each pick into one of that expert's physical copies, in a way that splits the tokens evenly,
since that is what the placement assumed. vLLM and SGLang do this differently.

**vLLM** does it in a small Triton kernel right after the top-$k$, which also records the load:

```python
replica_idx = (token_idx * 2654435769) % 2**32 % logical_replica_count[expert_id]  # Knuth hash
physical_id = logical_to_physical_map[expert_id, replica_idx]
expert_load_pass[physical_id] += 1  # atomic add, padding tokens skipped
```

A multiplicative hash of the token's position in the batch spreads consecutive tokens over the
replicas, and unlike a random pick it is deterministic, so the same batch is always routed the same
way.

**SGLang** offers two choices, set by `--ep-dispatch-algorithm`. With `static`, each rank has a
table that sends each logical expert to its nearest copy: one on the same GPU if there is one, else
one on the same node if same-node preference is on, else a fair random pick fixed when the table
is built. With `dynamic`, each token picks a copy afresh: at random with an all-to-all backend, and
by token row with the default `none` backend, for the same reason as vLLM's hash. If you enable
EPLB without choosing, SGLang picks `static` with an all-to-all backend and `dynamic` without one,
and refuses `static` with the `none` backend, because ranks that pick different copies of the
same expert would count it twice in the all-reduce.

### EPLB in vLLM

`--enable-eplb` requires `--enable-expert-parallel`, and is configured with `--eplb-config`:

| Setting | Meaning (default) |
| --- | --- |
| `num_redundant_experts` | extra physical experts in total; with 0, only re-placement (0) |
| `window_size` | engine steps of load kept in a sliding window (1000) |
| `step_interval` | steps between rearrangements (3000) |
| `use_async` | move the weights in the background (true) |
| `communicator` | library for the weight transfers: `torch_nccl`, `pynccl`, `nixl`, … (auto) |

Every rearrangement sums the window, maps physical loads back to logical experts, and all-reduces
them over the EP group, so every rank computes the same placement. It uses the hierarchical policy
when the expert groups divide evenly over the nodes, and the global one otherwise. vLLM then
reorders each GPU's new experts so that any expert that stays on a GPU keeps its old slot, and only
the experts that really move are sent, with point-to-point transfers between GPUs. In the default
async mode a background thread copies them into a buffer while serving continues, and the new maps
are committed once every rank has its weights.

Redundant experts cost memory that would otherwise hold KV cache: vLLM's documentation puts one
redundant expert per rank at about 2.4 GB for DeepSeek-V3, its 58 MoE layers × three
$7168 \times 2048$ FP8 matrices.

### EPLB in SGLang

`--enable-eplb` with `--ep-num-redundant-experts`, which also defaults to 0. A recorder counts the
tokens each expert receives in a circular buffer, and every `--eplb-rebalance-num-iterations`
forward passes, 1000 by default, an `EPLBManager` recomputes the placement, unless the measured GPU
utilization is already above `--eplb-min-rebalancing-utilization-threshold` (1.0 by default, so it
always rebalances). `--eplb-algorithm auto` chooses DeepSeek's hierarchical algorithm when the
groups divide over the nodes and the global one otherwise; there is also a vectorized version and an
`elasticity_aware` one for elastic EP, which keeps experts off ranks that have failed. The expert
weights then move with batched point-to-point sends and receives, optionally a few layers per
forward pass (`--eplb-rebalance-layers-per-chunk`) to spread the pause.

SGLang can also skip the online part: `--init-expert-location` takes a `.json` or `.pt` file with
either a ready placement (`physical_to_logical_map`) or recorded per-expert token counts
(`logical_count`), from which it computes one at startup. That
is closest to what DeepSeek describes for its own deployment, a placement computed from the recent
load and refreshed periodically rather than continuously.
