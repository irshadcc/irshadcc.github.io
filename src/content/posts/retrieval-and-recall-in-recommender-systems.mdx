## Background

Suppose a video service has ten million playable videos. Its best ranking model needs a
millisecond to score one video. Running it over the catalog would take almost three hours for one
request. The feed has about 100 milliseconds.

Large recommenders solve this with a cascade. **Retrieval**, also called **candidate generation**
or **recall**, cheaply finds perhaps a thousand plausible items. Later models spend more compute
ordering that small set. Retrieval matters because ranking cannot recover an item it never sees:
the best possible ranker is bounded by the candidates that reach it.

This post first makes “recall” precise. It then derives the main retrieval algorithms—popularity
and content rules, item-to-item collaborative filtering, matrix factorization and two-tower
models, sequence and multi-interest models, graph methods, and tree-based retrieval. Finally, it
explains approximate nearest-neighbor indexes, training and evaluation, and how YouTube, Amazon,
Twitter, Pinterest and Tmall combined these ideas in published systems. For how their large ID
tables are stored during training, see [Storing an Embedding Table in a HashMap](/blog/embedding-table-hashmap/).

Let the eligible catalog for request $u$ be $I_u$, and let an ideal but too-expensive relevance
function be $r(u,i)$. Retrieval must return a set $C_K(u) \subset I_u$ of at most $K$ candidates
under a latency and memory budget. Ranking then orders only $C_K(u)$.

That contract creates three separate design problems, a useful decomposition made explicit by the
2024 survey *A Comprehensive Survey on Retrieval Methods in Recommender Systems*:

1. **Matching:** how do we estimate that user $u$ and item $i$ belong together?
2. **Indexing:** how do we find high-scoring items without evaluating every $i \in I_u$?
3. **Training:** which positives, negatives and loss teach the matching function what serving
   actually needs?

A model can improve matching but be impossible to index. An index can be fast but lose the best
neighbors. A loss can look good offline while learning exposure bias. Production retrieval is the
joint design of all three.

### Three meanings of recall

The word **recall** is overloaded:

- The *recall stage* is the production stage this post calls retrieval.
- **Recall@$K$** is an offline relevance metric. If $R_u$ is the set of relevant held-out items,
  then

  $$
  \operatorname{Recall@K}(u)=\frac{|C_K(u)\cap R_u|}{|R_u|}.
  $$

- **ANN recall@$K$** measures an approximate index against exact vector search. If $E_K(u)$ is
  the exact top-$K$ under the same vector score and $A_K(u)$ is the approximate result, then

  $$
  \operatorname{ANNRecall@K}(u)=\frac{|A_K(u)\cap E_K(u)|}{K}.
  $$

The last metric says whether the index reproduced its model. It says nothing about whether the
model retrieved items a person wants. A system may have 99% ANN recall and poor relevance recall,
or the reverse.

There is another measurement trap. With one held-out positive per user, Recall@$K$ is either zero
or one and is numerically identical to HitRate@$K$. With several relevant items, hit rate only asks
whether at least one was found; recall measures the fraction found. Always state the evaluation
protocol with the number.

### A ten-item worked example

Take a two-dimensional user vector $q=(0.8,0.2)$ and ten item vectors. For unit-normalized vectors,
cosine similarity and dot product induce the same order. The exact scores, computed rather than
hand-rounded, are:

| Rank | Item | Cosine score | Relevant? |
| ---: | --- | ---: | :---: |
| 1 | road shoes | 0.9996 | yes |
| 2 | socks | 0.9972 | yes |
| 3 | trail shoes | 0.9910 | yes |
| 4 | sandals | 0.9908 | yes |
| 5 | hiking pack | 0.9841 | yes |
| 6 | tent | 0.9487 | yes |
| 7 | cookbook | 0.5855 | no |
| 8 | dumbbells | 0.5369 | no |
| 9 | headphones | 0.3610 | no |
| 10 | yoga mat | 0.3482 | no |

There are six relevant items. Exact Recall@3 is $3/6=0.500$; Recall@5 is $5/6=0.833$; Recall@6 is
$6/6=1.000$. Check one score by hand: road shoes has $v=(0.82,0.18)$, so

$$
\cos(q,v)=\frac{0.8(0.82)+0.2(0.18)}
{\sqrt{0.8^2+0.2^2}\sqrt{0.82^2+0.18^2}}=0.9996.
$$

If an approximate index returns `road shoes, socks, trail shoes, hiking pack, sandals` at $K=5$,
its relevance Recall@5 remains $5/6=0.833$, but its ANN recall is also $5/5=1$: order does not
matter for set recall. If it replaces sandals with cookbook, relevance Recall@5 and ANNRecall@5
both fall to $4/6=0.667$ and $4/5=0.800$, respectively. The denominators differ.

## Retrieval is usually an ensemble

One model rarely covers fresh items, sparse users, long-term taste, current intent and policy at
once. Industrial systems run **candidate channels** in parallel and take a controlled union. The
figure shows a common arrangement; each branch fails differently, which is why their union is
more useful than a larger result from one branch.

In the worked example, suppose an item-to-item channel finds `trail shoes, road shoes, sandals,
socks`, while a content channel finds `hiking pack, tent, trail shoes`. Each has recall $4/6$ and
$3/6$. Their deduplicated union has all six relevant items and recall $1.0$. This is the central
reason to inspect *marginal* recall—the new positives a channel contributes after earlier channels
have run—not only its standalone recall.

The merge needs quotas or score calibration. Scores from different channels are not naturally
comparable: a cosine similarity of 0.8 is not the same thing as an item co-occurrence count of 80.
Systems commonly reserve per-channel quotas, normalize scores, learn a lightweight blender, or let
the pre-ranker score the union. They also over-fetch before eligibility filters; otherwise a
nearest-neighbor query that returns 100 blocked or unavailable items may leave fewer than 100
candidates.

## The algorithms

### Popularity, recency and content

The simplest channel keeps top-item lists by context: trending videos in a country, popular shoes
in a category, or fresh posts from followed creators. A decayed popularity score might be

$$
s(i,t)=\sum_{e\in E_i} w(e)\exp[-\lambda(t-t_e)],
$$

where event $e$ occurred at $t_e$ and $w(e)$ gives a purchase more weight than a view. A heap or
sorted cache per segment makes online retrieval a lookup. The half-life is
$t_{1/2}=\ln 2/\lambda$: with a six-hour half-life, an event contributes one quarter of its
original weight after 12 hours.

This channel is fast, fresh and robust for anonymous users. It is also self-reinforcing: exposure
creates interactions, which raise popularity, which creates more exposure. Segmenting, time decay,
exploration slots and exposure-corrected training reduce—but do not erase—that loop.

**Content retrieval** embeds item text, images, audio or metadata and searches for items similar
to a query or a user's recent items. It is especially valuable for a new item with no interaction
history. Its weakness is that semantic resemblance is not the same as collaborative taste: two
products can look alike but be substitutes that users rarely want together, or look different but
be complements.

### Item-to-item collaborative filtering

**Collaborative filtering** infers preference from collective behavior rather than item content.
For item-to-item CF, make one sparse vector $x_i$ per item, with a coordinate for each user. A
binary coordinate says whether the user interacted; a weighted coordinate can encode event type,
frequency or time decay. Cosine similarity is

$$
\operatorname{sim}(i,j)=\frac{x_i^\top x_j}{\lVert x_i\rVert_2\lVert x_j\rVert_2}.
$$

For every item in user $u$'s history $H_u$, look up its precomputed neighbor list $N(i)$ and add
the evidence:

$$
s(u,j)=\sum_{i\in H_u}w(u,i)\operatorname{sim}(i,j),\qquad j\in\bigcup_{i\in H_u}N(i).
$$

The index is an item ID to a short neighbor list, so online work depends on history length and
neighbors per item, not the catalog size. Amazon's published 2003 system emphasized this scaling
property: compute item similarities offline, then combine the neighbors of items in the shopper's
cart or history online.

Raw co-occurrence $c_{ij}$ over-recommends popular items. Cosine normalization divides by
$\sqrt{c_i c_j}$. Other choices include Jaccard $c_{ij}/(c_i+c_j-c_{ij})$, pointwise mutual
information, shrinkage toward zero for small $c_{ij}$, and down-weighting prolific users. The
algorithm remains easy to debug—“because you viewed $i$”—but cannot reach an item disconnected
from the user's history and needs a plan for new users and new items.

### Matrix factorization

Let $X_{ui}$ be an implicit interaction matrix. **Matrix factorization** learns a user vector
$p_u\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and item vector $q_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ so their dot product predicts affinity:

$$
\hat y_{ui}=p_u^\top q_i+b_u+b_i.
$$

For implicit data, one common objective is weighted squared error over observed and unobserved
pairs; another is Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR):

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{BPR}}=-\sum_{(u,i,j)}
\log \sigma(p_u^\top q_i-p_u^\top q_j)+\lambda\lVert\Theta\rVert_2^2,
$$

where $i$ is a positive item and $j$ a sampled unobserved item. BPR asks only that the positive
score higher than the negative. Once learned, maximum inner-product search over $q_i$ retrieves
candidates.

Factorization compresses the sparse interaction graph into latent dimensions and can find items
that share no direct co-occurrence with a user's latest item. Plain ID factors, however, cannot
compute a vector for an unseen user or item. Feature encoders lead to the modern generalization.

### Two-tower retrieval

A **two-tower model** separately encodes the request and item:

$$
z_u=f_\theta(\text{history, profile, context}),\qquad
z_i=g_\phi(\text{ID, content, metadata}),\qquad
s(u,i)=z_u^\top z_i.
$$

The separation is the important constraint. Item vectors can be computed offline and indexed;
only $f_\theta$ runs per request. Arbitrary cross-attention between a user history and each item
would be more expressive, but would destroy this factorization and require a catalog scan. The
retriever spends expressiveness to buy indexability; the ranker restores rich cross-features on a
small candidate set.

YouTube's 2016 candidate generator cast the next watch as extreme multiclass classification. Given
the user's watch and search history plus context, a network produced a user vector; each video ID
was an output-class vector. Full softmax over millions of videos was too expensive, so training
sampled thousands of negative classes and corrected for the sampling distribution.

For a batch of $B$ observed user-item pairs, **in-batch softmax** reuses the other $B-1$ items as
negatives:

$$
\mathcal L=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\log\frac{\exp(s(u_b,i_b)/\tau)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{B}\exp(s(u_b,i_k)/\tau)}.
$$

The temperature $\tau$ sets how strongly score differences affect the probability. In-batch
negatives are computationally efficient, but the batch sampling distribution biases the loss
toward popular items and can create **false negatives**—items the user would like but happened not
to consume. Correcting logits for sampling probability, mixing random with hard negatives, and
removing known positives are common remedies.

Negative choice changes the question learned by the model:

| Negative source | What it teaches | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniform catalog | Coarse separation from arbitrary items | Mostly too easy |
| Popularity sampled | Distinguish plausible popular inventory | Reinforces exposure distribution |
| In-batch | Many negatives with one matrix multiply | Biased by batch composition; false negatives |
| ANN hard negatives | Separate near misses around the decision boundary | Stale index; mislabeled relevant items |
| Shown but skipped | Distinguish items the user had a chance to choose | Position and presentation bias |

Training-serving consistency also matters. If training compares a positive against random items
but serving compares it against its nearest embedding neighbors, the model has not practiced its
hardest decision. Periodically mining hard negatives with the serving index narrows that gap.

### Sequence and multi-interest retrieval

A mean of history embeddings is orderless. A **sequential retriever** uses a recurrent network or
Transformer to make $z_u$ depend on order, time gaps and the current session. It can represent the
transition from “camera” to “memory card,” not only a bag of long-term interests. The final vector
still enters a dot-product index, so the result remains retrievable.

One vector can be a bottleneck when a person shops for running shoes, reads science fiction and
watches cooking videos. Alibaba's **Multi-Interest Network with Dynamic routing (MIND)** produces
$K$ user vectors. Starting from behavior embeddings $e_i$, it iterates:

$$
w_{ij}=\operatorname{softmax}_j(b_{ij}),\qquad
z_j=\sum_i w_{ij}Se_i,\qquad
u_j=\operatorname{squash}(z_j),
$$

$$
b_{ij}\leftarrow b_{ij}+u_j^\top Se_i.
$$

The agreement update raises the assignment of behavior $i$ to interest capsule $j$ when their
vectors align. Repeating it performs a soft clustering of behaviors. During training, the positive
target chooses the relevant interest through label-aware attention; during serving, every $u_j$
issues an ANN query and the results are merged. MIND reported retrieving thousands of items from a
billion-scale pool in under 15 ms before sending the top 1,000 to ranking at Mobile Tmall.

Multiple vectors improve coverage but multiply index queries and create a quota question. Taking
100 neighbors for each of eight interests may return far fewer than 800 unique items, and a noisy
interest can consume its full quota. Adaptive interest counts, per-interest budgets and
deduplication are part of the algorithm, not mere plumbing.

### Graph walks and graph neural networks

Represent interactions as a bipartite graph with users on one side and items on the other. A
two-hop path user $u\rightarrow$ item $i\rightarrow$ user $v$ finds someone who touched the same
item; one more hop reaches items touched by $v$. A random-walk recommender repeatedly samples these
edges and counts destination items. Degree-aware transition probabilities or a restart probability
keep the walk local and reduce domination by hubs.

Twitter's **GraphJet** maintained a real-time, in-memory user–tweet interaction graph. Its random
walk with restart used the query user's recent edges as seeds, visited similar users, and gathered
their tweets. Because new edges were immediately visible to concurrent readers, the candidate
channel captured breaking interactions that a batch embedding job would miss.

**PinSage** turned Pinterest's pin–board graph into item embeddings. For each pin it used short
random walks to select important local neighbors, transformed their features, pooled them with
importance weights, concatenated the result with the pin's own representation, and normalized the
output. Stacking these convolutions carried information across multiple hops. Pinterest reported
training on 7.5 billion examples from a graph with 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges, then using
the embeddings for nearest-neighbor retrieval.

Graph walks preserve very recent collaborative structure; graph neural networks amortize graph and
content information into indexable vectors. The former needs a low-latency graph store, while the
latter needs neighborhood sampling and embedding refreshes. Both must handle high-degree nodes and
feedback loops.

### Tree-based retrieval

Vector search restricts the score to a geometry an ANN index understands. **Tree-based deep
models** instead assign items to leaves of a tree and train a classifier for each internal routing
decision. At serving time, beam search keeps the $B$ most promising nodes at one level, expands
their children, and repeats until it reaches leaves.

For a balanced tree with branching factor $b$ and $N$ items, the depth is about $\log_b N$ and a
beam evaluates roughly $Bb\log_b N$ nodes rather than $N$ items. Unlike a dot product, the node
scorer may use richer features. The cost is search error: pruning the right subtree near the root
loses all of its items.

The tree is part of the model. Alibaba's TDM work used a max-heap-like target in which a parent's
preference reflected its best child, then retrieved leaves with layer-wise beam search. Later work
jointly optimized item placement and the prediction model because a semantic clustering objective
for the tree need not produce the best recommendation routing decisions.

Recent **generative retrieval** replaces the explicit tree walk with an autoregressive model that
generates an item's discrete identifier token by token. Each prefix is effectively a tree node;
beam search over tokens retrieves IDs. It can share sequence-model machinery and condition on rich
context, but identifier construction, constrained decoding, catalog updates and invalid IDs make it
less operationally mature than vector retrieval. It is best understood as learned index traversal,
not as making retrieval disappear.

## Indexes: making a score searchable

With $N$ item vectors of dimension $d$, exact search costs $O(Nd)$ dot-product work per query. A
GPU can make that baseline surprisingly competitive for moderate catalogs or large query batches,
and exact results are invaluable as an evaluation oracle. At larger scale or tight tail latency,
an **approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN)** index trades some model recall for speed and memory.

### Inverted files and product quantization

An **inverted file (IVF)** clusters item vectors into $L$ coarse cells. At query time it finds the
closest `nprobe` centroids and scans only their posting lists. Raising `nprobe` increases both ANN
recall and work. The failure mode is a boundary: a true neighbor may sit in an unprobed adjacent
cell.

**Product quantization (PQ)** splits a $d$-dimensional vector into $m$ subvectors. Each subvector is
replaced by the ID of its nearest small codeword. With 256 codewords, one subvector costs one byte;
the database stores $m$ bytes per item instead of $4d$ bytes of float32. A query builds $m$
distance tables and sums table entries for each compressed item. IVF-PQ first prunes cells, then
scores compact codes, often re-ranking a short list with original vectors. Quantization saves
memory bandwidth but distorts scores. ScaNN's anisotropic quantization weights error parallel to a
database vector more heavily because that component changes the inner product more.

### HNSW

**Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)** builds a proximity graph with sparse upper layers
and a denser bottom layer. Search starts at an entry point on the highest layer, greedily moves to
a neighbor closer to the query, descends a layer, and finally runs a best-first search at layer
zero. Long upper-layer links cross the space; short bottom-layer links refine the answer.

`M`, the maximum neighborhood size, trades memory and build time for connectivity. `efSearch`, the
candidate queue size at query time, trades latency for ANN recall. HNSW is accurate and does not
require a trained quantizer, but the graph adds pointer-heavy memory overhead; filtering and rapid
deletion are also harder than a simple posting list.

There is no universally best index. Benchmark on the model's actual vectors and filters, report
p50 and p99 latency, index size, build/refresh time, and ANN recall against exact top-$K$. Random
public vectors may have very different density and norm distributions.

## What published systems actually used

These systems were published in different years and products; the table is evidence of design
patterns, not a current inventory of each company's private stack.

| System | Published retrieval solution | Why it fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amazon, 2003 | Offline item-to-item similarities; online aggregation from a shopper's items | Online work scaled independently of total customers and catalog size |
| YouTube, 2016 | Deep user encoder trained as sampled multiclass classification; hundreds of candidates plus other sources | Learned broad collaborative signals over millions of videos, then left fine discrimination to ranking |
| Twitter GraphJet, 2016 | Real-time bipartite graph and random-walk recommendations | Newly created tweets and interactions needed to become retrievable immediately |
| Pinterest PinSage, 2018 | Random-walk neighborhood sampling plus graph convolutions; nearest-neighbor lookup of pin embeddings | Combined visual/content features with a multi-billion-node pin–board graph |
| Alibaba TDM, 2018–2019 | Learned tree index and layer-wise beam search with a deep node scorer | Allowed sublinear retrieval without requiring the score to be a dot product |
| Tmall MIND, 2019 | Multiple user-interest vectors, one ANN query per interest, merged before ranking | A single vector blurred a shopper's unrelated interests |

Three lessons recur. First, the deployed system is an ensemble; even YouTube's diagram includes
other candidate sources. Second, the data structure determines which model is serveable. Third,
freshness is architectural: GraphJet updated a live graph, while embedding systems need incremental
item encoders, frequent index builds or delta indexes.

## Training the retriever you intend to serve

### Build examples without leaking the future

For next-item training, choose an event at time $t$ as the label and use only events before $t$ as
features. A random interaction split can put a user's future behavior into the training history
used to predict their past. A temporal split is closer to deployment and also exposes catalog
churn.

An interaction is not automatically preference. A click depends on whether an item was exposed,
its position and thumbnail. Non-interaction with an unshown item is missing data, not a clean
negative. Impression logs support shown-but-skipped negatives and inverse-propensity weighting,
though propensity estimates can themselves be noisy.

### Match the objective to the product

Clicks are abundant but can reward clickbait. Watch time, completion, saves, purchases and
long-term return represent different utilities. Multi-task training can share representations
while predicting several outcomes; serving can retrieve for one composite value or run separate
channels. The label horizon matters too: a model trained only on immediate next clicks may forget
durable interests.

Sampled softmax estimates a probability over items; pairwise losses optimize positive-versus-
negative order; contrastive losses organize the embedding space. None directly optimizes the final
page after filtering, ranking and re-ranking. That mismatch is why offline improvements need online
experiments.

### Keep item and index versions aligned

If training changes $g_\phi$, every item vector changes. Serving a new user tower against an old
item index creates a coordinate-system mismatch even when dimensions agree. Version both towers
and the index as one artifact, roll them out atomically, and monitor score distributions. For new
items between full builds, encode them into a small mutable delta index and query both main and
delta indexes.

## Evaluating a retrieval system

Offline evaluation should answer different questions at different layers:

| Layer | Useful measurements | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matching model, exact search | Recall@$K$, HitRate@$K$, NDCG@$K$, coverage | Did the learned score put relevant items near the top? |
| ANN index | ANN recall@$K$, p50/p99 latency, QPS, memory, build time | Did acceleration preserve the model's top neighbors cheaply enough? |
| Channel ensemble | Union recall, marginal recall, overlap, source share | Does each source add candidates the others miss? |
| Post-filter set | Valid count, filter-loss rate, category/creator coverage | Did policy and availability erase useful candidates? |
| Online product | CTR, watch time, conversion, retention, complaints, diversity | Did the whole system improve user and business outcomes? |

NDCG gives more credit when a relevant item appears earlier and supports graded relevance:

$$
\operatorname{DCG@K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{2^{rel_k}-1}{\log_2(k+1)},
\qquad
\operatorname{NDCG@K}=\frac{\operatorname{DCG@K}}{\operatorname{IDCG@K}}.
$$

Retrieval often cares more about set coverage than fine ordering because a ranker follows, but the
retriever's order still matters when a fixed quota truncates results.

Slice every metric. New users, long-history users, new items, regions, languages and tail creators
can move in opposite directions. Also plot recall against candidate count and latency rather than
reporting one arbitrary $K$. A channel that reaches the same recall with half the candidates saves
ranking compute.

Finally, replay evaluation only judges items that the logging policy exposed. A new retriever may
surface unseen inventory with no labels. Exploration traffic, interleaving or an A/B test is needed
to measure that counterfactual. YouTube's paper explicitly notes that live results did not always
correlate with offline experiments.

## Choosing a design

Start with the simplest channel whose failure mode you can measure:

| Situation | Good first choice | Add when needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small catalog | Exact scoring, popularity and business rules | ANN only after profiling proves the scan is too slow |
| Strong basket/session signal | Item-to-item CF | Sequence tower for order and context |
| Rich user and item features | Two-tower model + exact or ANN search | Hard-negative mining; multi-interest vectors |
| Interaction graph changes by the second | Online graph walk | GNN embeddings for broader, feature-aware generalization |
| Severe new-item cold start | Content encoder and fresh-item channel | Distill collaborative embeddings into the content tower |
| Dot product is too restrictive | Tree retrieval or a wider pre-ranker | Generative retrieval only with a catalog/update plan |

A solid first production stack is often less exotic than its model diagram: segmented popular and
fresh lists, item-to-item neighbors, one two-tower ANN channel, strict eligibility filters, and a
ranker over their union. Log source attribution and exact-search samples from day one. Those logs
show whether the next improvement belongs in matching, indexing, training, or simply a missing
candidate channel.

## Summary

- Retrieval is the high-recall, low-cost stage that makes ranking over a huge catalog possible;
  ranking cannot rescue an item that retrieval omitted.
- Keep relevance Recall@$K$, ANN recall, and the “recall stage” distinct. Measure the matching
  model exactly before blaming or tuning the index.
- Item CF, two-tower vectors, sequences, multi-interest capsules, graph walks and trees are not one
  ladder of sophistication. They expose different signals and support different indexes.
- Production systems combine complementary channels, then deduplicate, quota, filter and pre-rank
  their union. Marginal recall reveals whether a channel earns its latency and candidate budget.
- The negative sampler, temporal data split, index version, freshness path and online experiment
  are as much a part of retrieval quality as the encoder architecture.
