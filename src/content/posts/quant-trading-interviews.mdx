## Background

A **quantitative trader** decides what to buy and sell, at what price and in what size, using
numbers rather than hunches. Firms such as Jane Street, Optiver, Susquehanna (SIG), IMC, Citadel
Securities and Hudson River Trading make their money by **market making**: they stand ready to
buy slightly below what something is worth and sell slightly above it, thousands of times a
day. Each trade earns a sliver. The job is to know what "worth" means when the future is
random, and to stay alive when you are wrong.

That job explains the interview. Firms do not test finance trivia; they test whether you can
reason about uncertainty quickly and out loud. Can you find an expected value in your head? Do
you notice when a counterparty knows more than you? Will you bet the right amount when you have
an edge, and not more? Questions about dice, coins and cards are the cheapest way to probe
these skills, so they dominate. Options and statistics follow, because they are the language
of the products and the research.

This post teaches the whole syllabus from first principles. It starts with **what the interview
tests**, then works through the topics in the order they build on each other: **mental maths**,
**probability**, **random walks and martingales**, **betting with an edge (Kelly)**, **market
making**, **options**, **statistics and regression**, **portfolios and risk**, and finally how
to handle **brainteasers, coding and the live interview**. Every section has a worked example
with real numbers and ends with exercises; click "Solution" to check your answer. For the
probability basics in more depth, see [Probability foundations](/blog/probability-foundations/);
for how an exchange matches orders, see [Building an order book in C++](/blog/order-book-cpp/).

#### What interviewers are grading

It helps to know what the person across the table writes down, because it is rarely "got the
right number". Reports from candidates and interviewers converge on five things:

1. **Structure.** Do you turn a vague question into a precise one ("by 'fair price' I mean the
   expected payout")? Do you name the states, the events or the unknowns before computing?
2. **Speed with accuracy.** Traders act in seconds. An answer that is roughly right now beats
   an exact answer in five minutes, *as long as you say it is approximate* and can refine it.
3. **Sanity checks.** Does the answer pass obvious tests? A probability must lie in $[0, 1]$; an
   expected wait for a 3-flip pattern cannot be below 3; a call cannot be worth more than the
   stock.
4. **Updating.** When the interviewer adds information ("the second die is a 6", "I just bought
   ten from you"), do you change your answer, and in the right direction?
5. **Risk sense.** Do you size a bet to your edge and your uncertainty, rather than maximising
   the expected value of one bet?

Every section below trains at least one of these. The exercises are written in interview
style: try to answer aloud in under two minutes before opening the solution.

## Mental maths and estimation

Speed matters because a trader's edge disappears while they reach for a calculator. Interview
arithmetic is not about memorising tables; it is about a handful of tricks that turn hard
products into easy ones.

**Split one factor into a round number plus a correction.** To multiply $37 \times 24$, note
that $24 = 25 - 1$ and $25 = 100 / 4$:

$$
37 \times 24 = 37 \times 25 - 37 = 3700 / 4 - 37 = 925 - 37 = 888.
$$

**Use the difference of squares when two numbers sit either side of a round one.**
$(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2$, so $47 \times 53 = 50^2 - 3^2 = 2500 - 9 = 2491$.

**Square numbers near 50 or 100 by expanding.** $(50 + a)^2 = 2500 + 100a + a^2$, so
$53^2 = 2500 + 300 + 9 = 2809$. Likewise $(100 - a)^2 = 10000 - 200a + a^2$, so
$98^2 = 10000 - 400 + 4 = 9604$.

**Know the common fractions as percentages.** Prices, probabilities and returns arrive as
fractions; converting on sight saves seconds every time.

| Fraction | Percent | Fraction | Percent | Fraction | Percent |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| 1/3 | 33.33 | 1/7 | 14.29 | 1/11 | 9.09 |
| 1/6 | 16.67 | 1/8 | 12.50 | 1/12 | 8.33 |
| 3/8 | 37.50 | 1/9 | 11.11 | 1/16 | 6.25 |
| 5/8 | 62.50 | 2/7 | 28.57 | 1/36 | 2.78 |

**Know the market constants.** A year has about 252 trading days and $\sqrt{252} \approx 15.9$,
so an annual volatility of 16% is about 1% a day ($0.16 / 15.87 = 0.0101$). Money doubles in
about $72 / r$ years at $r$% a year (the **rule of 72**: at 8%, about 9 years).

**Percentages are commutative.** $x\%$ of $y$ equals $y\%$ of $x$, so "17% of 60" is
"60% of 17" $= 10.2$. Pick whichever order is easier.

**Percentage changes do not cancel.** A fall of 50% followed by a rise of 50% leaves
$0.5 \times 1.5 = 0.75$ of the start: a loss of 25%. In general a fall of $x$ needs a rise of
$x/(1 - x)$ to recover: a 20% drawdown needs a 25% gain. This asymmetry is the volatility drag
that reappears in the Kelly section.

**Use first-order expansions for small numbers.** For $|x|$ much less than 1:

- $(1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx + \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}x^2$. Example: $1.02^{10} \approx 1 + 0.2 + 45 \times 0.0004 = 1.218$ (exact 1.219).
- $1/(1 - x) \approx 1 + x$. Example: $1/0.96 \approx 1.04$ (exact 1.0417).
- $\sqrt{a^2 + b} \approx a + b/(2a)$. Example: $\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{49 + 1} \approx 7 + 1/14 = 7.0714$ (exact 7.0711).
- $e^x \approx 1 + x$ and $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$, which is why small percentage returns and log
  returns are nearly equal.

Three logarithms are worth knowing by heart: $\ln 2 = 0.693$, $\ln 10 = 2.303$ and
$\log_{10} 2 = 0.301$. With them, "how many doublings to grow a thousandfold?" is
$\log_2 1000 = 3 / 0.301 \approx 10$ in your head.

#### Number sequences

Optiver's reported assessment adds a **sequences** test: given the start of a sequence, find the
next term. The patterns come from a small family, so check them in order:

1. **Differences.** Write the gaps between terms; if they are constant or form a simple
   pattern, extend them. $4, 7, 12, 19, 28$ has gaps $3, 5, 7, 9$, so the next gap is 11 and the
   answer is 39.
2. **Ratios.** $1, 2, 6, 24, 120$ multiplies by $2, 3, 4, 5$: next is $720$ (factorials).
3. **A formula in $n$.** $2, 6, 12, 20, 30$ is $n(n + 1)$: next is $42$.
4. **A recursion.** $3, 5, 9, 17, 33$ is "double and subtract 1": next is $65$. Fibonacci-like
   sequences add the previous two terms.
5. **Two interleaved sequences.** $1, 10, 2, 20, 3, 30$ alternates $1, 2, 3$ and $10, 20, 30$:
   next is 4.
6. **Special sets.** Primes ($2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 \to 17$), squares, cubes, digits of a number.

If two passes do not reveal a pattern, look at differences of differences, then at
interleaving. Under time pressure, skip and come back.

**Estimate before you compute.** A **Fermi estimate** breaks an unknown quantity into factors
you can guess within a factor of two, then multiplies. Errors in independent guesses partly
cancel, so the product is usually right to an order of magnitude. Interviewers care about the
decomposition and the stated assumptions, not the final digit. For example, "How many piano
tuners work in Chicago?": assume about 3 million people, 1 piano per 20 households of 2.5 people
(60,000 pianos), each tuned once a year, and a tuner who does 4 tunings a day on 250 days
(1,000 a year). That gives $60\,000 / 1\,000 = 60$ tuners: an answer of "about 50 to 100".

**Practice daily.** Timed drills (for example the free Zetamac arithmetic game) build speed in
a few weeks; aim to say every intermediate step aloud, because the live rounds grade the
reasoning too.

## Probability

Probability is the core of every trading interview. Three tools solve almost every question:
**counting**, **conditioning**, and **expected value**, with **first-step analysis** for anything
that unfolds over time.

### Counting

When all outcomes are equally likely, a probability is a ratio of counts: favourable outcomes
over all outcomes. Two counts do most of the work. The number of ordered sequences of $k$ items
from $n$ with repeats is $n^k$. The number of unordered subsets of size $k$ is the **binomial
coefficient** $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$.

**Worked example: three dice sum to 10.** There are $6^3 = 216$ equally likely ordered rolls.
Count the rolls that sum to 10 by the first die $a$: the other two must sum to $10 - a$.
Two dice sum to $s$ in $6 - |s - 7|$ ways (for $2 \le s \le 12$), so

| First die $a$ | Remaining sum | Ways |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 9 | 4 |
| 2 | 8 | 5 |
| 3 | 7 | 6 |
| 4 | 6 | 5 |
| 5 | 5 | 4 |
| 6 | 4 | 3 |

The total is $27$, so $P(\text{sum} = 10) = 27/216 = 1/8$. The figure shows the whole
distribution: it is symmetric about the mean $10.5$, so 10 and 11 tie as the most likely sums.

### Conditional probability and Bayes

The probability of $A$ **given** $B$ restricts the world to outcomes where $B$ happened:
$P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) / P(B)$. Rearranging both ways gives **Bayes' theorem**,

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}.
$$

It turns "how likely is the evidence if the hypothesis is true" into "how likely is the
hypothesis now that I have seen the evidence". Traders use it constantly: every trade against
you is evidence about what the other side knows.

**Worked example: a two-headed coin.** A bag holds one fair coin and one two-headed coin. You
draw one at random and flip it three times: three heads. What is the chance it is the
two-headed coin? Before the flips each coin has probability $1/2$. The two-headed coin produces
three heads with probability 1, the fair coin with probability $1/8$. Bayes gives

$$
P(\text{two-headed} \mid HHH) = \frac{1 \cdot \tfrac12}{1 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac18 \cdot \tfrac12} = \frac{8}{9}.
$$

**Worked example: Monty Hall.** A prize is behind one of three doors. You pick door 1. The
host, who knows where the prize is and always opens a losing door you did not pick, opens door
3. Should you switch to door 2? Condition on where the prize is. If it is behind door 1
(probability $1/3$), switching loses. If it is behind door 2 or 3 (probability $2/3$), the host
is forced to reveal the other losing door, and switching wins. So switching wins with
probability $2/3$. The host's choice carries information because it is not random.

A useful habit: when a conditional question feels slippery, **list the equally likely
outcomes and cross out the ones the condition rules out.** "Two dice, at least one is a 6:
what is the chance the sum is 7?" The condition leaves 11 of 36 outcomes; two of them, (1, 6)
and (6, 1), sum to 7. The answer is $2/11$, not $1/6$.

### Expected value and linearity

The **expected value** of a random variable is its probability-weighted average,
$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, P(X = x)$. It is the fair price of a bet that pays $X$: pay less and
you profit on average, pay more and you lose.

Its most useful property is **linearity**: $\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]$
**even when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.** This lets you split a hard count into easy
**indicator variables**, each 1 when an event happens and 0 otherwise, whose expectation is just
the event's probability.

**Worked example: matching hats.** $n$ people throw their hats in a pile and each takes one at
random. How many get their own hat back, on average? Let $I_k$ be 1 if person $k$ gets their
hat. Each person is equally likely to get any of the $n$ hats, so $\mathbb{E}[I_k] = 1/n$, and

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{n} I_k\Big] = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n} = 1,
$$

for every $n$. The indicators are dependent (if the first $n - 1$ people match, so does the
last), but linearity does not care.

**Worked example: coupon collector.** How many rolls of a die until every face has appeared?
Split the wait into stages. After seeing $k$ distinct faces, each roll shows a new face with
probability $(6 - k)/6$, so the stage lasts $6 / (6 - k)$ rolls on average (a geometric wait).
Summing the stages,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{rolls}] = \frac{6}{6} + \frac{6}{5} + \frac{6}{4} + \frac{6}{3} + \frac{6}{2} + \frac{6}{1} = 6\left(1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac16\right) = 14.7.
$$

**Worked example: the re-roll game.** You roll a die and win its face value in dollars. After
seeing the first roll you may re-roll once and take the second result instead. What is the
game worth? Work backwards. A re-roll is worth $3.5$ on average, so keep any first roll above
$3.5$ (4, 5 or 6) and re-roll 1, 2 or 3. Half the time you keep a roll averaging 5; half the
time you re-roll and get 3.5:

$$
\mathbb{E} = \tfrac12 \cdot 5 + \tfrac12 \cdot 3.5 = 4.25.
$$

With two re-rolls the threshold rises to the new continuation value 4.25: keep 5 or 6, so the
value is $\tfrac13 \cdot 5.5 + \tfrac23 \cdot 4.25 = 14/3 \approx 4.67$. This
"compare now to the value of waiting" pattern is **dynamic programming**, and it reappears in
American option pricing below.

#### Variance and conditional expectation

The expected value says where a quantity is centred; the **variance** says how far it typically
strays: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2$.
Its square root is the **standard deviation**. For a die, $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 91/6$, so the
variance is $91/6 - 3.5^2 = 35/12$ and the standard deviation is 1.71. Variance is not linear:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}X + b^2 \operatorname{Var}Y + 2ab\, \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y),
$$

where the **covariance** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\, \mathbb{E}Y$ is zero
for independent variables. So variances of independent quantities add, and standard deviations
add "in quadrature": three dice have variance $3 \times 35/12$ and standard deviation
$\sqrt{3} \times 1.71 = 2.96$, not $3 \times 1.71$. This is the most important fact in risk
management, and it is why the portfolio section works.

When a quantity depends on another random quantity, condition on the first one. The **law of
total expectation** (also called the **tower property**) says
$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid N]\big]$, and the **law of total variance** says

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid N)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid N]\big).
$$

**Worked example.** Roll a die, then flip that many coins. How many heads, on average, and with
what variance? Given that the die shows $N$, the heads count is binomial with mean $N/2$ and variance
$N/4$. So $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[N]/2 = 1.75$, and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[N/4] + \operatorname{Var}(N/2) = \frac{3.5}{4} + \frac{35/12}{4} = 0.875 + 0.729 = 1.604.
$$

The first term is the noise of the coins; the second is the extra noise from not knowing how
many coins there will be. Traders meet the same split when an option's payoff depends on a
volatility that is itself uncertain.

#### The distributions to know

Most interview questions reduce to one of a handful of distributions. Knowing their means and
variances saves deriving them under pressure.

| Distribution | Describes | Mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli($p$) | One trial: 1 with probability $p$ | $p$ | $p(1 - p)$ |
| Binomial($n, p$) | Successes in $n$ independent trials | $np$ | $np(1 - p)$ |
| Geometric($p$) | Trials up to and including the first success | $1/p$ | $(1 - p)/p^2$ |
| Poisson($\lambda$) | Count of rare events in an interval | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ |
| Uniform($a, b$) | Any point in $[a, b]$ equally likely | $(a + b)/2$ | $(b - a)^2/12$ |
| Exponential($\lambda$) | Waiting time between Poisson events | $1/\lambda$ | $1/\lambda^2$ |
| Normal($\mu, \sigma^2$) | Sums of many small effects (CLT) | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ |

Two facts about them come up constantly. The **geometric** wait is memoryless: having waited 10
rolls for a 6 does not shorten the remaining wait, which is still 6 on average (with variance
30). And the **normal** distribution puts 68%, 95% and 99.7% of its mass within 1, 2 and 3
standard deviations of the mean.

#### Continuous problems and symmetry

With continuous variables, probabilities become areas or volumes. Two techniques solve nearly
every interview question.

**Draw the sample space.** Two people agree to meet between 12:00 and 13:00, each arriving at a
uniformly random time and waiting 15 minutes. What is the chance they meet? Plot the two arrival
times as a point in the unit square; they meet when $|x - y| \le 1/4$, a band around the
diagonal. The two corner triangles where they miss each have legs $3/4$, so the answer is
$1 - (3/4)^2 = 7/16 = 0.4375$.

**Use the CDF of a maximum or minimum.** The largest of $n$ independent Uniform(0, 1) values is
below $x$ only if all of them are, so $P(\max \le x) = x^n$. Differentiating gives the density
$n x^{n-1}$ and the mean $n/(n + 1)$: $2/3$ for two values, $3/4$ for three. By symmetry
($x \mapsto 1 - x$) the minimum has mean $1/(n + 1)$. The $n$ points split $[0, 1]$ into $n + 1$
gaps, and by symmetry each gap has the same expected length $1/(n + 1)$, the same argument as
the first-ace exercise below.

### First-step analysis

Many questions ask how long something takes: "How many coin flips, on average, until two heads
in a row?" **First-step analysis** defines one unknown per state, writes how one flip moves
between states, and solves the linear equations.

**Worked example: waiting for HH.** Let $E_0$ be the expected number of further flips when the
last flip was not H (or at the start), and $E_1$ when the last flip was H. One flip costs 1 and
moves you on:

$$
E_0 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_1 + \tfrac12 E_0, \qquad E_1 = 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 E_0.
$$

The first gives $E_0 = 2 + E_1$; substituting the second, $E_0 = 3 + \tfrac12 E_0$, so
$E_0 = 6$.

**The same method for HT gives 4, not 6.** From state "last flip H", a T finishes and another
H keeps you in the same state ($E_1 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_1$, so $E_1 = 2$, and $E_0 = 2 + E_1 = 4$).
For HH a T sends you all the way back to the start. The two patterns are equally likely in any
given pair of flips, yet HH takes longer to appear because its occurrences clump: HHH contains
two HH's that overlap. The figure shows the full waiting-time distributions.

#### Which pattern comes first? Penney's game

A sharper twist: flip until either HHT or HTH appears. Which is more likely to come first? The
expected waits are 8 and 10, which suggests HHT, but expected waits do not answer "which
first". First-step analysis does, with probabilities instead of waiting times.

Let $x_s$ be the probability that HHT wins from state $s$, where the state is the useful suffix
of the flips so far: nothing, H, HH or HT.

- From **HH**, HHT has already won the race: another H leaves you at HH, and the first T
  completes HHT. So $x_{HH} = 1$.
- From **HT**, an H completes HTH (HHT loses); a T sends you back to the start:
  $x_{HT} = \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 x_\varnothing$.
- From **H**: $x_H = \tfrac12 x_{HH} + \tfrac12 x_{HT}$.
- From the start, a T changes nothing, so $x_\varnothing = x_H$.

Substituting, $x_H = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 x_H$, so $x_H = 2/3$. **HHT comes first two times in
three** (a simulation of 100,000 races gave 0.669). The reason is visible in the states: once
HH appears, HHT cannot lose, but HTH can be destroyed by a single wrong flip. In **Penney's
game** the second player can always pick a pattern that beats the first player's, a favourite
interview twist about non-transitive bets.

you expect to deal, including the ace?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The 4 aces split the 48 other cards into 5 gaps; by symmetry each gap holds $48/5 = 9.6$ cards
on average. You deal the first gap and then the ace: $9.6 + 1 = 10.6 = 53/5$. (Indicator check:
each non-ace precedes all four aces with probability $1/5$.)
</details>

**2.3** What is the expected number of flips until HTH? Until HHH?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

States = length of the matched prefix. For HTH: $E_0 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_1 + \tfrac12 E_0$,
$E_1 = 1 + \tfrac12 E_1 + \tfrac12 E_2$ (H keeps "H"), $E_2 = 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 E_0$

shortcut: the answer is $\sum 2^k$ over every $k$ where the pattern's first $k$ flips equal its
last $k$: HTH overlaps itself at $k = 1, 3$ ($2 + 8 = 10$); HHH at $k = 1, 2, 3$ ($2 + 4 + 8 = 14$).
</details>

## Random walks and martingales

Prices move step by step, and a trader's P&L is a running sum of wins and losses. The simplest
model of both is a **random walk**: start at $S_0 = k$ and add $+1$ with probability $p$ or
$-1$ with probability $q = 1 - p$ at each step. Questions about how far it wanders and when it
hits a level are interview staples, and they lead to the **martingale**, the tool that prices
options.

### Gambler's ruin

**The classic question.** You have \$$k$ and bet \$1 at a time on a coin that wins with
probability $p$. You stop when you reach \$$N$ or go broke. What is the chance you reach $N$?

First-step analysis gives it. Let $P_k$ be the chance of reaching $N$ from $k$. One bet later
you are at $k + 1$ or $k - 1$, so

$$
P_k = p\, P_{k+1} + q\, P_{k-1}, \qquad P_0 = 0,\ P_N = 1.
$$

For a fair coin the solution is a straight line, $P_k = k/N$. For $p \ne \tfrac12$, write
$r = q/p$; the solution is

$$
P_k = \frac{1 - r^k}{1 - r^N}.
$$

The expected length of the game is $k(N - k)$ for a fair coin, and
$\frac{k}{q - p} - \frac{N}{q - p} P_k$ otherwise.

**Worked example.** Start with $k = 3$, target $N = 10$. With a fair coin, $P_3 = 3/10 = 0.3$
and the game lasts $3 \times 7 = 21$ bets on average. Tilt the coin by one percentage point
against you, $p = 0.49$: $r = 0.51/0.49 = 1.0408$ and $P_3 = (1 - 1.0408^3)/(1 - 1.0408^{10})
= 0.259$. A tiny edge changes a lot: at $k = 50$, $N = 100$ the same 1% tilt cuts your chance
from 0.5 to 0.119. **The house edge compounds over many bets**, which is why a trader with a
small edge wants many small independent trades, and a player with a small disadvantage should
make few large bets.

reached $N$ with the theory, then move $p$ to 0.49 and watch the green share fall from about 30%
to about 26%.

### Martingales and optional stopping

A **martingale** is a process whose expected next value, given everything seen so far, equals
its current value: $\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1} \mid M_0, \ldots, M_n] = M_n$. It is the mathematical
form of a **fair game**: no information about the past lets you predict the next move's
direction. A fair-coin random walk is a martingale.

The **optional stopping theorem** says that if you stop a martingale at a stopping time $\tau$
(a rule that depends only on the past, such as "stop at 0 or $N$") and the process stays
bounded until then, then $\mathbb{E}[M_\tau] = M_0$. **No stopping rule turns a fair game into a
winning one.**

That gives gambler's ruin in one line. The walk ends at $N$ with probability $P_k$ and at 0
otherwise, so $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau] = N P_k = k$, and $P_k = k/N$.

Martingales also find durations. For a fair walk, $M_n = S_n^2 - n$ is a martingale too
(each step adds 1 to $\mathbb{E}[S^2]$, and the $-n$ cancels it). Optional stopping gives
$\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2] - \mathbb{E}[\tau] = k^2$. Since $S_\tau^2$ is $N^2$ with probability
$k/N$ and $0$ otherwise, $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = Nk - k^2 = k(N - k)$. For the unfair walk the trick
is to find a function of $S_n$ that is a martingale: $r^{S_n}$ with $r = q/p$ works, because
$p\, r^{s+1} + q\, r^{s-1} = r^s (q + p) = r^s$. Optional stopping then gives the formula for
$P_k$ above.<Sidenote>Optional stopping needs a condition: here the walk is bounded between 0 and
$N$ before $\tau$, and $\tau$ is finite with probability 1. Without one, the theorem fails: "double
your bet after every loss" stops with a win almost surely, but needs unbounded credit.</Sidenote>

#### Wald's identity

A close cousin of optional stopping handles sums with a random number of terms. If
$S_\tau = X_1 + \cdots + X_\tau$ adds independent steps with mean $m$, and $\tau$ is a stopping
time with finite mean, then **Wald's identity** says $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau] = m\, \mathbb{E}[\tau]$: the
average total is the average step times the average number of steps. (The process
$S_n - nm$ is a martingale, and optional stopping does the rest.)

For the unfair walk, each step has mean $p - q$, so
$\mathbb{E}[S_\tau] - k = (p - q)\, \mathbb{E}[\tau]$. Since $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau] = N P_k$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\tau] = \frac{N P_k - k}{p - q}.
$$

Check with $k = 5$, $N = 10$, $p = 0.45$: $P_5 = 0.268$, so
$\mathbb{E}[\tau] = (2.683 - 5)/(-0.1) = 23.2$, matching the formula above. The same identity
prices "how long until the casino takes my money": a gambler with \$100 betting \$1 at a time on
a game with a 1% house edge ($p - q = -0.01$) and no upper target loses it all in
$100 / 0.01 = 10{,}000$ bets on average.

#### The reflection principle

How likely is a walk to *touch* a level at some point, rather than to end above it? For a
symmetric walk there is a beautiful trick. Take any path that reaches level $a > 0$ and ends
below it. Reflect the part of the path after the first visit to $a$ in the line $y = a$. The
reflected path ends above $a$, and the reflection is a one-to-one pairing between the two sets of
paths. So paths that touch $a$ and end below it are exactly as common as paths that end above
it, and

$$
P\Big(\max_{t \le n} S_t \ge a\Big) = P(S_n \ge a) + P(S_n \gt a).
$$

**Worked example.** Ten fair steps, level $a = 2$. $S_{10} \ge 2$ needs at least 6 heads:
$386/1024$. $S_{10} \gt 2$ needs at least 7: $176/1024$. So the walk touches 2 with probability
$562/1024 = 0.549$, well above the chance of ending there or above (0.377). Enumerating all
1,024 paths gives the same number. As the steps get finer the second term approaches the first,
and the ratio tends to exactly 2.

For Brownian motion the correction term vanishes, and $P(\max_{s \le t} W_s \ge a) = 2P(W_t \ge a)$.
A stock with 20% volatility and no drift touches a level 10% above today (in log terms) within
a year with probability $2(1 - N(0.1 / 0.2)) = 2(1 - N(0.5)) = 0.617$, even though it ends the
year above that level only 31% of the time. **Barrier options** (which switch on or off when a
level is touched) are priced with exactly this argument.

The same reflection solves the **ballot problem**: if candidate A receives $a$ votes and B
receives $b \lt a$, counted in random order, the chance A leads throughout the count is
$(a - b)/(a + b)$. With 6 votes to 4 that is $0.2$, which a brute-force count of all 210 orders
confirms.

### Brownian motion and Itô's lemma

Shrink the steps of a random walk and speed them up, keeping variance per unit time fixed, and
the limit is **Brownian motion** $W_t$: a continuous path with $W_0 = 0$, independent
increments, and $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)$. Its paths are so rough that over a short
time $dt$ the move $dW$ has size $\sqrt{dt}$, much larger than $dt$. So $(dW)^2$ does not
vanish: it equals $dt$ on average and, in the limit, exactly.

That one fact is **Itô's lemma**. For a smooth function $f(X_t)$ of a process with
$dX = \mu\, dt + \sigma\, dW$, a Taylor expansion to second order keeps the $(dX)^2 = \sigma^2 dt$
term:

$$
df = f'(X)\, dX + \tfrac12 f''(X)\, \sigma^2\, dt.
$$

**Worked example: $W_t^2$.** With $f(x) = x^2$, $f' = 2x$, $f'' = 2$, $\mu = 0$ and
$\sigma = 1$: $d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\, dW_t + dt$. The $dW$ term has mean zero, so
$\mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = t$, matching the variance. It also shows $W_t^2 - t$ is a martingale, the
continuous version of $S_n^2 - n$.

**Worked example: stock prices.** The standard model, **geometric Brownian motion**, has
returns rather than prices following Brownian motion: $dS = \mu S\, dt + \sigma S\, dW$. Apply
Itô to $\ln S$ ($f' = 1/S$, $f'' = -1/S^2$):

$$
d \ln S = \left(\mu - \tfrac12 \sigma^2\right) dt + \sigma\, dW, \qquad S_t = S_0\, e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t}.
$$

The mean price grows at $\mu$ ($\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}$), but the **median** grows at
$\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2$. With $\mu = 10\%$ and $\sigma = 20\%$ a year, the typical path grows
at only 8% a year. This gap, the **volatility drag**, is the same effect that makes over-betting
ruinous in the next section.

**Hitting levels with drift.** Gambler's ruin has a continuous version too. For a Brownian
motion with drift, $X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t$ with $\mu \gt 0$, the process $e^{-2\mu X_t/\sigma^2}$ is
a martingale (check with Itô: the $dt$ terms cancel). Optional stopping then gives the
probability of *ever* falling to $-a$:

$$
P(\text{ever reach } -a) = e^{-2\mu a / \sigma^2}.
$$

A strategy whose log wealth drifts up 10% a year with 20% volatility ever loses half its log
value ($a = 0.5$) with probability $e^{-2 \times 0.1 \times 0.5 / 0.04} = e^{-2.5} = 0.082$. A
positive drift does not rule out deep drawdowns; it only makes them exponentially unlikely in
$\mu a / \sigma^2$. The Kelly section turns this into a formula for drawdowns.

**3.3** Use Itô's lemma to find $\mathbb{E}[W_t^4]$.

<details>

With $f(x) = x^4$: $d(W^4) = 4W^3\, dW + 6W^2\, dt$. Taking expectations kills the $dW$ term:

you guarantee a positive expected profit if your losses are capped at \$100?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

No. Your wealth is a martingale and it is bounded below by $-100$ and stopped by your rule. With

expected profit 0. The famous "double after a loss" system only wins on average if you are
allowed unbounded losses.
</details>

**3.5** A symmetric random walk takes 10 steps. What is the probability it never goes below its
starting point (it may touch 0)?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Going below 0 means touching $-1$. By reflection in $y = -1$, that has probability
$P(S_{10} \le -1) + P(S_{10} \lt -1)$. After an even number of steps $S_{10}$ is even, so both events
are "$S_{10} \le -2$" (at most 4 heads), with probability $386/1024$ each. The walk therefore
stays at or above 0 with probability $1 - 772/1024 = 252/1024 = 0.246$. That equals
$P(S_{10} = 0) = \binom{10}{5}/2^{10}$, a known identity, and a count of all 1,024 paths agrees.
</details>

<details>

Over a quarter, the standard deviation of the log price is $0.3 \times \sqrt{0.25} = 0.15$. The

$2(1 - N(1)) = 2 \times 0.159 = 0.317$, against $0.159$ for ending above it.
</details>

## Betting and the Kelly criterion

Suppose you have found an edge: a bet that wins with probability $p = 0.6$ at even money
($b = 1$, so a win pays your stake). The expected profit per dollar is $pb - q = 0.2$. How much
of your wealth should you bet each time?

**Maximising expected wealth is the wrong goal.** Expected wealth after $n$ bets of fraction
$f$ is $(1 + 0.2f)^n$, largest at $f = 1$: bet everything every time. But then one loss ruins
you, and over 100 bets you are almost surely broke ($1 - 0.6^{100}$). The mean is propped up by
a single astronomically lucky path.

**Maximise the growth rate instead.** After $n$ bets with $W$ wins, wealth is
$(1 + fb)^W (1 - f)^{n - W}$. Its logarithm divided by $n$ converges to the **growth rate**

$$
g(f) = p \ln(1 + fb) + q \ln(1 - f).
$$

Almost every path grows like $e^{n g(f)}$, so $g$ describes the typical (median) outcome. Setting
$g'(f) = \frac{pb}{1 + fb} - \frac{q}{1 - f} = 0$ gives the **Kelly criterion**

$$
f^* = p - \frac{q}{b} = \frac{\text{edge}}{\text{odds}}.
$$

**Worked example.** For $p = 0.6$, $b = 1$: $f^* = 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2$. Then
$g(0.2) = 0.6 \ln 1.2 + 0.4 \ln 0.8 = 0.1094 - 0.0893 = 0.0201$ per bet. Some neighbours:

| Fraction $f$ | Growth $g(f)$ per bet | Median wealth after 300 bets |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.10 (half Kelly) | 0.0150 | 91 |
| 0.20 (Kelly) | 0.0201 | 420 |
| 0.30 | 0.0147 | 83 |
| 0.40 (double Kelly) | −0.0024 | 0.48 |
| 0.50 | −0.0340 | 0.00004 |

Two facts are worth memorising. **Half Kelly keeps three quarters of the growth** with far
smaller swings (near the peak $g$ is a parabola, so $g(f^*/2) \approx \tfrac34 g(f^*)$; here 0.0150
vs 0.0201). And **double Kelly grows at roughly zero**: here slightly negative, because the
growth curve crosses zero at $f = 0.389$. Over-betting an edge loses money even though every bet
is favourable. In practice traders bet a fraction of Kelly because $p$ itself is only an
estimate, and over-estimating it pushes you into the steep right side of the curve.

0.39 and watch the median collapse while the mean keeps rising.

#### Why the logarithm?

The step from "wealth" to "log wealth" deserves a closer look, because interviewers probe it.
Wealth after $n$ bets is a *product* of $n$ random factors, $(1 + fb)$ or $(1 - f)$. Products
are awkward, but their logarithms are sums:

$$
\ln W_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(\text{factor}_i).
$$

By the law of large numbers, a sum of $n$ independent terms is close to $n$ times their mean
when $n$ is large. So $\ln W_n \approx n\, g(f)$ with high probability, and the *typical* wealth is
$e^{n g(f)}$. The *mean* wealth, $(1 + f(pb - q))^n$, is a different and misleading number: it is
dominated by rare paths with many more wins than average. At $f = 0.5$ in the example, mean
wealth after 300 bets is astronomically large while the median is $0.00004$: almost everyone is
ruined, and a vanishing few are rich enough to pull the average up.

So Kelly is not a matter of taste. **Any other fixed fraction ends up with less wealth than Kelly
with probability approaching 1** as the number of bets grows. Equivalently, Kelly maximises
expected *log* utility, which is why economists describe it as the choice of a log-utility
investor.

#### Drawdowns and fractional Kelly

Growth is not the only thing a trader cares about; a firm will shut down a strategy that loses
half its capital, however good its long-run growth. The continuous model gives a clean answer.
Bet a fraction $c$ of the Kelly amount (so $c = 1$ is full Kelly, $c = 0.5$ is half Kelly) on an
investment with Sharpe ratio $\lambda$. Log wealth then drifts at $\lambda^2 c(1 - c/2)$ with
volatility $c\lambda$, and the drift-hitting formula from the previous section gives the
probability of *ever* falling to a fraction $x$ of today's wealth:

$$
P(\text{wealth ever falls to } x \text{ of today}) = x^{2/c - 1}.
$$

| Fraction of Kelly $c$ | Share of the maximum growth $c(2 - c)$ | P(ever lose half) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2 (double Kelly) | 0 | 1 |
| 1 (full Kelly) | 1 | 0.5 |
| 0.5 (half Kelly) | 0.75 | 0.125 |
| 0.25 (quarter Kelly) | 0.4375 | 0.0078 |

**Full Kelly halves your money at some point with probability one half.** Half Kelly gives up a
quarter of the growth and cuts that risk to one in eight; quarter Kelly makes it negligible. This
table is the main reason professional traders run at a fraction of Kelly.<Sidenote>Formula from
the continuous-time Kelly literature (see Thorp 2006 in the References). Check: a simulation of
4,000 discretised paths over 200 years gave 0.478 for full Kelly and 0.117 for half Kelly; the
small shortfall comes from the finite horizon and weekly steps.</Sidenote> Notice it does not
depend on the Sharpe ratio: a better strategy grows faster, but its drawdowns at full Kelly are
just as deep.

#### Estimation error

The other reason to bet less than Kelly is that $p$ is never known exactly. Suppose you believe
$p = 0.6$ and bet $f = 0.2$, but the true $p$ is 0.55. For the true bet, Kelly is $0.1$, so you are
at double Kelly, and your growth is $0.55 \ln 1.2 + 0.45 \ln 0.8 = -0.00014$ per bet: a winning
bet, sized from a five-point overestimate, loses money in the long run. Betting half of your
estimated Kelly ($f = 0.1$) would have landed exactly on the true optimum. The growth curve is
flat on the left of the peak and steep on the right, so **errors in the direction of betting too
little are cheap and errors in the direction of betting too much are expensive.**

## Market making

A **market maker** quotes two prices: a **bid**, at which they will buy, and an **ask** (or offer),
at which they will sell. The gap is the **spread**; the midpoint is their estimate of **fair
value**. A quote is written "bid at ask", for example "9.5 at 11.5", often with sizes. In an
exchange, these quotes rest in a **limit order book**: buy orders sorted by price on one side,
sell orders on the other. An incoming order that crosses the spread trades against the best
resting price, with ties broken by time. The figure steps through matching on a small book; see
[the order book post](/blog/order-book-cpp/) for the engine behind it.

<MatchingSteps />

The market maker's profit comes from the spread: buying at the bid and selling at the ask earns
the spread on a round trip. Two forces push back. **Adverse selection**: some counterparties
know more than you, and they only trade when your price is wrong. **Inventory risk**: after you
buy, you own something whose price can fall before you sell it.

### Making a market on a dice game

The most common trading-interview game: "Make me a market on the sum of three dice." It tests
whether you can find a fair value, size a spread to the uncertainty, and update as information
arrives.

**Step 1: fair value.** Each die averages 3.5, so the sum averages $10.5$. Its variance is
$3 \times 35/12 = 8.75$ (a die has variance $\mathbb{E}[X^2] - 3.5^2 = 91/6 - 12.25 = 35/12$), so
the standard deviation is $2.96$.

**Step 2: a spread.** Quote around fair value, wide enough to cover what you cannot know.
"9.5 at 11.5" (half-spread $h = 1$) is a reasonable opening. Against an **uninformed** trader,
who buys or sells at random, you earn $h = 1$ per trade on average. Against an **informed**
trader, who knows the dice, you lose: they buy at 11.5 whenever the total is 12 or more and sell
at 9.5 whenever it is 9 or less, which happens 75% of the time, and their expected profit per
trade is $\mathbb{E}[(S - 11.5)^+] + \mathbb{E}[(9.5 - S)^+] = 1.54$.

**Step 3: update.** When a die is revealed, fair value becomes the known part plus 3.5 per
hidden die, and the uncertainty shrinks. Reveal a 4: fair value $4 + 7 = 11$, standard deviation
$2.42$. Reveal a 6 next: fair value $10 + 3.5 = 13.5$, standard deviation $1.71$. Your quote moves
with fair value, and you can afford to narrow it.

The figure steps through exactly this. Green bars are totals inside your quote; red bars are
totals at which an informed trader would hit you. Widen the half-spread to 2 and the informed
trader's chance of trading drops from 0.75 to 0.52 and their profit from 1.54 to 0.91; but in a
real market, wide quotes also attract fewer uninformed trades.

<DiceMarketSteps dice="4,6,2" halfSpread={1} />

**How interviewers play it.** The interviewer may trade against you, hint that they know
something, or ask you to make a market on a strange quantity ("the number of windows in this
building"). Good habits: state your fair value and your uncertainty, quote symmetric around
fair value at first, **move your price after each trade** (a buyer suggests the value is higher),
and keep track of your position. If you are long 5 contracts and do not want more, shade both
prices down so buyers are more likely to take your offer.

#### A full game, trade by trade

Here is the dice game played to settlement, with the dice from the figure (4, 6, 2, so the total
is 12). Each contract settles at the total: if you are **short** (sold more than you bought), you
pay the total for each contract at the end. Track **position** (contracts held, negative when
short) and **cash** separately; P&L is cash plus position times the settlement value.

| Event | Your quote | Trade | Position | Cash |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Start | 9.5 – 11.5 | | 0 | 0 |
| Interviewer buys 2 at your ask | 9.5 – 11.5 | you sell 2 at 11.5 | −2 | +23.0 |
| You raise the quote: buying is evidence | 10.5 – 12.5 | | −2 | +23.0 |
| Interviewer sells 1 at your bid | 10.5 – 12.5 | you buy 1 at 10.5 | −1 | +12.5 |
| First die revealed: 4, fair value 11 | 10 – 12 | | −1 | +12.5 |
| Interviewer buys 1 at your ask | 10 – 12 | you sell 1 at 12 | −2 | +24.5 |
| Settlement: total is 12 | | you pay 2 × 12 | 0 | +0.5 |

You finish up 0.5. Before the last two dice were revealed, your expected P&L was
$24.5 - 2 \times 11 = 2.5$ (marking your position at fair value 11). The dice then came in high
(12 against an expected 11), which cost you 2 on a short position of 2. That is **inventory
risk** in miniature: trading at good prices had put you 2.5 up on paper, and the position you
were left holding gave back 2. Interviewers like to ask exactly this decomposition: "how much
did you make from the spread and how much from your position?" (Exercise 5.4 works one through.)

Notice also what the interviewer did: bought twice and sold once. If they had known the dice,
buying at 11.5 and 12 would have been right and selling at 10.5 wrong; a mixture like this is
consistent with noise. Saying this aloud ("their flow looks two-sided, so I won't move my fair
value much") is exactly the reasoning the round tests.

### Adverse selection and inventory

**Glosten–Milgrom: why spreads exist even with no costs.** An asset is worth either 90 or 110,
each with probability $\tfrac12$, so fair value is 100. A fraction $\mu = 0.3$ of traders know the
true value; the rest buy or sell at random with equal probability. A market maker who breaks
even sets the ask to the expected value **given that someone buys**. A buy is more likely when
the value is high:

$$
P(\text{buy} \mid 110) = \mu + \tfrac12(1 - \mu) = 0.65, \qquad P(\text{buy} \mid 90) = \tfrac12(1 - \mu) = 0.35.
$$

By Bayes, $\mathbb{E}[V \mid \text{buy}] = (0.65 \times 110 + 0.35 \times 90)/(0.65 + 0.35) = 103$.
By symmetry the bid is 97. The spread of 6, which equals $2\mu \times 10$, is pure protection
against informed traders: the market maker loses to them and wins it back from the uninformed.
**More informed flow means wider spreads.** And every trade moves the quote, because it is
evidence: after a buy, the market maker revises fair value to 103.

The updating continues trade by trade. After each trade the market maker's belief
$P(V = 110)$ changes by Bayes' rule, and the new ask is $90 + 20 \times P(V = 110 \mid \text{history}, \text{one more buy})$,
the new bid the same with one more sell:

| Trades so far | P(V = 110) | Bid | Ask |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| none | 0.500 | 97.0 | 103.0 |
| buy | 0.650 | 100.0 | 105.5 |
| buy, buy | 0.775 | 103.0 | 107.3 |
| buy, sell | 0.500 | 97.0 | 103.0 |

Two lessons follow. **Prices discover the truth**: a run of buys drags the quotes towards 110,
because only an informed trader (or bad luck) produces such a run. And **opposite trades cancel**:
a buy followed by a sell returns the market maker to where they started, because the two pieces
of evidence have equal and opposite weight. The bid after one buy (100) is just the fair value
after that buy: a sell would undo it.

**Inventory: skew, do not just widen.** A market maker who has bought too much wants to sell.
In the Avellaneda–Stoikov model the market maker quotes around a **reservation price**
$r = s - q\, \gamma \sigma^2 (T - t)$ rather than the mid $s$, where $q$ is inventory, $\gamma$
risk aversion, $\sigma$ volatility and $T - t$ the time left. Long inventory ($q > 0$) lowers
both quotes, making the sale more likely and the next purchase less likely. The effect is
larger when the asset is volatile or a long time remains, because the risk of holding is
larger.<Sidenote>Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008), "High-frequency trading in a limit order book";
the optimal spread in the same model adds a term that depends on how quickly order arrival
falls off with distance from the mid.</Sidenote>

**Worked example.** The mid is $s = 100$, you are long $q = 3$ units, risk aversion $\gamma = 0.1$,
volatility $\sigma = 2$ per unit time and half a unit of time remains. The reservation price is
$100 - 3 \times 0.1 \times 4 \times 0.5 = 99.4$: you value your inventory 0.6 below the mid, because
holding it is risky. The model's total spread is
$\gamma\sigma^2(T - t) + \tfrac{2}{\gamma}\ln(1 + \gamma/k)$; with an order-arrival decay $k = 1.5$
that is $0.2 + 1.29 = 1.49$, so you would quote about $98.65$ at $100.15$: a bid well below the
mid and an ask only a little above it. Buying from you has become attractive for counterparties
and selling to you unattractive, which is how the market maker sheds a long position without
crossing the spread itself.

## Options

An **option** is the right, but not the obligation, to trade an asset at a fixed **strike**
price $K$ on (or before) an **expiry** date $T$. A **call** is the right to buy, a **put** the
right to sell. A **European** option can be exercised only at expiry, an **American** one at any
time. Options are the main product of firms like Optiver, SIG and IMC, so their interviews test
payoffs, pricing and the **Greeks** (sensitivities).

### Payoffs and put–call parity

At expiry, a call is worth $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ and a put $\max(K - S_T, 0)$, where $S_T$ is the
stock price then. Their difference is linear in $S_T$:

$$
\max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K.
$$

So a portfolio of one call minus one put pays the same as one share minus $K$ in cash, in every
state of the world. Two portfolios with identical payoffs must have identical prices today, or
someone could buy the cheap one, sell the dear one and lock in a riskless profit (an
**arbitrage**). Discounting the cash at rate $r$ gives **put–call parity** for European options:

$$
C - P = S - K e^{-rT}.
$$

**Worked example.** With $S = 100$, $K = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $T = 1$ year: $C - P = 100 - 95.12 =
4.88$. The Black–Scholes prices below, $C = 10.45$ and $P = 5.57$, differ by exactly 4.88. Parity
needs no model of how prices move, which is why interviewers love it: it is pure no-arbitrage
reasoning.

#### No-arbitrage bounds

The same "compare two portfolios" argument bounds option prices without any model:

- **A call is worth at most the stock**: $C \le S$. The call pays $\max(S_T - K, 0) \le S_T$.
- **A call is worth at least its discounted intrinsic value**: $C \ge \max(S - Ke^{-rT}, 0)$. Parity
  gives $C = S - Ke^{-rT} + P$ and $P \ge 0$. With $S = K = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $T = 1$ the bound is
  4.88; the Black–Scholes price 10.45 respects it.
- **A put is worth at most the discounted strike**: $P \le Ke^{-rT}$.
- **Call prices fall with the strike, but not too fast**: for $K_1 \lt K_2$,
  $0 \le C(K_1) - C(K_2) \le (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$. The **call spread** (long $K_1$, short $K_2$) pays
  between 0 and $K_2 - K_1$.
- **Call prices are convex in the strike**: a **butterfly** (long $K - h$, short two $K$, long
  $K + h$) pays something non-negative, so it must cost something non-negative:
  $C(K - h) - 2C(K) + C(K + h) \ge 0$. With $S = 100$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $r = 5\%$, $T = 1$:
  $16.70 - 2 \times 10.45 + 6.04 = 1.84 \ge 0$.

Interviewers test these by quoting prices that violate one and asking for the trade.

**Strategies are sums of payoffs.** Because payoffs add, any piecewise-linear payoff can be built
from calls, puts and the stock. A **straddle** (call plus put at the same strike) bets on a big
move in either direction. A **bull call spread** caps both the cost and the gain of a bullish view.
A **butterfly** bets that the stock ends near the middle strike.

The butterfly's tent shape is why convexity in the strike matters: shrink $h$ and the butterfly,
scaled by $1/h^2$, approaches a spike at $K$. Its price then measures the risk-neutral probability
density of ending at $K$, so **option prices across strikes encode the market's whole distribution
for the stock** (the Breeden–Litzenberger result). A cruder version of the same idea: a
**digital call**, paying 1 if $S_T \gt K$, is the limit of a call spread scaled by $1/h$, so its
price is $-\partial C / \partial K = e^{-rT} N(d_2)$. At the money with the inputs above that is
$0.9512 \times N(0.15) = 0.532$, a little above one half because the drift is positive under the
risk-neutral measure.

### The binomial model

How can anyone price an option without knowing whether the stock will rise? The answer is
**replication**: build the option's payoff from stock and cash.

**One step.** A stock at 100 will be 120 or 80 in one period; interest is zero. A call with
strike 100 pays 20 or 0. Buy $\Delta$ shares and borrow $B$ so that

$$
120\Delta - B = 20, \qquad 80\Delta - B = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta = \frac{20 - 0}{120 - 80} = 0.5,\ B = 40.
$$

The portfolio costs $100 \times 0.5 - 40 = 10$ today and pays exactly the option's payoff in
both states, so the call is worth 10. The real-world probability of an up move never entered.

**Risk-neutral pricing.** The same answer comes from pretending the stock earns the risk-free
rate on average. The **risk-neutral probability** $q$ solves $120q + 80(1 - q) = 100$, so
$q = 0.5$, and the price is $\mathbb{E}_q[\text{payoff}] = 0.5 \times 20 = 10$. With interest,
$q = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$ and the expectation is discounted. Under $q$ the discounted
stock price is a martingale, which ties pricing back to the previous sections.

**Many steps.** The **Cox–Ross–Rubinstein (CRR)** tree chops time into $n$ steps of length
$\Delta t$, with moves $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$. Prices are found backwards
from expiry: each node is the discounted risk-neutral average of its two children. An American
option also checks whether exercising now beats holding, the same "now versus the value of
waiting" comparison as the re-roll game.

**Worked example.** $S = 100$, $K = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$, three steps:
$u = 1.1224$, $d = 0.8909$, $q = 0.5438$. The European put is worth 6.17 on this tree. The
American put is worth more, 6.50, because at the node after two down moves ($S = 79.38$),
exercising pays $20.62$ while holding is worth only $18.97$. Exercising early lets you collect
the strike sooner and earn interest on it. Step through the figure and hover that node; then
switch to a call and note that the American call never exercises early on a stock without
dividends.

### Black–Scholes and the Greeks

Letting the number of steps go to infinity, or equivalently hedging continuously under
geometric Brownian motion, gives the **Black–Scholes** price of a European call:

$$
C = S\, N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},
$$

where $N$ is the standard normal CDF. $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability the call ends in
the money, and $N(d_1)$ is the number of shares in the replicating portfolio. With $S = K = 100$,
$r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$: $d_1 = 0.35$, $d_2 = 0.15$, $C = 10.45$.

**The at-the-money rule of thumb.** With $r = 0$ and $S = K$, expanding $N$ around 0 gives
$C \approx S\sigma\sqrt{T}/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.4\, \sigma \sqrt{T}\, S$. For $S = 100$,
$\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$: $0.4 \times 0.2 \times 100 = 8$ (exact: 7.97). Interviewers expect this
in seconds. An at-the-money **straddle** (call plus put) is about $0.8\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S$.

The **Greeks** measure how the price changes when one input moves. They are how a market maker
thinks about risk.

| Greek | Definition | Call at $S = K = 100$ (inputs above) | Meaning |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| Delta $\Delta$ | $\partial C / \partial S$ | 0.637 | Shares to hedge one option |
| Gamma $\Gamma$ | $\partial^2 C / \partial S^2$ | 0.0188 | How fast delta changes |
| Vega | $\partial C / \partial \sigma$ | 0.375 per vol point | Exposure to volatility |
| Theta $\Theta$ | $\partial C / \partial t$ | −0.0176 per day | Value lost to time |

**Gamma and theta are two sides of one trade.** A delta-hedged option holder makes money when
the stock moves a lot (gamma) and loses money as time passes (theta). With $r = 0$ the
Black–Scholes equation gives $\Theta = -\tfrac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma$ exactly, so over a short time
$dt$ the hedged P&L is

$$
\tfrac12 \Gamma S^2 \left(\sigma_{\text{realised}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{implied}}^2\right) dt.
$$

Buying an option is therefore a bet that the stock will move more than the **implied
volatility** priced into it. This is what options traders actually trade: volatility, not
direction.

In the figure, drag the spot to see delta run from 0 to 1, switch to gamma to see it peak at
the strike, and shorten $T$ to see gamma sharpen and theta grow as expiry nears.

<OptionGreeks K={100} S={100} sigma={0.2} T={1} r={0.05} kind="call" greek="delta" />

Parity ties the Greeks of calls and puts together. Differentiating $C - P = S - Ke^{-rT}$ with
respect to $S$ gives $\Delta_C - \Delta_P = 1$ (here $0.637 - (-0.363) = 1$), and a second time gives
equal gammas. Calls and puts at the same strike also share vega, because the right-hand side does
not depend on $\sigma$. So for volatility trading it does not matter whether you buy calls or puts;
the difference is a position in the stock.

#### Delta hedging, step by step

A market maker who sells an option does not want to bet on the stock's direction. They **delta
hedge**: hold $\Delta$ shares against each option sold, and rebalance as $\Delta$ changes. Here is a
short call hedged daily over five trading days, with $S = K = 100$, $\sigma = 20\%$ and $r = 0$.
The premium received is $1.124$ (Black–Scholes for five days). The stock path is invented for the
example.

| Day | Stock | Delta | Shares bought (+) or sold (−) | Cash after trade |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 100.0 | 0.506 | +0.506 | −49.44 |
| 1 | 101.5 | 0.727 | +0.221 | −71.90 |
| 2 | 99.8 | 0.468 | −0.259 | −46.04 |
| 3 | 100.9 | 0.696 | +0.228 | −69.02 |
| 4 | 102.2 | 0.959 | +0.263 | −95.89 |
| 5 | 101.0 | expiry | | |

At expiry the 0.959 shares are worth 96.81 and the call costs 1.00 to settle, so the P&L is
$-95.89 + 96.81 - 1.00 = -0.085$. Check one row: on day 2 the stock fell to 99.8, delta dropped from
0.727 to 0.468, so you **sold** 0.259 shares *after a fall*; on days 1, 3 and 4 you **bought** after
rises. A short-option hedger always buys high and sells low, and those small losses are the cost of
being short gamma. The premium pays for them if the stock moves as much as the 20% volatility
priced in. Here the path's realised volatility was 21.7%, a little more, so the hedger lost a
little. The gamma–theta formula predicts this: summing
$\tfrac12 \Gamma S^2 (\sigma^2 \Delta t - (\Delta S/S)^2)$ over the five days gives $-0.094$, close to the
actual $-0.085$.

#### Implied volatility and the smile

Of the five Black–Scholes inputs, four are observable: spot, strike, time and rate. Volatility is
not. Traders therefore turn the formula around: given an option's market price, the **implied
volatility** is the $\sigma$ that makes Black–Scholes match it. A one-year at-the-money call
trading at 12.00 (with $S = K = 100$, $r = 5\%$) implies $\sigma = 24.1\%$; at 10.45 it would imply
20%. Because price rises with volatility (vega is positive), the inversion has one answer, found
by a few steps of Newton's method.

Options desks quote and think in implied volatility, not price, because it makes options with
different strikes and maturities comparable. If Black–Scholes were exactly right, every strike
would show the same implied volatility. In practice the plot of implied volatility against strike
is a **smile** or **skew**: in equity markets, low-strike puts trade at higher implied volatility
than at-the-money options, because crashes are more likely than a lognormal model allows and
investors pay for protection. Saying "the skew prices in fat left tails" is the expected interview
answer.

**6.3** A stock at 50 will be 60 or 40 in one period, with zero interest. Price a put with strike

**6.4** Why is it never optimal to exercise an American call early on a stock that pays no
dividend?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Exercising now pays $S - K$. Holding is worth at least $S - Ke^{-r(T - t)}$ (the European lower
bound from parity, since $P \ge 0$), which is larger when $r > 0$: you pay the strike later. You
also keep the option's protection against a fall. So the call is worth more alive than dead;
selling it beats exercising it.
</details>

**6.5** What is the delta of an at-the-money call with a very short time to expiry, and why do
traders say it is "about 0.5"?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$d_1 = (r + \tfrac12\sigma^2)\sqrt{T}/\sigma \to 0$ as $T \to 0$, so $\Delta = N(d_1) \to 0.5$: the
stock is equally likely to finish above or below the strike. For longer maturities $d_1 > 0$ and
delta is above 0.5 (0.637 in the table).
</details>

**6.6** Calls on the same stock and expiry trade at 15 (strike 90), 9 (strike 100) and 2 (strike
110). Is there an arbitrage?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The butterfly costs $15 - 2 \times 9 + 2 = -1$: you would be *paid* 1 to enter a position whose payoff
is never negative. Buy the 90 and 110 calls, sell two 100 calls, collect 1 now, and receive a
non-negative payoff (up to 10 at $S_T = 100$) at expiry.
</details>

**6.7** You are long an at-the-money straddle and delta-hedged. The stock does not move for a
week. Did you make or lose money? What if it swings up and down 3% every day?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

A straddle is long gamma and short theta. With no movement you collect no gamma profits and pay a
week of theta: you lose. With large daily swings, each rebalance sells after rises and buys after
falls, locking in gains of about $\tfrac12 \Gamma S^2 (\Delta S/S)^2$ per day. If the realised
volatility of those swings (3% a day is about 48% a year) exceeds the implied volatility you paid,
you make money.
</details>

**6.8** Estimate the vega of a one-year at-the-money call on a \$100 stock, per volatility point.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

From $C \approx 0.4\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\,S$, $\partial C/\partial\sigma \approx 0.4\sqrt{T}\,S = 40$ per unit of
volatility, or 0.40 per point. Black–Scholes gives 0.397 with $r = 0$ (0.375 with $r = 5\%$). Vega is
largest at the money and grows with $\sqrt{T}$.
</details>

## Statistics and regression

Research roles ask how you would know an edge is real. That needs three ideas: standard errors,
regression, and the danger of searching too hard.

**Standard errors.** The average of $n$ independent observations with standard deviation
$\sigma$ has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: halving the error takes four times the data.
By the **central limit theorem** the average is roughly normal, so about 95% of averages land
within two standard errors of the truth. Example: 100 fair-coin flips have mean 50 and standard
deviation 5; 60 or more heads is two standard deviations out, and the exact probability is
0.028.

**How long to prove a Sharpe ratio.** The **Sharpe ratio** of a strategy is its mean excess
return over its volatility, per year. After $Y$ years of returns, the t-statistic of the mean is
roughly $\text{SR}\sqrt{Y}$. To reach $t = 2$ a Sharpe of 1 needs 4 years of data, a Sharpe of
0.5 needs 16, and a Sharpe of 2 one year. This is why high-frequency strategies, which can have
very high Sharpe ratios and many independent bets, can be validated quickly and slow strategies
cannot.

#### Testing whether an edge is real

A **hypothesis test** asks: if there were no edge (the **null hypothesis**), how surprising would
this data be? The **t-statistic** measures the observed mean in units of its standard error,
$t = \bar x / (s / \sqrt{n})$, and roughly $|t| \gt 2$ is "significant at 5%": data this extreme
would arise less than 5% of the time with no edge. The **p-value** is that probability.

**Worked example.** A strategy earns a mean daily return of 0.05% with a daily standard deviation
of 1% over 250 days. Then $t = 0.05 / 1 \times \sqrt{250} = 0.79$. That is well inside the noise:
a strategy with no edge at all would produce a $t$ this large about 43% of the time. The
annualised Sharpe ratio is $0.05 / 1 \times \sqrt{252} = 0.79$, the same number, which is the
"$t \approx \text{SR}\sqrt{Y}$" rule with $Y = 1$.

A **confidence interval** reports the same uncertainty as a range. A coin shows 60 heads in 100
flips; the estimate is $\hat p = 0.6$ with standard error $\sqrt{0.6 \times 0.4 / 100} = 0.049$, so
the 95% interval is $0.6 \pm 1.96 \times 0.049 = [0.504, 0.696]$. It just excludes 0.5. (Because a
fair coin has standard deviation 5 heads, 60 heads is two of them away from 50, matching the
earlier tail probability of 0.028 for "60 or more".)

Two warnings recur in interviews. **The sample variance divides by $n - 1$**, not $n$, because the
deviations are measured from the sample mean, which is itself fitted to the data and so sits
slightly closer to the points than the true mean does. And **"not significant" is not "no
effect"**: a test with little data cannot distinguish a small edge from none, as the example
above shows.

**Linear regression.** Ordinary least squares fits $y = \alpha + \beta x + \varepsilon$ by
minimising the sum of squared residuals. The solution is

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \rho\, \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x}, \qquad \hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta \bar x,
$$

and $R^2 = \rho^2$ is the share of the variance of $y$ explained. In finance, regressing a stock's
returns on the market's gives its **beta** (market exposure) and **alpha** (return not explained
by the market).

**Worked example.** Ten days of market and stock returns, in percent:

| Market $x$ | −4 | −2 | −1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | −3 | 3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Stock $y$ | −5 | −3 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 5 | −4 | 3 |

The means are $\bar x = 0$ and $\bar y = 0.2$. The sums are $\sum (x - \bar x)^2 = 54$,
$\sum (x - \bar x)(y - \bar y) = 69$ and $\sum (y - \bar y)^2 = 93.6$. So $\hat\beta = 69/54 = 1.28$,
$\hat\alpha = 0.2$, $\rho = 69/\sqrt{54 \times 93.6} = 0.971$ and $R^2 = 0.94$. Check one residual: on
the first day the fit predicts $0.2 + 1.28 \times (-4) = -4.91$ against $-5$, a miss of $-0.09$. The
residual standard deviation is 0.82 and the slope's standard error is $0.82/\sqrt{54} = 0.11$, so
$t = 11.4$: the beta is clearly not zero (with only ten points, the alpha of 0.2 is not
distinguishable from zero).

The band is the 95% prediction interval for a new day ($t_{8} = 2.306$); it widens away from
$\bar x$ because the slope is uncertain.

**A classic trap: regression is not symmetric.** Regressing $x$ on $y$ gives slope
$69/93.6 = 0.74$, not $1/1.28 = 0.78$. The two slopes multiply to $\rho^2 = 0.94$. Both lines shrink
towards the mean of the variable being predicted, which is **regression to the mean**.

**Overfitting and multiple testing.** Test 20 strategies with no edge at the 5% significance
level and the chance at least one looks significant is $1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$. A backtest that
tried many parameters must be judged on data it never saw (an out-of-sample period), with the
significance bar raised for the number of things tried. Interviewers often ask "your backtest
has a Sharpe of 3; what could be wrong?": look-ahead bias (using data not yet available),
survivorship bias (testing only stocks that still exist), ignored transaction costs and
overfitting.

How much higher should the bar be? The simplest correction, **Bonferroni**, divides the
significance level by the number of tests. For 20 strategies at an overall 5% level, each test
must pass at $0.25\%$, which for a two-sided normal test means $|t| \gt 3.02$ instead of 1.96.
Harvey, Liu and Zhu argued on these grounds that new return factors in finance research should
clear $t \gt 3$. Bonferroni is conservative when the tests are correlated (similar strategies
tend to succeed or fail together), but it is the right first answer in an interview.

#### More regression traps

**Omitted variables.** Suppose returns really follow $y = \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon$, but
you regress $y$ on $x_1$ alone. The fitted slope converges to

$$
\beta_1 + \beta_2 \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x_1, x_2)}{\operatorname{Var}(x_1)},
$$

not $\beta_1$. If a stock's true model is $y = 1 \cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2$ and the omitted factor has
regression slope 0.5 on $x_1$, the one-factor regression reports a slope of $1 + 2 \times 0.5 = 2$:
the omitted factor's effect is credited to the one you kept. This is why a "new signal" is
always checked against known factors (market, size, value, momentum) before anyone believes it.

**Correlated regressors.** When two regressors are highly correlated (**multicollinearity**), the
regression cannot tell their effects apart: the individual slopes get large standard errors and
can flip sign between samples, even though their combined prediction is stable.

**Overlapping data.** Five-day returns computed every day overlap by four days, so consecutive
observations share 80% of their content. Treating them as independent overstates the number of
observations about fivefold and shrinks standard errors by about $\sqrt{5}$, the same fake precision
as duplicating rows (Exercise 7.2).

#### Exercises

**7.1** The regression of $y$ on $x$ has slope 0.8 and the regression of $x$ on $y$ has slope 0.45.
What is the correlation?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The slopes are $\rho\sigma_y/\sigma_x$ and $\rho\sigma_x/\sigma_y$; their product is $\rho^2 = 0.36$,
so $\rho = 0.6$ (positive, like the slopes).
</details>

**7.2** You duplicate every row of your dataset and rerun the regression. What happens to
$\hat\beta$ and its standard error?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\hat\beta$ is unchanged: every sum doubles and the ratio stays the same. The reported standard
error shrinks, roughly by $\sqrt{2}$ for large $n$ (for the ten-point example, from 0.112 to
0.075, a factor of 1.5 because the degrees of freedom go from 8 to 18). The shrinkage is fake:
no new information was added. The same error appears when overlapping returns are treated as
independent.
</details>

**7.3** Stocks A and B have correlation 0.9, and B and C have correlation 0.9. What is the
lowest possible correlation between A and C?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The correlation matrix must be positive semidefinite, which gives
$\rho_{AC} \ge \rho_{AB}\rho_{BC} - \sqrt{(1 - \rho_{AB}^2)(1 - \rho_{BC}^2)} = 0.81 - 0.19 = 0.62$.
Geometrically: correlations are cosines of angles between vectors; A and C are each within
25.8° of B, so at most 51.7° apart, and $\cos 51.7° = 0.62$.
</details>

**7.4** A strategy shows an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.5 over two years of daily data. Is it
significant? What if it was the best of 50 variants you tried?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$t \approx 1.5 \sqrt{2} = 2.12$, just above 1.96 on its own. As the best of 50 tries, the Bonferroni
threshold is $0.05/50 = 0.1\%$, about $|t| \gt 3.3$; at $t = 2.12$ it is no longer convincing. It needs
out-of-sample data, or about $(3.3/1.5)^2 \approx 4.8$ years at the same Sharpe.
</details>

**7.5** You estimate a daily volatility from 100 daily returns. Roughly how precise is the
estimate?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

For roughly normal returns, the standard error of a sample standard deviation is about
$\sigma/\sqrt{2n} = \sigma/\sqrt{200} \approx 0.07\sigma$: a 7% relative error. Volatility is much easier
to estimate than the mean (whose relative error here would be enormous), which is one reason
options traders can trade volatility with confidence while directional traders struggle to measure
their edge.
</details>

## Portfolios and risk

Combining positions is where most of a trading firm's risk management lives. The key fact is
that **variances add, with a correlation term**:

$$
\sigma_p^2 = w^2 \sigma_1^2 + (1 - w)^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w (1 - w) \rho\, \sigma_1 \sigma_2.
$$

When $\rho \lt 1$, the portfolio is less volatile than the weighted average of its parts. That is
**diversification**.

**Worked example.** Two assets have volatilities 20% and 30% and correlation 0.2. Setting the
derivative to zero gives the **minimum-variance** weight in the first asset,

$$
w^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2} = \frac{0.09 - 0.012}{0.13 - 0.024} = 0.736,
$$

with portfolio volatility 18.1%: lower than either asset alone. With $\rho = -1$ the risk
can be removed completely (at $w = 0.6$); with $\rho = 1$ there is no benefit at all.

**Diversification has a floor.** An equal-weighted portfolio of $N$ assets, each with volatility
$\sigma$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, has variance $\sigma^2\left(\rho + (1 - \rho)/N\right)$.
With $\sigma = 20\%$ and $\rho = 0.3$: 20% for one asset, 12.2% for ten, 11.1% for a hundred, and
never below $\sqrt{0.3} \times 20\% = 11.0\%$. The correlated part, **systematic risk**, cannot be
diversified away; this is the idea behind the **CAPM**, in which only an asset's beta to the
market earns a risk premium.

#### Many assets: the tangency portfolio

With $n$ assets, write the weights as a vector $w$ and the covariances as a matrix $\Sigma$ (variances
on the diagonal, covariances off it). The portfolio variance is $w^\top \Sigma w$, the matrix form
of the two-asset formula. Two portfolios matter:

- The **minimum-variance portfolio** has $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$, scaled so the weights sum to 1.
- The **tangency portfolio** maximises the Sharpe ratio $(w^\top\mu - r)/\sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}$ and has
  $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r)$. Its squared Sharpe ratio is $(\mu - r)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mu - r)$.

**Worked example.** Add expected returns to the two assets above: 8% and 12%, with a risk-free
rate of 2%. On their own their Sharpe ratios are $6/20 = 0.30$ and $10/30 = 0.33$. Solving
$\Sigma w = \mu - r$ and normalising gives weights 56.1% and 43.9%, an expected return of 9.75%,
volatility 18.9% and a Sharpe ratio of $7.75/18.9 = 0.41$: better than either asset alone. Every
investor who can borrow and lend at the risk-free rate should hold this mix, scaled up or down to
their taste for risk. This is **two-fund separation**, and with the Kelly result above it says how
much: $f^* = (\mu - r)/\sigma^2$ applied to the tangency portfolio.

**CAPM.** If every investor holds the tangency portfolio, it must be the market portfolio, and
each asset's expected return depends only on its **beta** to the market:
$\mathbb{E}[R_i] = r + \beta_i(\mathbb{E}[R_m] - r)$. The stock from the regression example, with
$\beta = 1.28$, a 2% risk-free rate and a 5% market premium, should earn $2\% + 1.28 \times 5\% = 8.4\%$.
Anything earned above this line is **alpha**, which is what a quant fund is paid to find.

In practice the inputs are the weak point. Expected returns are estimated with huge errors (the
statistics section showed why), and the optimiser leans hardest on exactly the assets whose
expected returns were overestimated. Practitioners shrink the estimates, add constraints, or
build portfolios from risk alone (equal risk contribution) for this reason.

**Combining strategies.** Two uncorrelated strategies with the same Sharpe ratio $S$, combined
optimally, have Sharpe $S\sqrt{2}$: returns add linearly but uncorrelated risks add in
quadrature. This is why trading firms run many small, independent strategies rather than one
big one, and why the **fundamental law of active management** says that skill per bet times the
square root of the number of independent bets sets the overall Sharpe.

**Measuring risk.** **Value at Risk (VaR)** at 99% is the loss exceeded on only 1% of days; for
normal returns it is $2.33\sigma$. It says nothing about how bad the worst 1% is, so firms also use
**expected shortfall** (the average loss beyond VaR), stress tests and position limits. For a
normal distribution the 99% expected shortfall is $\varphi(2.326)/0.01 = 2.67\sigma$, only 15% beyond
VaR; for fat-tailed returns the gap is much larger, which is exactly when it matters. Expected
shortfall also has a property VaR lacks: the shortfall of a combined book is never more than the
sum of its parts, so it rewards diversification as it should.

#### Exercises

**8.1** Two uncorrelated strategies each have a Sharpe ratio of 1. What is the best Sharpe you
can get by combining them?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Allocate equal risk to each. Mean returns add ($\mu_1 + \mu_2 = 2\mu$ for unit risk each) and
variances add ($\sigma^2 + \sigma^2$), so Sharpe $= 2\mu/(\sqrt2\sigma) = \sqrt2 \times 1 = 1.41$.
</details>

**8.2** A portfolio has daily volatility of \$1 million. What is its approximate one-day 99% VaR
and ten-day 99% VaR, assuming normal, independent daily returns?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

One day: 2.33 × \$1 million = \$2.33 million. Ten days: volatility scales with $\sqrt{10} = 3.16$,
so the VaR is 2.33 × 3.16 × \$1 million = \$7.4 million. Real returns have fat tails and serial correlation, so this is a
lower bound on prudence, not a guarantee.
</details>

**8.3** A stock has beta 1.5 to the market and 40% volatility; the market has 16% volatility.
What share of the stock's variance is market risk, and what is its idiosyncratic volatility?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Market variance contributed: $(1.5 \times 0.16)^2 = 0.0576$; total variance $0.4^2 = 0.16$. So market
risk is $0.0576/0.16 = 36\%$ of the variance (this is the regression $R^2$), and the idiosyncratic
volatility is $\sqrt{0.16 - 0.0576} = \sqrt{0.1024} = 32\%$.
</details>

## Brainteasers, coding and the live interview

**Brainteasers** test structured thinking under pressure more than knowledge. A dependable
approach: restate the problem, try the smallest cases, look for symmetry or an invariant, and
work backwards from the end. Two examples:

- **25 horses, 5 lanes, no timer: how many races to find the fastest three?** Race five groups
  (5 races), then race the five winners (race 6). Only the top three groups can contain a top-3
  horse, and only the candidates that could still be second or third remain: the second and third
  of the winners' race, the second and third from the fastest group, and the second from the
  second-fastest group. Race those five (race 7). Answer: 7.
- **Two ropes each take an hour to burn, unevenly. Measure 45 minutes.** Light rope A at both
  ends and rope B at one end. A burns out in 30 minutes; at that moment light B's other end, and
  it burns out 15 minutes later.
- **Two eggs, 100 floors: find the highest safe floor with the fewest drops in the worst case.**
  With one egg you must go floor by floor. With two, drop the first egg at floor $k$, then
  $k + (k - 1)$, then $k + (k - 1) + (k - 2)$, and so on: each time the first egg survives, the
  remaining linear search with the second egg gets one floor shorter, which keeps the worst case
  constant at $k$ drops. You need $k + (k - 1) + \cdots + 1 = k(k + 1)/2 \ge 100$, so $k = 14$
  ($14 \times 15 / 2 = 105$, while 13 gives only 91).
- **100 lockers, all closed. Person $k$ toggles every $k$-th locker, for $k = 1, \ldots, 100$.
  Which stay open?** Locker $n$ is toggled once per divisor of $n$. Divisors come in pairs
  $(d, n/d)$, except when $d = n/d$, so only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors. The
  10 lockers 1, 4, 9, ..., 100 stay open.
- **Ants on a 1-metre stick walk at 1 m/min in random directions and reverse when they collide.
  How long until all have fallen off?** At most one minute: two ants bouncing off each other look
  exactly like two ants passing through each other with labels swapped, so the collisions change
  nothing about *when* ants leave the stick. The invariant does all the work.

**Coding rounds** for traders usually ask for a short simulation or a clean implementation of a
probability calculation; developer and researcher roles ask standard algorithm questions. A good
habit is to check an analytic answer with a quick Monte Carlo:

```python
import random

def flips_until(pattern: str) -> int:
    seq = ""
    while not seq.endswith(pattern):
        seq += random.choice("HT")
    return len(seq)

trials = 200_000
print(sum(flips_until("HH") for _ in range(trials)) / trials)  # about 6.0
print(sum(flips_until("HT") for _ in range(trials)) / trials)  # about 4.0
```

Dynamic programming is the other recurring pattern: the value of a game with choices, computed
backwards. The re-roll game takes three lines, and the loop is the same "keep if better than the
value of continuing" rule an American option uses:

```python
def reroll_value(rerolls: int) -> float:
    value = 3.5                      # with no re-rolls left, take the roll
    for _ in range(rerolls):
        value = sum(max(face, value) for face in range(1, 7)) / 6
    return value

print([round(reroll_value(n), 4) for n in range(5)])  # [3.5, 4.25, 4.6667, 4.9444, 5.1296]
```

**In the room.** Think out loud, so the interviewer can follow and help. State assumptions
("I'll assume the dice are fair and independent"). Give a quick estimate before an exact
answer, and sanity-check the result (a probability above 1, or an expected wait shorter than the
pattern, means an error). In trading games, a decision you can justify beats a fast guess, and
changing your mind when new information arrives is the point, not a weakness.

## Summary

- Trading interviews test **expected value under uncertainty, computed quickly and explained
  aloud**; dice, coins and cards are just the cheapest way to probe it.
- **Counting, conditioning, linearity of expectation and first-step analysis** solve most
  probability questions; **martingales with optional stopping** solve most random-walk ones.
- An edge is not enough: **bet sizing** matters, and Kelly's $f^* = p - q/b$ maximises long-run
  growth; betting twice that much grows at about zero.
- A **market maker** quotes around fair value with a spread that pays for **adverse
  selection**, updates on every trade, and skews quotes to manage **inventory**.
- Options are priced by **replication and no-arbitrage** (put–call parity, binomial trees,
  Black–Scholes); traders think in Greeks, and owning gamma is a bet that realised volatility
  will beat implied.
