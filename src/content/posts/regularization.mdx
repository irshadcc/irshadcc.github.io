## Background

A sufficiently large neural network can memorize its training set. That does not make it useful:
we care about unseen inputs. **Regularization** is any mechanism that biases learning toward
solutions expected to generalize, even when many parameter settings fit the observed data.

This post follows Chapter 9 of Bishop and Bishop's *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts*. It
covers inductive bias, invariance and equivariance, weight decay and generalized norms, early
stopping, parameter sharing, soft weight sharing, residual connections, ensembles, and dropout.
It ends with worked derivations for every exercise. It completes a series with
[Gradient Descent](/blog/gradient-descent/) and [Backpropagation](/blog/backpropagation/).

## Inductive bias

Learning a distribution from finitely many samples is an **inverse problem**. Infinitely many
distributions assign nonzero probability to the observed points, so data alone cannot select one.
An **inductive bias** is the preference that resolves this ambiguity: smoothness, locality,
translation symmetry, sparse parameters, or a particular architecture.

The no-free-lunch theorem says that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, no
learner is better than another. It does *not* say learning is futile. Real problems are not
uniformly random; useful learning comes precisely from choosing biases that match their structure.

### Inverse problems

The forward problem starts with a distribution $p(\mathbf t\mid\mathbf x)$ and draws observations.
The learning problem runs in reverse: from finitely many observed pairs, infer behaviour at inputs
that were not observed. This inverse is not unique. A high-degree interpolating polynomial, a
piecewise constant lookup table, and a smooth low-degree curve can all pass through the same finite
training set.

An inverse problem is **ill posed** when a solution need not exist, need not be unique, or is not
stable under small changes to the data. Neural-network training commonly faces the latter two.
Regularization makes the problem better posed by ranking the many data-fitting solutions. A
smoothness prior, for example, says that two nearby inputs should usually have nearby outputs.

### No free lunch

Imagine averaging a learner's error uniformly over every possible assignment of labels to inputs.
Whenever one algorithm's bias helps on one assignment, an opposite assignment makes that same bias
hurt by the same amount. Averaged over this artificial universe, all learners tie. The theorem
therefore denies a universally best bias; it does not deny useful, domain-specific biases.

Choosing a convolutional network for images assumes locality and shared translation structure.
Choosing an unrestricted lookup table assumes no relation between nearby pixels. Neither is
universally correct, but natural images strongly favour the first. Model choice is consequently a
claim about the data-generating process.

### Invariance, equivariance, and groups

A set of transformations is a **group** when it is closed under composition, composition is
associative, there is an identity, and every element has an inverse. A predictor $f$ is invariant
to $T$ when $f(Tx)=f(x)$. A mapping is equivariant when

$$
f(Tx)=T_e f(x).
$$

Classification should often be translation invariant; segmentation should be translation
equivariant because moving the image should move the mask. We can encode these facts in features,
an architecture such as convolution, a penalty, or data augmentation. Augmentation is valid only
when the transformation really preserves the target relationship.

More formally, let a group $G$ act on inputs through transformations $T_g$ and on outputs through
$S_g$. Equivariance requires

$$
f(T_g\mathbf x)=S_gf(\mathbf x)\qquad\text{for every }g\in G.
$$

Invariance is the special case $S_g=I$. The group law matters because applying transformations
$g_1$ then $g_2$ must agree with the representation of their composition. Without this consistency,
the model could obey the desired relationship for individual transformations but contradict itself
when they are composed.

There are three different ways to supply the same bias. An invariant feature map discards the
transformation before learning. An equivariant architecture restricts every representable
function. Data augmentation leaves the architecture unrestricted but adds evidence that transformed
examples should agree. These are not equivalent with finite data and finite optimization: a hard
architectural constraint guarantees the property, while augmentation only encourages it at sampled
transformations.

## Weight decay

The simplest explicit regularizer adds a quadratic penalty:

$$
\widetilde E(\mathbf w)=E(\mathbf w)+\frac\lambda2\mathbf w^T\mathbf w,qquad
\nabla\widetilde E=\nabla E+\lambda\mathbf w.
$$

Under gradient descent,
$\mathbf w\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda)\mathbf w-\eta\nabla E$: unsupported weights shrink on every
step, hence **weight decay**. From a probabilistic view, this is maximum a posteriori estimation
with an isotropic zero-mean Gaussian prior.

For a quadratic data error with Hessian eigenvalue $h_i$ and unregularized optimum component
$w_i^\star$, the regularized optimum is

$$
\widetilde w_i=\frac{h_i}{h_i+\lambda}w_i^\star.
$$

Flat, weakly determined directions ($h_i\ll\lambda$) shrink strongly; well-determined directions
barely move.

### Consistent regularizers

Reparameterizations can leave a network function unchanged. Scaling inputs and compensating in
first-layer weights, or scaling outputs and compensating in the last layer, should not arbitrarily
change the strength of the prior. A single global $\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2$ does change. Separate
coefficients per layer and appropriate treatment of biases can preserve the intended transformation
properties. The lesson is that a penalty is a prior over *parameters*, and parameterization matters.

To see the inconsistency algebraically, consider a two-layer network

$$
z_j=h\!\left(\sum_iw_{ji}^{(1)}x_i+w_{j0}^{(1)}\right),\qquad
y_k=\sum_jw_{kj}^{(2)}z_j+w_{k0}^{(2)}.
$$

Rescale inputs as $\widetilde x_i=ax_i+b$. The same function is recovered by
$\widetilde w_{ji}^{(1)}=w_{ji}^{(1)}/a$ and
$\widetilde w_{j0}^{(1)}=w_{j0}^{(1)}-(b/a)\sum_iw_{ji}^{(1)}$. A single penalty on every weight
and bias changes under this harmless choice of units, even though the represented mapping does not.
The regularizer is then expressing a preference for metres versus centimetres, not a preference
among functions.

A more consistent construction uses separate coefficients for weight groups and does not penalize
biases in the same way. From the Bayesian view, this means placing distinct priors on parameters
with different transformation roles. It also explains the common practice of excluding biases and
normalization offsets from weight decay.

### Bayesian reading of weight decay

Suppose the data likelihood is $p(\mathcal D\mid\mathbf w)$ and the prior is isotropic Gaussian,

$$
p(\mathbf w)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\right)^{W/2}
\exp\!\left(-\frac\lambda2\mathbf w^T\mathbf w\right).
$$

Maximum a posteriori estimation maximizes
$p(\mathbf w\mid\mathcal D)\propto p(\mathcal D\mid\mathbf w)p(\mathbf w)$. Taking a negative log
turns the product into

$$
-\log p(\mathbf w\mid\mathcal D)
=E_D(\mathbf w)+\frac\lambda2\mathbf w^T\mathbf w+\text{constant}.
$$

Thus weight decay is not merely an optimization trick: it is the parameter-space preference of a
Gaussian prior. The caution is equally important. In a neural network, many parameter vectors can
represent the same function, so a simple isotropic prior over parameters need not correspond to a
simple or invariant prior over functions.

### Generalized weight decay and sparsity

Consider

$$
\Omega_q(\mathbf w)=\frac\lambda2\sum_j|w_j|^q.
$$

$q=2$ has circular contours and smooth shrinkage. $q=1$ has corners on the axes; a tangent error
contour often touches at a corner, giving exact zeros. $q<1$ promotes sparsity even more strongly
but is nonconvex. $q>2$ penalizes large coordinates strongly but does not prefer axes. The
penalized problem is equivalent, for a corresponding Lagrange multiplier, to minimizing $E$ under
$\sum_j|w_j|^q\leq\eta$.

Rotate the $L_1$ surface below and then look down from above. Its level sets are diamonds, and the
surface has creases along the coordinate axes. A smooth data-error contour often first touches the
feasible diamond at a corner; that geometric contact is why an $L_1$ solution often contains exact
zeros rather than merely small values.

Adding zero-mean Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ to the inputs of linear least squares gives

$$
\mathbb E_\epsilon[\widetilde E]
=E_D+\frac{N\sigma^2}{2}\sum_i w_i^2,
$$

up to whether $E_D$ is defined as a sum or mean. Noise injection is therefore exactly weight decay
in this case and approximately a smoothness penalty for nonlinear models.

## Learning curves and early stopping

A **learning curve** plots a metric against training progress. Training error usually falls;
validation error can fall and then rise as the model begins fitting sample-specific detail.
**Early stopping** saves the parameters at the lowest validation error, not merely those from the
last iteration.

For quadratic error and initialization at zero, eigen-coordinate $j$ evolves as

$$
w_j^{(\tau)}=\left[1-(1-\rho h_j)^\tau\right]w_j^\star.
$$

Large-curvature components are learned early; small-curvature components remain suppressed for
$h_j\ll1/(\rho\tau)$. Compare this with weight decay's filter $h_j/(h_j+\lambda)$: training time
$\rho\tau$ behaves roughly like $1/\lambda$. Early stopping is thus an implicit spectral
regularizer, not merely a time-saving trick. The validation set must not also be used for the final
unbiased performance estimate; keep a separate test set.

This small teaching curve is illustrative rather than measured. Training error decreases
monotonically. Validation error initially follows it, reaches a minimum, and then rises as later
updates fit training-specific detail. Early stopping keeps the checkpoint at that validation
minimum even though later checkpoints have lower training error.

### Double descent

The classical bias--variance picture predicts a U-shaped test-error curve as model complexity
increases. Modern overparameterized models often show **double descent** instead. Test error first
falls, then rises sharply near the point where the model can just interpolate the training data,
and finally falls again as the number of parameters grows far beyond the number needed to fit it.

Why can the interpolation threshold be dangerous? Near that threshold, fitting every target—noise
included—may require a solution with very large parameter norm. Small perturbations of the data can
then cause large changes in the fitted function. In linear least squares, the effect appears when
the smallest singular values of the design matrix approach zero: inversion multiplies residual
components by $1/\sigma_i$, so weakly supported directions explode.

Past the threshold there are infinitely many interpolating solutions. Gradient-based training from
small initialization tends to select a particular low-complexity member—for linear regression, the
minimum-Euclidean-norm interpolant. Adding more features can allow that selected solution to spread
the fit across more coordinates and reduce its norm, so test error can fall again. This selection
by the optimizer is **implicit regularization**.

Double descent can be plotted against parameter count, training time, or data-set size. It is not a
promise that “larger is always better.” Its shape depends on label noise, architecture, optimizer,
regularization, and how complexity is measured. Explicit weight decay and early stopping often
smooth or suppress the interpolation peak.

The schematic curve separates the regimes. Before interpolation, capacity reduces approximation
bias. Around normalized complexity $1$, the model can just fit every target and instability creates
a peak. Far beyond interpolation, the optimizer's preferred interpolating solution can become
smoother again. The curve is explicitly illustrative: its shape depends on the data, architecture,
optimizer, and regularization.

## Sharing and compressing parameters

**Parameter sharing** forces several connections to use one value $\lambda$. The chain rule gives
$\partial E/\partial\lambda=\sum_k\partial E/\partial w_k$. Convolutions use this idea to express
translation equivariance while using far fewer independent parameters.

To derive the shared gradient, temporarily regard the tied copies as independent variables
$w_1,\ldots,w_K$, then impose $w_k(\lambda)=\lambda$. The total derivative is

$$
\frac{dE}{d\lambda}
=\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\partial E}{\partial w_k}\frac{dw_k}{d\lambda}
=\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\partial E}{\partial w_k}.
$$

Thus sharing does not average the evidence from uses of a parameter; it accumulates it. Whether an
implementation later divides a batch loss by the number of examples is a separate convention.

**Soft weight sharing** uses a mixture-of-Gaussians prior over weights. Each weight is pulled toward
each centre $\mu_j$ in proportion to its posterior responsibility

$$
\gamma_j(w_i)=\frac{\pi_j\mathcal N(w_i\mid\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)}
{\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(w_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}.
$$

Learning centres, widths, and mixing coefficients clusters weights into a small codebook. Hard
quantization can then store a cluster index instead of a full-precision number.

The complete regularizer is the negative log probability of every weight under the mixture:

$$
\Omega(\mathbf w)=-\sum_i\log\left[
\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\mathcal N(w_i\mid\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)
\right].
$$

Differentiate with respect to a weight. Factoring the mixture responsibility gives

$$
\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial w_i}
=\sum_j\gamma_j(w_i)\frac{w_i-\mu_j}{\sigma_j^2}.
$$

Each component behaves like a spring pulling $w_i$ toward $\mu_j$. Its force is stronger for a
narrow component and is weighted by the probability that the component owns the weight. The
centres are learnable too:

$$
\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu_j}
=\sum_i\gamma_j(w_i)\frac{\mu_j-w_i}{\sigma_j^2}.
$$

At a stationary point, $\mu_j$ is the responsibility-weighted mean of its assigned weights. The
width update makes $\sigma_j^2$ their responsibility-weighted squared deviation. Parameterizing
$\sigma_j=e^{\xi_j}$ keeps every width positive. Finally, softmax logits for $\pi_j$ lead to a
gradient proportional to $\pi_j-\gamma_j(w_i)$, moving each mixture mass toward its average
responsibility. Soft weight sharing is therefore a differentiable clustering algorithm trained
jointly with the predictive model.

## Residual connections

A residual block computes $z_{l+1}=F_l(z_l)+z_l$. Repeated substitution shows a direct identity
path from early representations to later ones. Its Jacobian is $I+J_{F_l}$, so gradients have a
route that does not require multiplying only through learned nonlinear maps. Residual connections
change optimization and the function class; they are an architectural inductive bias rather than a
penalty added to the loss.

For $L$ residual blocks, repeated substitution gives

$$
z_L=z_0+\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}F_l(z_l).
$$

This does not mean the network is merely a sum of independent functions, because each $F_l$ is
evaluated at a state containing all earlier residual updates. It does show that the identity signal
is preserved additively. Backpropagation multiplies by
$\partial z_{l+1}/\partial z_l=I+J_{F_l}$ rather than only $J_{F_l}$. When the residual branch is
small, the Jacobian is near the identity and gradients can propagate without repeated severe
contraction or expansion.

## Ensembles and dropout

For $M$ predictors $y_m=y+\epsilon_m$ with zero-mean, uncorrelated, equal-variance errors, their
average has error variance

$$
\mathbb E\left[\left(\frac1M\sum_m\epsilon_m\right)^2\right]
=\frac1M\mathbb E[\epsilon_m^2].
$$

Correlation limits the gain: with pairwise correlation $c$, the factor becomes
$c+(1-c)/M$. Diversity, not the count alone, is what helps. Jensen's inequality also proves that
for convex loss the loss of the averaged prediction is no greater than the average member loss.

The plot quantifies the price of correlated mistakes. Ten independent members reduce variance to
$1/10$ of one member's variance. At correlation $0.5$, the factor is already $0.55$; at correlation
$1$, all members make the same error and averaging gives no reduction.

**Dropout** samples Bernoulli masks and trains many thinned networks with shared weights. For a
linear model with keep probability $\rho$, averaging over masks produces the ordinary error for
the scaled predictor plus

$$
\rho(1-\rho)\sum_{n,k,i}w_{ki}^2x_{ni}^2,
$$

an input-dependent quadratic regularizer. At inference, inverted dropout has already divided kept
activations by $\rho$, so no further scaling is needed. Dropout's exact equivalence to an ensemble
or $L_2$ penalty does not extend unchanged to deep nonlinear networks, but both views explain part
of its effect.

For the linear mapping $y_{nk}=\sum_iw_{ki}R_{ni}x_{ni}$ with independent
$R_{ni}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\rho)$,

$$
\mathbb E[R_{ni}]=\rho,qquad
\mathbb E[R_{ni}R_{nj}]=
\begin{cases}
\rho,&i=j,\\
\rho^2,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}
$$

Expanding squared error and applying these two moments separates it into

$$
\mathbb E_R[E]
=\sum_{n,k}\left(t_{nk}-\rho\sum_iw_{ki}x_{ni}\right)^2
+\rho(1-\rho)\sum_{n,k,i}w_{ki}^2x_{ni}^2.
$$

The first term trains the mean scaled network. The second is a feature-dependent quadratic
penalty: a weight connected to a feature with large empirical squared magnitude is regularized more
strongly. Standardizing inputs makes this closer to ordinary isotropic $L_2$ decay. In a deep
nonlinear network masks alter later activations, so this exact decomposition no longer holds; that
is why the linear result should be treated as an explanation, not a universal identity.

### Exercises 9.1--9.5: groups, noise, decay, and constraints

Multiples of $90^\circ$ satisfy closure, associativity, the $0^\circ$ identity, and inverse
$360^\circ-a$; image-axis translations form a group under vector addition (9.1). Expanding
$\widetilde y_n=y_n+\sum_iw_i\epsilon_{ni}$ and taking expectations removes linear cross terms and
leaves the quadratic penalty $N\sigma^2\sum_iw_i^2/2$ (9.2). The continuous-time limit of weight
decay is $d\mathbf w/dt=-\widetilde\eta\mathbf w$, whose solution is
$\mathbf w(t)=\mathbf w(0)e^{-\widetilde\eta t}$ (9.3).

Substitution of transformed inputs $\widetilde x=ax+b$, first-layer weights
$\widetilde w=w/a$, and adjusted bias cancels $a,b$; the analogous output transformation cancels
$c,d$, proving functional invariance (9.4). Adding the constraint
$\sum_j|w_j|^q-\eta\leq0$ with multiplier $\lambda/2$ gives the generalized penalty after the
constant $-\lambda\eta/2$ is dropped (9.5).

### Exercises 9.6--9.7: early stopping and shared gradients

Projecting quadratic gradient descent onto Hessian eigenvector $j$ yields
$w_j^{(\tau)}=(1-\rho h_j)w_j^{(\tau-1)}+\rho h_jw_j^\star$. Induction gives the closed form above;
expanding for small $\rho h_j$ proves flat directions stay suppressed (9.6). If
$w_1=\cdots=w_K=\lambda$, the chain rule gives
$\partial E/\partial\lambda=\sum_k\partial E/\partial w_k$ (9.7).

### Exercises 9.8--9.12: soft weight sharing

Bayes' rule applied to mixture prior $p(w)=\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N_j(w)$ gives $\gamma_j(w)$ (9.8).
Differentiating $-\sum_i\log p(w_i)$ gives
$\sum_j\gamma_j(w_i)(w_i-\mu_j)/\sigma_j^2$ (9.9). Differentiating instead by $\mu_j$ pulls the
centre toward its responsibility-weighted weights (9.10); differentiating by
$\xi_j=\log\sigma_j$ drives $\sigma_j^2$ toward their weighted squared deviations (9.11).
Finally $\partial\pi_k/\partial\eta_j=\pi_k(\delta_{jk}-\pi_j)$ for softmax logits, so
$\partial\widetilde E/\partial\eta_j=\lambda\sum_i[\pi_j-\gamma_j(w_i)]$ (9.12).

### Exercises 9.13--9.18: residuals, committees, and dropout

Repeated substitution into $z_{l+1}=F_l(z_l)+z_l$ expands the three-block residual mapping and
exhibits the direct $x$ term (9.13). With uncorrelated member errors, all $m\ne l$ cross terms
vanish, leaving $E_{COM}=E_{AV}/M$ (9.14). Convexity of $x^2$ and Jensen give
$(M^{-1}\sum_m\epsilon_m)^2\leq M^{-1}\sum_m\epsilon_m^2$, so averaging never increases squared
error even without independence (9.15); applying Jensen to any convex loss proves the analogous
claim (9.16).

A weighted prediction stays between every possible set's minimum and maximum iff its coefficients
are nonnegative and sum to one: sufficiency is the convex-hull property; necessity follows by
choosing one member as $1$ and the rest $0$, then choosing all members equal (9.17). For Bernoulli
dropout masks, $\mathbb E[R_i]=\rho$ and
$\mathbb E[R_iR_j]=\delta_{ij}\rho+(1-\delta_{ij})\rho^2$; expansion of squared error then yields the
scaled linear predictor plus the data-dependent $L_2$ term shown above (9.18).

## Summary

- Generalization is impossible without inductive bias; regularization makes that bias explicit or
  builds it into the training process and architecture.
- Quadratic weight decay shrinks poorly determined Hessian directions most, while $L_1$-like
  penalties promote exact sparsity.
- Early stopping is a spectral filter, parameter sharing encodes known symmetries, and residual
  paths improve signal and gradient flow.
- Ensembles help when their errors differ; convexity guarantees that averaging predictions is safe
  for convex losses.
- Dropout averages stochastic subnetworks and, in the linear case, induces an exact
  feature-dependent quadratic penalty.
