## Background

Ordinary calculus asks how a scalar output changes when a scalar input moves. Machine learning,
statistics, and numerical computing ask the same question with vectors and matrices: how does a
loss change when every entry of a weight matrix moves, and how can we compute that change without
writing millions of scalar derivatives?

**Matrix calculus** is the language for answering those questions. It explains the gradients in
least squares, the backward pass of a neural-network layer, the derivative of an inverse, and the
Hessian information used by second-order optimization. The difficult part is usually not the
calculus. It is keeping shapes, transposes, and derivative conventions consistent.

This post starts with derivatives as linear maps, then introduces Jacobians and differentials. We
will learn a small set of differential and trace rules, use them to derive gradients of scalar
losses, work through a complete linear-layer backward pass, connect the result to forward- and
reverse-mode automatic differentiation, and finish with Hessians and gradient checking. Every
numeric result comes from a verification script; the examples stay small enough to check by hand.

## One derivative, four shapes

The derivative always answers one question: *what is the first-order output change caused by a
small input change?* The shapes of the input and output determine how we represent that answer.
The table below uses input type along the horizontal axis and output type along the vertical axis.

| output $\downarrow$ / input $\rightarrow$ | scalar $x\in\mathbb R$ | vector $x\in\mathbb R^n$ | matrix $X\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **scalar** $f\in\mathbb R$ | **Scalar derivative**<br/>$f'(x)\in\mathbb R$<br/>$df=f'(x)\,dx$ | **Gradient**<br/>$\nabla_xf\in\mathbb R^n$<br/>$df=(\nabla_xf)^Tdx$ | **Matrix gradient**<br/>$\nabla_Xf\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}$<br/>$df=\operatorname{tr}((\nabla_Xf)^TdX)$ |
| **vector** $y\in\mathbb R^m$ | **Derivative vector**<br/>$dy/dx\in\mathbb R^m$<br/>$dy=(dy/dx)\,dx$ | **Jacobian matrix**<br/>$J\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$<br/>$dy=J\,dx$ | **Third-order derivative**<br/>$\mathcal J\in\mathbb R^{m\times p\times q}$<br/>or $J\in\mathbb R^{m\times pq}$ after flattening $X$ |
| **matrix** $Y\in\mathbb R^{r\times s}$ | **Derivative matrix**<br/>$dY/dx\in\mathbb R^{r\times s}$<br/>$dY=(dY/dx)\,dx$ | **Third-order derivative**<br/>$\mathcal J\in\mathbb R^{r\times s\times n}$<br/>maps $dx$ to $dY$ | **Fourth-order derivative**<br/>$\mathcal J\in\mathbb R^{r\times s\times p\times q}$<br/>maps $dX$ to $dY$ |

The order of a derivative structure counts its independent axes. A scalar derivative has no
array axis. A gradient or derivative vector has one, a Jacobian matrix has two, and differentiating
a matrix with respect to a matrix appears to require four. In the last two columns, $\mathcal J$
denotes a higher-order derivative tensor, not an ordinary matrix.

In practice, we rarely build those third- and fourth-order arrays. We keep the derivative as a
**linear operator** that accepts a perturbation and returns the resulting output change. If a
two-dimensional representation is required, flatten the input and output consistently:

$$
\operatorname{vec}(dY)
=J\operatorname{vec}(dX),
\qquad
J\in\mathbb R^{(rs)\times(pq)}.
$$

The operator view preserves the natural matrix shapes and avoids committing to a row-major or
column-major flattening convention until an implementation actually needs one.

The most useful unifying definition treats a **derivative as a linear map**. Near $x$, a small
perturbation $dx$ produces

$$
f(x+dx)=f(x)+Df(x)[dx]+o(\lVert dx\rVert).
$$

$Df(x)[dx]$ is linear in the perturbation $dx$. The remainder
$o(\lVert dx\rVert)$ becomes negligible compared with $\lVert dx\rVert$ as the perturbation
shrinks. In one dimension, the linear map is multiplication by $f'(x)$. For vector inputs and
outputs, it can be represented by a Jacobian matrix.

### The Jacobian stores output-by-input derivatives

Let $y=f(x)$ with $x\in\mathbb R^n$ and $y\in\mathbb R^m$. This post uses the
**numerator-layout Jacobian**

$$
J_f(x)=\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial y_1/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial y_1/\partial x_n\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\partial y_m/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial y_m/\partial x_n
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}.
$$

Each row belongs to one output; each column belongs to one input. This convention makes the
differential relation $dy=J_f(x)dx$ type-check: $(m\times n)(n\times1)=m\times1$.
Some books transpose this definition, which is why memorized formulas can appear to disagree.
State the convention, then let dimensions catch mistakes.

Consider

$$
f(x_1,x_2)=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1x_2\\
\sin x_1+x_2^2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its Jacobian is

$$
J_f(x)=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_2 & x_1\\
\cos x_1 & 2x_2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $x=(2,3)$, using radians,

$$
J_f(2,3)=
\begin{bmatrix}
3 & 2\\
-0.416147 & 6
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For the direction $v=(0.1,-0.2)$, the **directional derivative** is the
Jacobian--vector product (JVP)

$$
J_fv=
\begin{bmatrix}
3(0.1)+2(-0.2)\\
-0.416147(0.1)+6(-0.2)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}-0.1\\-1.241615\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check the first entry: $x_1x_2$ gains $3(0.1)$ when $x_1$ moves and loses $2(0.2)$ when
$x_2$ moves, for a net change of $-0.1$ per unit step along $v$.

### A gradient is shaped like its input

When $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ has one output, its Jacobian is a $1\times n$ row. We usually
store its transpose as the **gradient**

$$
\nabla_x f=
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial f/\partial x_1\\
\vdots\\
\partial f/\partial x_n
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
df=(\nabla_x f)^Tdx.
$$

The gradient has the same shape as $x$. It points in the direction of steepest increase under the
usual Euclidean inner product. Therefore $-\nabla f$ is the steepest local descent direction.
This geometric statement depends on the chosen inner product; changing the geometry changes what
"steepest" means.<Sidenote>The natural gradient and preconditioned methods exploit this fact. They
change the metric or rescale the gradient rather than changing the underlying differential.</Sidenote>

## Differentials: calculate without giant Jacobians

A **differential** names the first-order change itself. The notation behaves like familiar algebra
as long as order is preserved. That last clause matters because matrix multiplication is not
commutative.

The core rules are

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(A+B) &= dA+dB,\\
d(cA) &= c\,dA && \text{for constant }c,\\
d(AB) &= (dA)B+A(dB),\\
d(A^T) &= (dA)^T,\\
d\,\phi(A) &= \phi'(A)\odot dA && \text{for elementwise }\phi.
\end{aligned}
$$

$\odot$ is the **Hadamard product**, or entrywise multiplication. The product rule keeps factor
order: $(dA)B+A(dB)$, not $B(dA)+(dB)A$.

### The chain rule composes linear maps

If $y=g(x)$ and $z=h(y)$, then

$$
dz=Dh(y)[dy]=Dh(y)[Dg(x)[dx]].
$$

In Jacobian coordinates,

$$
J_{h\circ g}(x)=J_h(g(x))J_g(x).
$$

The order follows the computation: $dx$ meets $J_g$ first, then $J_h$. If
$J_g\in\mathbb R^{k\times n}$ and $J_h\in\mathbb R^{m\times k}$, their product is
$m\times n$, exactly the shape needed to map $n$ inputs to $m$ outputs.

### Broadcasting is a linear map too

Suppose a batch $X\in\mathbb R^{b\times d}$ receives a bias $b\in\mathbb R^d$:

$$
Y=X+\mathbf 1b^T.
$$

The forward pass copies $b$ into every row. The backward pass is the adjoint operation: sum the
row gradients,

$$
\nabla_b L=(\nabla_Y L)^T\mathbf 1.
$$

This is why an autodiff system reduces gradients over broadcast dimensions. A broadcast is not
"free" from the calculus viewpoint; it has a definite linear transpose.

## From a differential to a matrix gradient

For matrices, the ordinary dot product becomes the **Frobenius inner product**

$$
\langle A,B\rangle_F
=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}
=\operatorname{tr}(A^TB).
$$

If $L$ is scalar and $X$ is a matrix, define $\nabla_XL$ by

$$
dL=\langle\nabla_XL,dX\rangle_F
=\operatorname{tr}((\nabla_XL)^TdX).
$$

This is the pattern to aim for. Derive $dL$, rearrange it until $dX$ is the final factor inside a
trace, then read off the matrix that transposes to the coefficient of $dX$.

The trace makes this rearrangement possible because

$$
\operatorname{tr}(ABC)=\operatorname{tr}(BCA)=\operatorname{tr}(CAB),
$$

but it does **not** allow arbitrary swaps: generally
$\operatorname{tr}(ABC)\ne\operatorname{tr}(ACB)$. The trace of a scalar is the scalar itself,
and $\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^T)$.

### Warm-up: a bilinear form

Let $L=x^TAy$, where $x$ and $y$ are constant and $A$ varies. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
dL &= x^T(dA)y\\
   &= \operatorname{tr}(x^T(dA)y)\\
   &= \operatorname{tr}(yx^T dA).
\end{aligned}
$$

Compare this with $dL=\operatorname{tr}((\nabla_A L)^TdA)$. Therefore

$$
\nabla_A L=xy^T.
$$

The shape check is decisive: $x\in\mathbb R^m$ and $y\in\mathbb R^n$, so $xy^T$ is
$m\times n$, the shape of $A$.

### Quadratic forms and symmetry

For $L=x^TAx$ with constant $A$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
dL
&=(dx)^TAx+x^TA(dx)\\
&=x^TA^Tdx+x^TAdx\\
&=\big((A+A^T)x\big)^Tdx.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\nabla_xL=(A+A^T)x.
$$

Only when $A$ is symmetric does this simplify to $2Ax$. The familiar rule is not wrong; it has a
hidden assumption. With the conventional factor $L=\tfrac12x^TAx$ and symmetric $A$, the
gradient is simply $Ax$.

## Worked example: matrix least squares

Consider

$$
L(X)=\frac12\lVert AX-B\rVert_F^2,
$$

where $A$ and $B$ are constant. Define the residual $R=AX-B$. First differentiate each stage:

$$
dR=A\,dX,
$$

and

$$
dL=\langle R,dR\rangle_F
=\operatorname{tr}(R^TA\,dX)
=\operatorname{tr}((A^TR)^TdX).
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\nabla_XL=A^T(AX-B)}.
$$

Now use real numbers:

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\\2&1\end{bmatrix},\quad
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\2&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&-1\\0&3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The forward pass is

$$
AX=\begin{bmatrix}5&-1\\-2&0\\4&-2\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
R=AX-B=\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\-4&1\\4&-5\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Thus

$$
L=\frac12(4^2+(-1)^2+(-4)^2+1^2+4^2+(-5)^2)=37.5,
$$

and

$$
\nabla_XL=A^TR
=\begin{bmatrix}12&-11\\16&-8\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one entry by hand. The $(1,1)$ entry is the first column of $A$ dotted with the first
column of $R$: $1(4)+0(-4)+2(4)=12$.

We can also check the whole gradient in the direction

$$
D=\begin{bmatrix}0.2&-0.1\\0.3&0.4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The analytic directional derivative is

$$
\langle\nabla_XL,D\rangle_F
=12(0.2)+(-11)(-0.1)+16(0.3)+(-8)(0.4)=5.1.
$$

The one-sided finite difference $[L(X+\epsilon D)-L(X)]/\epsilon$ equals $5.1955$ at
$\epsilon=10^{-1}$, $5.100955$ at $10^{-3}$, and $5.100010$ at $10^{-5}$. It converges to the
analytic value as the neglected second-order term shrinks.

## A complete linear-layer backward pass

A dense layer over a batch computes

$$
Y=XW+\mathbf 1b^T,
$$

with $X\in\mathbb R^{N\times D}$, $W\in\mathbb R^{D\times K}$,
$b\in\mathbb R^K$, and $Y\in\mathbb R^{N\times K}$. Use the squared-error loss

$$
L=\frac12\lVert Y-T\rVert_F^2.
$$

Let $E=Y-T$. The loss differential is $dL=\operatorname{tr}(E^TdY)$, while the layer differential
is

$$
dY=(dX)W+X(dW)+\mathbf 1(db)^T.
$$

Substitute and separate the three independent perturbations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
dL
&=\operatorname{tr}(E^T(dX)W)
 +\operatorname{tr}(E^TX(dW))
 +\operatorname{tr}(E^T\mathbf 1(db)^T)\\
&=\operatorname{tr}((EW^T)^TdX)
 +\operatorname{tr}((X^TE)^TdW)
 +(E^T\mathbf 1)^Tdb.
\end{aligned}
$$

Read off all three gradients:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_XL=EW^T,\qquad
\nabla_WL=X^TE,\qquad
\nabla_bL=E^T\mathbf 1.
}
$$

The shapes explain the transposes:

| gradient | multiplication | resulting shape |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| $\nabla_XL$ | $(N\times K)(K\times D)$ | $N\times D$ |
| $\nabla_WL$ | $(D\times N)(N\times K)$ | $D\times K$ |
| $\nabla_bL$ | $(K\times N)(N\times1)$ | $K\times1$ |

For a concrete batch, choose

$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\\3&1\end{bmatrix},\quad
W=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\1&3\end{bmatrix},\quad
b=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\-0.5\end{bmatrix},\quad
T=\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\-1&-3\\7&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
Y=\begin{bmatrix}4.5&4.5\\-0.5&-3.5\\7.5&-0.5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
E=Y-T=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.5\\0.5&-0.5\\0.5&-0.5\end{bmatrix},
$$

and $L=1.75$. The backward pass gives

$$
\nabla_WL=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\3&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\nabla_bL=\begin{bmatrix}2.5\\-0.5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\nabla_XL=\begin{bmatrix}2.5&3\\1.5&-1\\1.5&-1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one weight gradient: $W_{11}$ connects the first input feature to the first output. Its
gradient is $1(1.5)+0(0.5)+3(0.5)=3$, the first input column dotted with the first error column.
This is exactly the $(1,1)$ entry of $X^TE$.

## Why backpropagation uses vector--Jacobian products

Suppose a computation is a chain

$$
x\xrightarrow{g}u\xrightarrow{h}y\xrightarrow{\ell}L.
$$

Writing the complete Jacobian gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}
=\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}
 \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}
 \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}.
$$

An autodiff system need not construct those middle matrices. **Forward-mode automatic
differentiation** starts with a chosen input tangent $v$ and repeatedly computes JVPs:

$$
v\mapsto J_gv\mapsto J_h(J_gv).
$$

One forward sweep answers, "how do all outputs respond to this one input direction?" It is
attractive when there are few input directions and many outputs.

**Reverse-mode automatic differentiation**, also called backpropagation or the adjoint method,
starts with an output cotangent $\bar y$ and repeatedly computes vector--Jacobian products (VJPs):

$$
\bar y^T\mapsto\bar y^TJ_h\mapsto\bar y^TJ_hJ_g.
$$

For a scalar loss, the initial cotangent is just $1$. One reverse sweep accumulates derivatives
with respect to every input and parameter. This is why reverse mode fits machine learning: a model
can have billions of parameters but typically ends in one scalar loss. PyTorch's autograd API
describes its core operation as a VJP.<Sidenote>Libraries differ in whether cotangents are written
as rows or stored in arrays shaped like the corresponding value. The computation is the same;
the latter avoids exposing transposed storage conventions.</Sidenote>

Reverse mode trades memory for work. It usually stores or reconstructs forward-pass values needed
by local backward rules. Activation checkpointing makes this trade explicit: save fewer values,
then recompute some of them during the backward pass.

## Useful matrix derivatives, derived

Formula tables are helpful after the method is clear. The differential derivations below show the
assumptions and transpose placement that a table can hide.

### Inverse

Let $Y=X^{-1}$ for invertible square $X$. Start from $XY=I$:

$$
d(XY)=(dX)Y+X(dY)=0.
$$

Multiply on the left by $X^{-1}$ and substitute $Y=X^{-1}$:

$$
\boxed{d(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}}.
$$

The order is essential. In practice, avoid forming an inverse merely to evaluate this rule;
linear solves are normally more stable and efficient.

### Determinant and log-determinant

Jacobi's formula gives

$$
d\det X=\det(X)\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX).
$$

Therefore

$$
\nabla_X\det X=\det(X)X^{-T}.
$$

For positive-determinant $X$, the log-determinant removes the leading determinant:

$$
d\log\det X=\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX),
\qquad
\boxed{\nabla_X\log\det X=X^{-T}}.
$$

For $X=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$, $\det X=5$ and

$$
X^{-T}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6&-0.2\\-0.2&0.4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check the top-left partial directly: changing $X_{11}$ changes the determinant
$X_{11}X_{22}-X_{12}X_{21}$ at rate $X_{22}=3$. The derivative of $\log\det X$ is therefore
$3/5=0.6$, matching the matrix above.

### Elementwise nonlinearities and softmax

For an elementwise function $y=\phi(x)$,

$$
J_\phi(x)=\operatorname{diag}(\phi'(x)),
$$

so a backward pass multiplies the incoming gradient entrywise by $\phi'(x)$. There is rarely a
reason to materialize the diagonal matrix.

Softmax is not elementwise because every output depends on every logit. If
$p_i=e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$, then

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j}=p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j),
\qquad
J_{\text{softmax}}=\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^T.
$$

Combine softmax with cross-entropy $L=-\log p_t$ for a one-hot target $t$, and the chain rule
simplifies to

$$
\boxed{\nabla_zL=p-e_t}.
$$

At $z=(1,2,-1)$ with target class 2, softmax is approximately
$(0.259496,0.705385,0.035119)$, the loss is $0.349012$, and

$$
\nabla_zL=(0.259496,-0.294615,0.035119).
$$

The entries sum to zero. Adding the same constant to every logit leaves softmax unchanged, so the
direction $(1,1,1)$ must have zero directional derivative; the zero sum confirms it.

## Hessians and second-order information

For scalar $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$, the **Hessian** differentiates the gradient:

$$
H_f(x)=\nabla_x^2f,
\qquad
(H_f)_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}.
$$

Where the mixed partial derivatives are continuous, $H_f$ is symmetric. Its quadratic form gives
the second-order term in the local Taylor approximation:

$$
f(x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T\Delta x
+\frac12\Delta x^TH_f(x)\Delta x.
$$

Positive curvature means the function bends upward along a direction; negative curvature means it
bends downward. At a stationary point, a positive-definite Hessian identifies a strict local
minimum, while an indefinite Hessian identifies a saddle.

For

$$
f(x)=\frac12x^THx+q^Tx,qquad
H=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad
q=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\3\end{bmatrix},
$$

we have $\nabla f=Hx+q$ and $\nabla^2f=H$. At $x=(2,-1)$, the gradient is $(6,3)$. For the
direction $v=(1,2)$, the **Hessian--vector product** (HVP) is

$$
Hv=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}6\\5\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Many second-order algorithms need $Hv$, not every entry of $H$. Automatic differentiation can
compute an HVP by differentiating the scalar $\nabla f(x)^Tv$, avoiding an $n\times n$ matrix.
That distinction is crucial when $n$ is millions or billions.

## How to derive and debug gradients reliably

A repeatable process prevents most matrix-calculus mistakes:

1. **Write every shape.** If a proposed gradient does not have the input's shape, it is wrong.
2. **Name intermediate values.** For $L=\ell(AX+b)$, write $z=AX+b$ first. Local rules stay small.
3. **Take differentials.** Apply the sum, product, transpose, and elementwise rules without
   commuting matrix factors.
4. **Collect one perturbation at a time.** Rewrite a scalar differential as
   $\operatorname{tr}(G^TdX)$, then read off $G=\nabla_XL$.
5. **Check a single entry.** Expand one partial derivative as a scalar sum. This catches misplaced
   transposes.
6. **Check one random direction.** Compare $\langle\nabla_XL,D\rangle_F$ with a finite difference.

Use the centered finite difference when possible:

$$
\frac{L(X+\epsilon D)-L(X-\epsilon D)}{2\epsilon}
\approx\langle\nabla_XL,D\rangle_F.
$$

It has $O(\epsilon^2)$ truncation error, compared with $O(\epsilon)$ for a one-sided difference.
Do not make $\epsilon$ arbitrarily tiny: subtracting nearly equal floating-point values eventually
causes roundoff error to dominate. A sweep over several powers of ten should show an interval where
the error falls, followed by a roundoff floor.

Also test non-generic cases deliberately: a broadcast dimension of one, repeated indices, a zero
input, or a non-symmetric matrix in a quadratic form. These are where a superficially plausible
formula often fails.

## Summary

- A derivative is a local linear map. A Jacobian is its matrix representation, and a gradient is
  the same-shape representation used for a scalar output.
- Differentials plus the Frobenius identity
  $dL=\operatorname{tr}((\nabla_XL)^TdX)$ turn matrix-gradient derivations into systematic algebra.
- Shapes determine transpose placement. Preserve matrix order, and state the Jacobian convention
  before comparing formulas from different sources.
- Forward mode pushes an input direction through JVPs. Reverse mode pulls a loss sensitivity
  through VJPs, which makes it efficient for scalar-loss models with many parameters.
- Full Jacobians and Hessians are often unnecessary. JVPs, VJPs, and HVPs compute the products an
  algorithm actually needs.
