## Background

Every computer comes with a source of random numbers that are uniform on $(0, 1)$. Deep
learning keeps asking for something else: a Gaussian to initialise weights, a token from a
softmax, an image from a generative model, or the average of a loss over a distribution we can
only evaluate pointwise. **Sampling**, also called **Monte Carlo sampling**, is the craft of
turning uniform numbers into draws from the distribution $p(\mathbf{z})$ we actually want, or
into averages under it.

It matters because most quantities in probabilistic modelling are integrals that have no closed
form: expectations, marginals, posterior predictions, and the gradient of the log-likelihood of
an energy-based model. Sampling replaces each integral by an average over draws. The price is
noise, and the art is in keeping that noise small, especially in high dimensions where the
simplest methods fail exponentially fast.

This post follows chapter 14 of Bishop and Bishop's *Deep Learning: Foundations and
Concepts*<Sidenote>C. M. Bishop and H. Bishop, *Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts*,
Springer, 2024, chapter 14, pp. 429–458. Equation numbers such as (14.27) refer to the book.
Every number in this post was computed by a script, using the same TypeScript code the figures
run, and cross-checked with SciPy where an independent answer exists.</Sidenote> equation by
equation, in order:

1. **Monte Carlo expectations**: why an average of samples estimates an integral, and how its
   error shrinks.
2. **Standard distributions**: the transformation method, Box–Muller and the Cholesky trick.
3. **Rejection sampling**, its adaptive version, and why it collapses in high dimensions.
4. **Importance sampling** and sampling-importance-resampling.
5. **Markov chain Monte Carlo**: Metropolis, the theory of Markov chains, Metropolis–Hastings,
   Gibbs sampling and ancestral sampling.
6. **Langevin sampling** and how it trains energy-based models.

Importance sampling has [its own post](/blog/importance-sampling/), which this one links to
rather than repeats.

## Monte Carlo expectations

### Expectations as averages

Often we do not want samples for their own sake but the **expectation** of a function $f$ under
a distribution $p$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] = \int f(\mathbf{z})\, p(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}. \tag{14.1}
$$

For discrete $\mathbf{z}$ the integral is a sum. The **Monte Carlo** idea is to draw $L$
independent samples $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(L)}$ from $p$ and average:

$$
\hat f = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big). \tag{14.2}
$$

Each term has expectation $\mathbb{E}[f]$, so their average does too: $\hat f$ is an
**unbiased estimator**, written

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{z})] \simeq \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big), \tag{14.3}
$$

where $\simeq$ means "equal on average over the randomness of the samples".<Sidenote>Exercise
14.1: $\mathbb{E}[\hat f] = \frac{1}{L}\sum_l \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})] = \frac{1}{L}
\cdot L\, \mathbb{E}[f] = \mathbb{E}[f]$, by linearity of expectation.</Sidenote> Because the
terms are independent, their variances add, and dividing by $L$ twice gives

$$
\operatorname{var}[\hat f] = \frac{1}{L}\, \mathbb{E}\big[(f - \mathbb{E}[f])^2\big]. \tag{14.4}
$$

<Sidenote>Exercise 14.2: $\operatorname{var}[\hat f] = \frac{1}{L^2}\sum_l
\operatorname{var}[f(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})] = \frac{1}{L^2} \cdot L \operatorname{var}[f]$, since the
covariances between independent terms are zero.</Sidenote>Two facts in (14.4) explain most of
this chapter:

- **The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{L}$, whatever the dimension of $\mathbf{z}$.** A grid over
  $\mathbf{z}$ needs exponentially many points in the dimension; Monte Carlo does not.
- **The constant is the variance of $f$ under $p$.** If $f$ is large where $p$ is small, a few
  rare samples carry the estimate, and $L$ must be large. And if the samples are not
  independent (as in MCMC below), the **effective sample size** is smaller than $L$.

### A worked example

Take $p(z) = e^{-z}$ for $z \ge 0$ (the exponential distribution) and $f(z) = z^2$. Then
$\mathbb{E}[f] = 2$ and $\operatorname{var}[f] = \mathbb{E}[z^4] - 4 = 24 - 4 = 20$, so by
(14.4) an average of $L$ samples has variance $20/L$.

Ten samples drawn with a fixed seed are $0.229, 0.378, 0.759, 2.194, 0.804, 1.160, 0.014,
0.736, 0.261, 0.698$. Their squares average to $\hat f = 0.867$, well below 2. That is not a
bug: with $L = 10$ the standard deviation of $\hat f$ is $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$, and one
sample ($2.194^2 = 4.81$) supplies more than half the sum. Repeating the experiment 2000 times
for each $L$ confirms both the mean and the variance:

| samples $L$ | mean of $\hat f$ over 2000 runs | variance of $\hat f$ | (14.4): $20/L$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 1.979 | 1.934 | 2.000 |
| 100 | 2.008 | 0.206 | 0.200 |
| 1000 | 1.997 | 0.0196 | 0.0200 |

## Sampling from standard distributions

### The transformation method

Suppose $z$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$ and we set $y = g(z)$ for some increasing function $g$. A
small interval $\mathrm{d}z$ maps to an interval $\mathrm{d}y$ holding the same probability, so
the densities are related by

$$
p(y) = p(z) \left| \frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}y} \right|, \tag{14.5}
$$

with $p(z) = 1$ here. We want to choose $g$ so that $p(y)$ is a target density. Integrating
(14.5) from $-\infty$ to $y$ gives

$$
z = h(y) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{y} p(\hat y)\, \mathrm{d}\hat y, \tag{14.6}
$$

the **cumulative distribution function** (CDF) of the target. So $y = h^{-1}(z)$: **push
uniform numbers through the inverse CDF**.<Sidenote>Exercise 14.3: $P(y \le a) = P(h^{-1}(z)
\le a) = P(z \le h(a)) = h(a)$, because $h$ is increasing and $z$ is uniform. A variable whose
CDF is $h$ has density $h' = p$.</Sidenote> Geometrically, a uniform $z$ on the vertical axis
hits the CDF where it is steep (where $p$ is large) for a wide range of $z$ values, so many
draws land there.

For the **exponential distribution**

$$
p(y) = \lambda \exp(-\lambda y), \qquad 0 \le y < \infty, \tag{14.7}
$$

the CDF is $h(y) = 1 - e^{-\lambda y}$, so $y = -\lambda^{-1}\ln(1 - z)$. Check one value with
$\lambda = 1$: $z = 0.3$ gives $y = -\ln 0.7 = 0.357$, and indeed $h(0.357) = 1 - e^{-0.357} =
0.3$. In the figure, follow the dashed guide from $z$ on the left axis across to the CDF and down
to $y$; the histogram of the $y$ values fills in the target.

The **Cauchy distribution**

$$
p(y) = \frac{1}{\pi}\, \frac{1}{1 + y^2} \tag{14.8}
$$

has CDF $h(y) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi \arctan y$, so $y = \tan\big(\pi(z - \tfrac12)\big)$
(Exercise 14.4). For example $z = 0.75$ gives $y = \tan(\pi/4) = 1$ and $z = 0.9$ gives $y =
3.078$. The Cauchy has such heavy tails that it has no mean, which makes it a useful proposal
later: it is hard to find a bell-shaped density it does not eventually cover.

### Several variables and Box–Muller

With several variables the derivative in (14.5) becomes the **Jacobian determinant** of the
change of variables:

$$
p(y_1, \dots, y_M) = p(z_1, \dots, z_M) \left| \frac{\partial(z_1, \dots, z_M)}{\partial(y_1, \dots, y_M)} \right|. \tag{14.9}
$$

The Gaussian CDF has no closed-form inverse, so the transformation method does not apply
directly. The **Box–Muller method** gets around this with two variables at once. Draw $z_1, z_2$
uniformly on $(-1, 1)$ (by $z \to 2z - 1$) and discard the pair unless $r^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2 \le
1$. The kept pairs are uniform in the unit disc, with density $1/\pi$. Then set

$$
y_1 = z_1 \left( \frac{-2 \ln r^2}{r^2} \right)^{1/2}, \tag{14.10}
$$

$$
y_2 = z_2 \left( \frac{-2 \ln r^2}{r^2} \right)^{1/2}. \tag{14.11}
$$

The map keeps the direction of $(z_1, z_2)$ and moves the radius $r$ to $\rho =
\sqrt{-2\ln r^2}$. Applying (14.9) gives

$$
p(y_1, y_2) = p(z_1, z_2) \left| \frac{\partial(z_1, z_2)}{\partial(y_1, y_2)} \right| =
\left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp(-y_1^2/2) \right] \left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp(-y_2^2/2) \right], \tag{14.12}
$$

so $y_1$ and $y_2$ are **independent standard Gaussians**.<Sidenote>Exercise 14.5, in polar
coordinates. The angle $\theta$ is uniform and unchanged. The squared radius $r^2$ is uniform on
$(0, 1)$, because the area inside radius $r$ is $\pi r^2$. The new squared radius is $\rho^2 =
-2\ln r^2$, an exponential variable with rate $\tfrac12$ by the transformation method above. A
uniform angle and $\rho^2 \sim \text{Exp}(1/2)$ is exactly the joint density
$\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\rho^2/2}$ of two independent standard Gaussians.</Sidenote> Check one pair:
$(z_1, z_2) = (0.3, -0.4)$ has $r^2 = 0.25$ and factor $\sqrt{-2\ln 0.25 / 0.25} = 3.330$, so
$(y_1, y_2) = (0.999, -1.332)$. A numerical Jacobian at this point gives $p(z)/|\det J| =
0.039789$, equal to the standard bivariate Gaussian density at $(0.999, -1.332)$.

/>

To get a Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, use $\sigma y + \mu$. For a
**multivariate Gaussian** with mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ and covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, factor
$\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top$ by the **Cholesky decomposition** ($\mathbf{L}$
lower triangular) and set $\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}$ with
$\mathbf{z}$ a vector of independent standard Gaussians. Then $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] =
\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\operatorname{cov}[\mathbf{y}] = \mathbf{L}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}
\mathbf{z}^\top]\,\mathbf{L}^\top = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top = \boldsymbol\Sigma$ (Exercise
14.6), and a linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian. For example

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & \sqrt2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & \sqrt2 \end{pmatrix},
$$

so the standard pair $\mathbf{z} = (1, -0.5)$ becomes $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z} = (2,\ 1 -
0.707) = (2, 0.293)$ for $\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf{0}$.

The transformation method needs a CDF we can compute *and* invert. Few distributions allow that,
so the rest of the chapter builds general tools on top of these simple ones.

## Rejection sampling

### The algorithm

Suppose we can evaluate the target only up to a constant:

$$
p(\mathbf{z}) = \frac{1}{Z_p}\, \tilde p(\mathbf{z}), \tag{14.13}
$$

where $\tilde p$ is easy to compute and the **normalizing constant** $Z_p$ is unknown. This is
the usual situation: a posterior is likelihood × prior divided by an intractable evidence.

**Rejection sampling** uses a simpler **proposal distribution** $q(\mathbf{z})$ that we can sample
from, and a constant $k$ large enough that the **comparison function** $kq(\mathbf{z})$ lies
above $\tilde p(\mathbf{z})$ everywhere. One step:

1. Draw $z_0$ from $q$.
2. Draw $u_0$ uniformly on $[0, kq(z_0)]$. The pair $(z_0, u_0)$ is now uniform under the curve
   $kq$.
3. Keep $z_0$ if $u_0 \le \tilde p(z_0)$; otherwise reject it.

The kept pairs are uniform under $\tilde p$, so their $z$ values are distributed as $p$. A
proposal $z$ is accepted with probability $\tilde p(z)/kq(z)$, so the overall acceptance rate is

$$
p(\text{accept}) = \int \left\{ \frac{\tilde p(z)}{kq(z)} \right\} q(z)\, \mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{k}\int \tilde p(z)\, \mathrm{d}z. \tag{14.14}
$$

It is the area under $\tilde p$ divided by the area under $kq$, so $k$ should be as small as
possible while keeping $kq \ge \tilde p$.<Sidenote>Exercise 14.7: the density of an accepted $z$
is $q(z) \cdot \tilde p(z)/kq(z)$ divided by the acceptance probability (14.14), which gives
$\tilde p(z) / \int \tilde p = p(z)$. The $q$ and $k$ cancel; they only affect how often we
succeed.</Sidenote>

### Gamma samples from a Cauchy proposal

The book's example is the **gamma distribution**

$$
\operatorname{Gam}(z \mid a, b) = \frac{b^a z^{a-1} \exp(-bz)}{\Gamma(a)}, \tag{14.15}
$$

which is bell-shaped for $a > 1$. A Cauchy is bell-shaped too and can be sampled by the
transformation method. Shifting and scaling it, $z = b \tan y + c$ with $y$ uniform on
$(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, gives<Sidenote>Exercise 14.8. The book's wording says $y$ uniform on $[0, 1]$;
the tangent must be applied to an angle uniform on $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, which is $\pi(u - \tfrac12)$
for $u$ uniform on $(0, 1)$. Also note the book reuses the letter $b$: in (14.16) it is the
Cauchy's scale, not the gamma's rate. Below, the scale is written $s$.</Sidenote>

$$
q(z) = \frac{k}{1 + (z - c)^2 / b^2}, \tag{14.16}
$$

where the constant $k$ in the numerator already folds in the comparison constant. The book
suggests $c = a - 1$ (the gamma's mode) and squared scale $2a - 1$. The book does not say which
$a$ it plots; here $a = 10$ and rate $1$, so $c = 9$ and $s = \sqrt{19} = 4.359$.

With the normalized Cauchy $q(z) = 1/\big(\pi s(1 + (z - c)^2/s^2)\big)$ and $\tilde p$ the
normalized gamma, the smallest valid constant is the largest ratio $\tilde p/q$. A grid search
puts it at $z = 9$, the mode, with $k_{\min} = 1.8042$ (SciPy's optimiser agrees). By (14.14)
the acceptance rate is $1/1.8042 = 0.554$. Three proposals show the acceptance probability
$\tilde p/kq$ at work:

| $z_0$ | $\tilde p(z_0)$ | $kq(z_0)$ | accept with probability |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 5 | 0.03627 | 0.07152 | 0.507 |
| 12 | 0.08736 | 0.08941 | 0.977 |
| 20 | 0.00291 | 0.01788 | 0.163 |

Running 100,000 proposals with the same code accepts 55.2% and gives a sample mean of 9.98,
against the gamma mean $a/b = 10$.

### Adaptive rejection sampling

Finding a tight $kq$ by hand is hard. **Adaptive rejection sampling** (Gilks and Wild, 1992)
builds the envelope from the target itself, provided $p$ is **log concave**, meaning $\ln p(z)$
has a non-increasing derivative. A concave function lies below each of its tangent lines. So the
tangents of $\ln \tilde p$ at a few grid points, cut where neighbours intersect, form a piecewise
linear upper bound of $\ln \tilde p$. Exponentiating gives an envelope made of exponential
pieces:

$$
q(z) = k_i \lambda_i \exp\{-\lambda_i (z - z_{i-1})\}, \qquad z_{i-1} < z \le z_i. \tag{14.17}
$$

Here $-\lambda_i$ is the slope of tangent $i$ and $z_{i-1}, z_i$ are the intersection points
that bound its piece. Sampling from (14.17) is easy: pick piece $i$ with probability equal to its
mass, then invert that piece's exponential CDF as in (14.7) (Exercise 14.10). If the sample is
rejected, it becomes a new grid point, which adds a tangent exactly where the envelope was loose.

**Worked example.** Take $\tilde p(z) = z^2 e^{-z}$ on $z > 0$ (an unnormalized Gam$(z \mid 3, 1)$,
whose area is $\Gamma(3) = 2$), so $\ln \tilde p(z) = 2\ln z - z$ with derivative $2/z - 1$, which
decreases: log concave. Start with grid points $1$ and $5$:

- At $z = 1$: $\ln \tilde p = -1$, slope $+1$. At $z = 5$: $\ln\tilde p = 2\ln 5 - 5 = -1.781$,
  slope $-0.6$.
- The tangents $-1 + (z - 1)$ and $-1.781 - 0.6(z - 5)$ meet at $z_1 = 2.012$.
- Piece 1, on $(0, 2.012]$, has mass $\int_0^{2.012} e^{z - 2}\, \mathrm{d}z = 0.877$. Piece 2,
  on $(2.012, \infty)$, has mass $e^{0.0118}/0.6 = 1.686$. Total $2.563$.
- The acceptance rate is $\int\tilde p / \int\text{envelope} = 2/2.563 = 0.780$. SciPy's
  quadrature gives the same masses.

In book notation, $\lambda_1 = -1$ (the first piece rises) and $\lambda_2 = 0.6$. Requiring
continuity at each $z_i$ and total mass 1 (Exercise 14.9) gives $k_i = e^{u_i(z_{i-1})} /
(\lambda_i M)$, where $u_i$ is tangent $i$ and $M$ the total mass. That is $k_1 = -0.053$ and
$k_2 = 0.658$; the product $k_i\lambda_i$ is positive in both pieces. Step through the figure.
The first rejection, at draw 9 ($z = 2.631$, right next to the kink of the first envelope),
lowers the envelope mass to $2.199$ and raises the acceptance rate to $0.910$. After 60 draws
there are 7 grid points, 55 draws were kept, and the acceptance rate is $0.958$.

### Why rejection fails in high dimensions

Consider an artificial but revealing case: target $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_p^2
\mathbf{I})$ in $D$ dimensions, proposal $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_q^2 \mathbf{I})$ with
$\sigma_q > \sigma_p$. The ratio $p/q$ peaks at the origin, where it is $(\sigma_q/\sigma_p)^D$,
so $k = (\sigma_q/\sigma_p)^D$. Both densities are normalized, so by (14.14) the acceptance rate
is $1/k = (\sigma_p/\sigma_q)^D$: **it decays exponentially with dimension**.

## Importance sampling

### Weighting instead of rejecting

**Importance sampling** estimates expectations directly, without producing samples from $p$. A
first idea is a grid:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] \simeq \sum_{l=1}^{L} p\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big) f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big), \tag{14.18}
$$

but the number of grid points grows exponentially with dimension, and most of them sit where
$p$ is negligible. Instead, draw from a proposal $q$ that is easy to sample and correct for the
mismatch:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] = \int f(\mathbf{z}) \frac{p(\mathbf{z})}{q(\mathbf{z})} q(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z} \simeq \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} \frac{p(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})}{q(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})} f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big). \tag{14.19}
$$

The ratios $r_l = p(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})/q(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})$ are the **importance weights**. A sample
from a region that $q$ over-visits gets a small weight, one from a region $q$ under-visits gets a
large weight, and no sample is thrown away. The [Importance Sampling](/blog/importance-sampling/)
post works this through for a rare-event probability, where the best $q$ is far from $p$.

### Unknown normalizing constants

When $p = \tilde p/Z_p$ and $q = \tilde q/Z_q$ are both known only up to constants,

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] = \frac{Z_q}{Z_p} \int f(\mathbf{z}) \frac{\tilde p(\mathbf{z})}{\tilde q(\mathbf{z})} q(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z} \simeq \frac{Z_q}{Z_p}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} \tilde r_l f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big), \tag{14.20}
$$

with $\tilde r_l = \tilde p(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})/\tilde q(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})$. The same samples estimate the
unknown ratio of constants:

$$
\frac{Z_p}{Z_q} = \frac{1}{Z_q}\int \tilde p(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z} = \int \frac{\tilde p(\mathbf{z})}{\tilde q(\mathbf{z})} q(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z} \simeq \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} \tilde r_l. \tag{14.21}
$$

Dividing (14.20) by (14.21), the constants cancel and the estimate becomes a weighted average,

$$
\mathbb{E}[f] \simeq \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l f\big(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\big), \tag{14.22}
$$

$$
w_l = \frac{\tilde r_l}{\sum_m \tilde r_m} = \frac{\tilde p(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}) / q(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})}{\sum_m \tilde p(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}) / q(\mathbf{z}^{(m)})}, \tag{14.23}
$$

with **self-normalized weights** $w_l \ge 0$ that sum to one. This estimator is slightly biased for
finite $L$ (it is a ratio of two estimates), but consistent as $L \to \infty$.

The danger is a $q$ that misses where $p f$ is large. Then a few weights dominate, the **effective
sample size** $1/\sum_l w_l^2$ (equal to $L$ for equal weights, 1 for a single nonzero weight)
collapses, and worse, if *no* sample lands in the important region, the weights can look fine
while the answer is wrong. **The proposal must not be small where $p$ is significant.**

### Sampling-importance-resampling

**Sampling-importance-resampling** (SIR) turns weights into samples without needing the constant
$k$ of rejection sampling. Draw $L$ samples from $q$, compute the weights (14.23), then draw $L$
new samples from the discrete distribution on $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(L)}$ with
probabilities $w_1, \dots, w_L$. In one dimension the resampled values have CDF

$$
p(z \le a) = \sum_{l:\, z^{(l)} \le a} w_l = \frac{\sum_l I(z^{(l)} \le a)\, \tilde p(z^{(l)})/q(z^{(l)})}{\sum_l \tilde p(z^{(l)})/q(z^{(l)})}, \tag{14.24}
$$

where $I(\cdot)$ is 1 when its argument is true and 0 otherwise. As $L \to \infty$ each sum
becomes an integral against $q$, and $q$ cancels:

$$
p(z \le a) = \frac{\int I(z \le a) \{\tilde p(z)/q(z)\} q(z)\, \mathrm{d}z}{\int \{\tilde p(z)/q(z)\} q(z)\, \mathrm{d}z} = \frac{\int I(z \le a)\, \tilde p(z)\, \mathrm{d}z}{\int \tilde p(z)\, \mathrm{d}z} = \int I(z \le a)\, p(z)\, \mathrm{d}z, \tag{14.25}
$$

the CDF of $p$. For finite $L$ the resampled values are only approximately from $p$; if $q = p$
all weights are $1/L$ and resampling changes nothing. Moments do not need the resampling step at
all:

$$
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{z})] = \frac{\int f(\mathbf{z}) [\tilde p(\mathbf{z})/q(\mathbf{z})] q(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\int [\tilde p(\mathbf{z})/q(\mathbf{z})] q(\mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}} \simeq \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l f(\mathbf{z}_l). \tag{14.26}
$$

**Worked example.** The target is two bumps, $\tilde p(z) = 3e^{-2(z+1)^2} + 2e^{-(z-2)^2/0.72}$:
Gaussians of standard deviation $0.5$ at $-1$ and $0.6$ at $2$. Its constant is $Z_p =
\sqrt{2\pi}(3 \cdot 0.5 + 2 \cdot 0.6) = 6.768$ and its mean is $(1.5 \cdot (-1) + 1.2 \cdot 2)/2.7
= 1/3$. The proposal is $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_q^2)$ with $L = 200$:

| proposal $\sigma_q$ | effective sample size | $\sum_l w_l z_l$ (true 0.333) | $Z_p$ by (14.21) (true 6.768) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 64.4 | 0.454 | 6.186 |
| 2 | 111.0 | 0.368 | 7.060 |
| 1 | 64.5 | 0.380 | 7.306 |
| 0.5 | 35.1 | **−0.487** | **3.267** |

With $\sigma_q = 2$ the proposal covers both bumps and the estimates are good. With $\sigma_q =
0.5$ only one of the 200 samples lands beyond $z = 1.2$, so the right bump is essentially
invisible: the mean comes out negative and $Z_p$ is half its true value. Yet the effective sample
size, 35, does not look alarming. That is the book's warning in numbers: importance sampling can
be **arbitrarily wrong with no diagnostic sign**.

## Markov chain Monte Carlo

Rejection and importance sampling draw every proposal from the same fixed $q$, so $q$ must
resemble $p$ globally, which is impossible in high dimensions. **Markov chain Monte Carlo**
(MCMC) only asks $q$ to make a *local* move from the current state $\mathbf{z}^{(\tau)}$: the
proposal is $q(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}^{(\tau)})$, and the sequence of states forms a **Markov
chain**. As before we only need $\tilde p$, not $Z_p$.

### The Metropolis algorithm

In the **Metropolis algorithm** (Metropolis et al., 1953) the proposal is **symmetric**,
$q(\mathbf{z}_A \mid \mathbf{z}_B) = q(\mathbf{z}_B \mid \mathbf{z}_A)$; a Gaussian centred on the
current state is the usual choice. A candidate $\mathbf{z}^\star$ is accepted with probability

$$
A(\mathbf{z}^\star, \mathbf{z}^{(\tau)}) = \min\left(1, \frac{\tilde p(\mathbf{z}^\star)}{\tilde p(\mathbf{z}^{(\tau)})}\right). \tag{14.27}
$$

Moves uphill (to higher $\tilde p$) are always accepted; moves downhill are accepted with
probability equal to the density ratio, so the chain can still explore the tails. The ratio
cancels $Z_p$. On rejection the chain **stays put and records the current state again**, unlike
rejection sampling, which discards the draw. Those repeats are what make the chain spend the
right amount of time in each region. Algorithm 14.1 in Python:

```python
import numpy as np

def metropolis(log_p_tilde, z0, rho, T, rng):
    z, chain, accepted = np.asarray(z0, float), [], 0
    for _ in range(T):
        z_star = z + rho * rng.standard_normal(z.shape)   # symmetric proposal
        if np.log(rng.uniform()) < log_p_tilde(z_star) - log_p_tilde(z):
            z, accepted = z_star, accepted + 1             # accept
        chain.append(z)                                    # a rejection repeats z
    return np.array(chain), accepted / T
```

Working in logs avoids overflow, and comparing $\ln u < \ln \tilde p(\mathbf{z}^\star) - \ln
\tilde p(\mathbf{z})$ is the same test as $u < A$. Successive states are correlated, so to get
nearly independent samples keep only every $M$-th state.

The figure reproduces the book's Figure 14.9 setting: a correlated Gaussian centred at $(1.5,
1.5)$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{\max} = 0.8$ along its long axis and $\sigma_{\min} = 0.2$
across it (covariance entries $0.34$ on the diagonal, $0.30$ off it), and an isotropic proposal
with $\rho = 0.2$. Starting from $(0.2, 0.4)$, 55 of the first 150 candidates are rejected (the
book reports 43 of 150 for its own run).

### Random walks are slow

Why does the chain crawl along the ellipse? Consider the simplest **random walk**, on the
integers:

$$
p(z^{(\tau+1)} = z^{(\tau)}) = 0.5, \tag{14.28}
$$

$$
p(z^{(\tau+1)} = z^{(\tau)} + 1) = 0.25, \tag{14.29}
$$

$$
p(z^{(\tau+1)} = z^{(\tau)} - 1) = 0.25. \tag{14.30}
$$

Starting from $z^{(0)} = 0$, symmetry gives $\mathbb{E}[z^{(\tau)}] = 0$. Each step adds an
increment $\delta$ with mean 0 and $\mathbb{E}[\delta^2] = \tfrac12$, independent of the current
position, so $\mathbb{E}[(z^{(\tau)})^2] = \mathbb{E}[(z^{(\tau-1)})^2] + \tfrac12$ and by induction
$\mathbb{E}[(z^{(\tau)})^2] = \tau/2$ (Exercise 14.11). Propagating the exact distribution of the
walk confirms $\mathbb{E}[z^2] = 0.5, 1, 5, 50$ after $1, 2, 10, 100$ steps. **The typical
distance travelled grows only as $\sqrt\tau$**: covering 14 states takes about 400 steps.

### Markov chains, invariance and detailed balance

When does a chain sample the right distribution? A **first-order Markov chain** is a sequence
$\mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(M)}$ in which each state depends on the past only through
the previous state:

$$
p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \mid \mathbf{z}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{z}^{(m)}) = p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \mid \mathbf{z}^{(m)}). \tag{14.31}
$$

The chain is specified by an initial distribution and **transition probabilities**
$T_m(\mathbf{z}^{(m)}, \mathbf{z}^{(m+1)}) \equiv p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \mid \mathbf{z}^{(m)})$; it is
**homogeneous** if they do not depend on $m$. The marginal distribution moves forward by

$$
p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)}) = \int p(\mathbf{z}^{(m+1)} \mid \mathbf{z}^{(m)})\, p(\mathbf{z}^{(m)})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}^{(m)}. \tag{14.32}
$$

A distribution $p^\star$ is **invariant** (or **stationary**) if one step leaves it unchanged:

$$
p^\star(\mathbf{z}) = \int T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z})\, p^\star(\mathbf{z}')\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}'. \tag{14.33}
$$

A chain can have several invariant distributions; with $T$ the identity, every distribution is
invariant. A simple **sufficient** condition for $p^\star$ to be invariant is **detailed
balance**:

$$
p^\star(\mathbf{z})\, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}') = p^\star(\mathbf{z}')\, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}). \tag{14.34}
$$

It says the probability flow from $\mathbf{z}$ to $\mathbf{z}'$ equals the flow back. Integrating
over $\mathbf{z}'$ shows invariance:

$$
\int p^\star(\mathbf{z}')\, T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}' = \int p^\star(\mathbf{z})\, T(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}')\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}' \tag{14.35}
$$

$$
= p^\star(\mathbf{z}) \int p(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}' \tag{14.36}
$$

$$
= p^\star(\mathbf{z}). \tag{14.37}
$$

A chain that satisfies detailed balance is called **reversible**. Invariance is not enough: we
also need the chain to *reach* $p^\star$ from any starting distribution. That property is
**ergodicity**, and the limit is then the **equilibrium distribution**, necessarily unique. A
homogeneous chain is ergodic under weak conditions on $p^\star$ and $T$ (Neal, 1993).

**Worked example.** Five states with $\tilde p = (1, 3, 6, 4, 2)$, so $p^\star = (1, 3, 6, 4, 2)/16$.
The proposal moves one state left or right with probability $\tfrac12$ each (a proposal off the
end is rejected), and (14.27) decides acceptance. From state 1, the move to 0 is downhill and
accepted with probability $1/3$, so $T(1, 0) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$; the move to
2 is uphill, so $T(1, 2) = \tfrac12$; the rest, $T(1, 1) = \tfrac13$, is staying put. Detailed
balance holds pair by pair because both flows equal $\tfrac12\min(p^\star_i, p^\star_j)$:

| pair $(i, j)$ | $p^\star_i\, T(i, j)$ | $p^\star_j\, T(j, i)$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| (0, 1) | $\tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \tfrac12 = 0.03125$ | $\tfrac{3}{16} \cdot \tfrac16 = 0.03125$ |
| (1, 2) | 0.09375 | 0.09375 |
| (2, 3) | 0.12500 | 0.12500 |
| (3, 4) | 0.06250 | 0.06250 |

Start with all mass on state 0 and apply (14.32) repeatedly. The total variation distance to
$p^\star$ is $0.75$ after one step, $0.178$ after 5, $0.0385$ after 10 and $0.0017$ after 20; from
then on each step multiplies it by $0.732$, the second-largest eigenvalue of $T$ (NumPy's `eig`
gives $0.73178$). In the figure, watch the two arrows between each pair: at first far more mass
flows right than left, and the flows equalise as the bars reach the outlines.

Practical samplers combine several **base transitions** $B_1, \dots, B_K$, either as a mixture

$$
T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k B_k(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}), \tag{14.38}
$$

with $\alpha_k > 0$ and $\sum_k \alpha_k = 1$, or by applying them in sequence:

$$
T(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}_1} \cdots \sum_{\mathbf{z}_{K-1}} B_1(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}_1) \cdots B_{K-1}(\mathbf{z}_{K-2}, \mathbf{z}_{K-1})\, B_K(\mathbf{z}_{K-1}, \mathbf{z}). \tag{14.39}
$$

If $p^\star$ is invariant under every $B_k$, it is invariant under both. Detailed balance survives
the mixture but not the sequence, unless the sequence is symmetrised as $B_1, \dots, B_K, B_K,
\dots, B_1$. The typical use is a set of base transitions that each update only some of the
variables, which is exactly Gibbs sampling below.<Sidenote>The book writes the sums in (14.39)
up to $\mathbf{z}_{n-1}$; the indices should run to $\mathbf{z}_{K-1}$, matching the last
factor.</Sidenote>

### Metropolis–Hastings

The **Metropolis–Hastings algorithm** (Hastings, 1970) allows an asymmetric proposal $q_k$. The
acceptance probability gains a correction for the asymmetry:

$$
A_k(\mathbf{z}^\star, \mathbf{z}^{(\tau)}) = \min\left(1, \frac{\tilde p(\mathbf{z}^\star)\, q_k(\mathbf{z}^{(\tau)} \mid \mathbf{z}^\star)}{\tilde p(\mathbf{z}^{(\tau)})\, q_k(\mathbf{z}^\star \mid \mathbf{z}^{(\tau)})}\right). \tag{14.40}
$$

If $q_k$ is symmetric the $q$ terms cancel and (14.40) is (14.27). The index $k$ labels a set of
available proposals; Algorithm 14.2 picks one per iteration. The extra factor rewards moves the
proposal finds hard to reverse. To prove the chain samples $p$, check detailed balance (14.34)
for the transition "propose $\mathbf{z}'$, then accept":

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\mathbf{z})\, q_k(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z})\, A_k(\mathbf{z}', \mathbf{z}) &= \min\big(p(\mathbf{z}) q_k(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z}),\ p(\mathbf{z}') q_k(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}')\big) \\
&= \min\big(p(\mathbf{z}') q_k(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}'),\ p(\mathbf{z}) q_k(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z})\big) \\
&= p(\mathbf{z}')\, q_k(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}')\, A_k(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}').
\end{aligned} \tag{14.41}
$$

The first line multiplies $p(\mathbf{z}) q_k(\mathbf{z}' \mid \mathbf{z})$ into both arguments of
the min in (14.40); the expression is symmetric in $\mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{z}'$, so it equals
the reverse flow. (Rejections only add mass to staying put, which trivially balances.)

**Choosing the proposal scale.** With a Gaussian proposal of standard deviation $\rho$ there is
a trade-off. Small $\rho$: almost every move is accepted, but the chain random-walks in tiny
steps. Large $\rho$: most candidates land in low-probability regions and are rejected. The book's
argument (Figure 14.10): to keep rejections low, $\rho$ should be of order $\sigma_{\min}$, and
then crossing the long axis by a random walk takes of order $(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min})^2$
steps, here $(0.8/0.2)^2 = 16$. It adds that in two dimensions the higher rejection rate of a
larger $\rho$ is offset by the larger accepted steps. The measurements bear that out. For each
$\rho$ we ran 100,000 moves on the Gaussian above, dropped the first 10% and measured the
**integrated autocorrelation time** $\tau_{\text{int}} = 1 + 2\sum_k \rho_k$ of the long-axis
coordinate ($\rho_k$ is the autocorrelation at lag $k$). One chain of $n$ moves is worth about
$n/\tau_{\text{int}}$ independent samples.

A high acceptance rate is not the goal. The best $\rho$ here is around $0.8$–$1.2$, with only

### Gibbs sampling

**Gibbs sampling** (Geman and Geman, 1984) updates one variable at a time by drawing it from its
**conditional distribution** given all the others, $p(z_i \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus i})$, where
$\mathbf{z}_{\setminus i}$ is $\mathbf{z}$ without $z_i$. With three variables, one sweep is

$$
z_1^{(\tau+1)} \sim p(z_1 \mid z_2^{(\tau)}, z_3^{(\tau)}), \tag{14.42}
$$

$$
z_2^{(\tau+1)} \sim p(z_2 \mid z_1^{(\tau+1)}, z_3^{(\tau)}), \tag{14.43}
$$

$$
z_3^{(\tau+1)} \sim p(z_3 \mid z_1^{(\tau+1)}, z_2^{(\tau+1)}), \tag{14.44}
$$

each update using the newest values of the others. Why it works: a step that redraws $z_i$
leaves $\mathbf{z}_{\setminus i}$ alone, so the marginal $p(\mathbf{z}_{\setminus i})$ is
unchanged, and it draws $z_i$ from the correct conditional. Marginal times conditional is the
joint, so $p$ is invariant under every step. Ergodicity needs more: it is guaranteed if no
conditional is zero anywhere, since then any state can reach any other in one sweep.<Sidenote>
Exercise 14.13 asks about a target that is uniform on two squares touching only at a corner.
Each axis-aligned move stays inside one square, so a chain started in one square never reaches
the other: Gibbs is not ergodic there and would sample the wrong distribution.</Sidenote>

Gibbs is a special case of Metropolis–Hastings. Take the proposal $q_k(\mathbf{z}^\star \mid
\mathbf{z}) = p(z_k^\star \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})$, which leaves $\mathbf{z}^\star_{\setminus k}
= \mathbf{z}_{\setminus k}$, and write $p(\mathbf{z}) = p(z_k \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})
p(\mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})$. The ratio in (14.40) is then

$$
A(\mathbf{z}^\star, \mathbf{z}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{z}^\star)\, q_k(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}^\star)}{p(\mathbf{z})\, q_k(\mathbf{z}^\star \mid \mathbf{z})} = \frac{p(z^\star_k \mid \mathbf{z}^\star_{\setminus k})\, p(\mathbf{z}^\star_{\setminus k})\, p(z_k \mid \mathbf{z}^\star_{\setminus k})}{p(z_k \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})\, p(\mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})\, p(z^\star_k \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus k})} = 1, \tag{14.45}
$$

because every starred "rest" equals the unstarred one. **Every Gibbs move is accepted.**

For a Gaussian with precision matrix $\boldsymbol\Lambda = \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$ the conditionals
are Gaussian with mean $\mu_i - \Lambda_{ii}^{-1}\sum_{j \ne i}\Lambda_{ij}(z_j - \mu_j)$ and
variance $1/\Lambda_{ii}$, which gives a ten-line sampler (Algorithm 14.3, with the
over-relaxation parameter of the next section):

```python
def gibbs(mu, Lam, z0, T, rng, alpha=0.0):
    z, chain = np.array(z0, float), []
    for _ in range(T):
        for i in range(len(z)):
            rest = [j for j in range(len(z)) if j != i]
            m = mu[i] - Lam[i, rest] @ (z[rest] - mu[rest]) / Lam[i, i]  # conditional mean
            s = 1 / np.sqrt(Lam[i, i])                                     # conditional sd
            z[i] = m + alpha * (z[i] - m) + s * np.sqrt(1 - alpha**2) * rng.standard_normal()
        chain.append(z.copy())
    return np.array(chain)
```

On the Gaussian above the conditional standard deviation is $l = 1/\sqrt{\Lambda_{11}} = 0.274$
and the marginal one is $L = \sqrt{\Sigma_{11}} = 0.583$. Steps are of size $l$, so by the
random-walk argument an independent sample takes of order $(L/l)^2 = 4.5$ sweeps (Figure
14.11). For a bivariate Gaussian with correlation $r$ this can be made exact: each coordinate,
sampled once per sweep, follows an autoregression with coefficient $r^2$, whose
integrated autocorrelation time is $(1 + r^2)/(1 - r^2)$. Here $r = 0.882$, so $8.0$ sweeps,
or $16.1$ single-coordinate updates. The simulation measures $17.8$ updates.

### Over-relaxation, blocking and graphs

**Over-relaxation** (Adler, 1981) reduces the random walk when the conditionals are Gaussian with
mean $\mu_i$ and variance $\sigma_i^2$. Instead of a fresh draw, set

$$
z_i' = \mu_i + \alpha_i (z_i - \mu_i) + \sigma_i (1 - \alpha_i^2)^{1/2} \nu, \tag{14.46}
$$

with $\nu$ a standard Gaussian and $-1 < \alpha_i < 1$. If $z_i$ has mean $\mu_i$ and variance
$\sigma_i^2$, so does $z_i'$: its mean is $\mu_i$ and its variance is $\alpha_i^2\sigma_i^2 +
\sigma_i^2(1 - \alpha_i^2) = \sigma_i^2$ (Exercise 14.14). So $p$ stays invariant. With $\alpha
= 0$ this is plain Gibbs; with $\alpha < 0$ the new value lands on the *opposite* side of the
conditional mean, so successive updates keep moving in the same direction instead of
diffusing. On our Gaussian (100,000 updates each):

| $\alpha$ | +0.5 | 0 (Gibbs) | −0.5 | −0.8 | −0.9 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\tau_{\text{int}}$ (updates) | 50.4 | 17.8 | 6.0 | 4.6 | 4.3 |

At $\alpha = -0.9$ the chain mixes about four times faster than Gibbs. The idea also works on
non-Gaussian targets that have Gaussian conditionals, such as $p(z, y) \propto \exp(-z^2y^2)$,
and **ordered over-relaxation** (Neal, 1999) generalises it further.

Gibbs needs conditionals that are easy to sample. In a directed graphical model the conditional
of a node depends only on its **Markov blanket**: its parents, children and co-parents (Figure
14.12). Many common choices of node conditionals make $p(z_i \mid \mathbf{z}_{\setminus i})$ log
concave, so adaptive rejection sampling can draw from them. Finally, **blocking Gibbs** (Jensen,
Kong and Kjaerulff, 1995) samples groups of variables jointly given the rest, a middle ground
between one-at-a-time updates and (intractable) joint draws.

### Ancestral sampling

For a directed graph, sampling the joint distribution needs no Markov chain at all. The joint
factorises as

$$
p(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{i=1}^{M} p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i)), \tag{14.47}
$$

where $\mathrm{pa}(i)$ are the parents of node $i$. **Ancestral sampling** visits the nodes in
an order where parents come first, and draws each from its conditional given the parent values
already sampled. One pass gives one exact, independent sample from the joint.

With **evidence**, some nodes observed, the simplest extension is **logic sampling** (Henrion,
1988): sample as before, and as soon as a sampled observed node disagrees with its observed
value, discard the whole sample and start again. The kept samples come from the posterior,
since this is joint sampling followed by conditioning, but the acceptance rate is the
probability of the evidence, which shrinks fast as more nodes are observed.

**Likelihood weighted sampling** (Fung and Chang, 1990; Shachter and Peot, 1990) clamps each
observed node to its value instead of sampling it, and gives the sample the weight

$$
r(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{\mathbf{z}_i \notin \mathbf{e}} \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i))}{p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i))} \prod_{\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbf{e}} \frac{p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i))}{1} = \prod_{\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbf{e}} p(\mathbf{z}_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i)). \tag{14.48}
$$

This is importance sampling: the target is the joint with the evidence fixed, and the proposal
samples the unobserved nodes from their conditionals and sets observed nodes deterministically,
so the ratio keeps only the evidence factors (Exercise 14.15).

**Worked example.** The classic sprinkler network: Cloudy ($C$) is a parent of Sprinkler ($S$)
and Rain ($R$), which are both parents of WetGrass ($W$), with $P(C) = 0.5$; $P(S \mid C) = 0.1$,
$P(S \mid \lnot C) = 0.5$; $P(R \mid C) = 0.8$, $P(R \mid \lnot C) = 0.2$; and $P(W \mid S, R) =
0.99$, $P(W \mid S, \lnot R) = P(W \mid \lnot S, R) = 0.9$, $P(W \mid \lnot S, \lnot R) = 0$.
Enumerating the 16 states gives $P(W = 1) = 0.6471$ and $P(R = 1 \mid W = 1) = 0.7079$.

The first sample with the figure's seed draws uniforms $0.288, 0.076, 0.580, 0.941$: $C = 1$
($0.288 < 0.5$), then $S = 1$ ($0.076 < P(S \mid C) = 0.1$), $R = 1$ ($0.580 < 0.8$), and $W = 1$
($0.941 < 0.99$). Logic sampling keeps it, since $W$ agrees with the evidence. Likelihood
weighting never draws $W$; it gives the sample weight $P(W = 1 \mid S, R) = 0.99$. After 5000
samples, logic sampling has kept 3276 (65.5%, close to $P(W = 1)$) and estimates $0.717$;
likelihood weighting uses all 5000 with total weight $3263.6$ and estimates $0.711$. A sample with
$S = R = 0$ gets weight $0$, the importance-sampling version of a rejection.

## Langevin sampling

Metropolis–Hastings proposes moves in every direction and lets the acceptance test sort them out,
which is a random walk. Training a network taught us a better habit: **follow the gradient**.
MCMC methods that use $\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \ln p(\mathbf{z})$ move preferentially toward high
probability. **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (Duane et al., 1987) does this and keeps a Metropolis
test; the book focuses instead on **Langevin sampling**, which drops the test and is widely used
in deep learning, especially for energy-based models.

### Energy-based models

A generative model $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})$ must integrate to one,

$$
\int p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} = 1, \tag{14.49}
$$

which restricts what a network can output.<Sidenote>The book prints (14.49) as $\int
p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\, p(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} = 1$; the extra
$p(\mathbf{x})$ is a typo.</Sidenote> An **energy-based model** (LeCun et al., 2006) drops the
constraint. Any real-valued **energy function** $E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})$, typically a network
with scalar output, defines

$$
p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{w})} \exp\{-E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\}, \tag{14.50}
$$

where low energy means high probability and the **partition function**

$$
Z(\mathbf{w}) = \int \exp\{-E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\}\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} \tag{14.51}
$$

makes it normalized (Exercise 14.16: integrate both sides of (14.50)). Every normalized model is an
energy-based model with $E = -\ln p$, but the term is used when $Z$ is intractable. For a data set
$\mathcal{D} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N)$ of independent points, the log-likelihood is

$$
\ln p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathbf{w}) = -\sum_{n=1}^{N} E(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{w}) - N \ln Z(\mathbf{w}), \tag{14.52}
$$

(take the log of (14.50) and sum over $n$; Exercise 14.17), and $Z(\mathbf{w})$ is exactly the
integral we cannot compute.

### The likelihood gradient

For one data point the gradient with respect to the parameters is

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}) = -\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) - \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ln Z(\mathbf{w}). \tag{14.53}
$$

Averaging over data from the unknown data distribution $p_{\mathcal{D}}$, and noting that the last
term does not depend on $\mathbf{x}$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\big] = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big] - \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ln Z(\mathbf{w}). \tag{14.54}
$$

Differentiate (14.51): $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} Z = -\int \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E\, e^{-E}\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}$,
and dividing by $Z$ turns $e^{-E}/Z$ into the model density:

$$
-\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ln Z(\mathbf{w}) = \int \{\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\}\, p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}, \tag{14.55}
$$

an expectation under the **model distribution** $p_{\mathcal{M}}$:

$$
\int \{\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\}\, p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{M}}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big]. \tag{14.56}
$$

Putting (14.54)–(14.56) together (Exercise 14.18):

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\big] = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x})}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big]. \tag{14.57}
$$

Read it as two forces on the energy surface (Figure 14.13). Gradient ascent with the first term
**lowers the energy at data points**, raising their probability. The second term **raises the
energy at points the model itself generates**, lowering their probability. Where the model puts
more mass than the data, the second force wins and probability drains away; where the data are
denser, the first wins. At the maximum the two expectations are equal.

The first term is an average over the training set,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big] \simeq \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{w}), \tag{14.58}
$$

with each $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E$ from automatic differentiation. The second needs **samples from the
model**, whose normalizing constant we do not know: an MCMC problem.

### Langevin dynamics

The **score function** is the gradient of the log density *with respect to the data*, not the
parameters:

$$
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}), \tag{14.59}
$$

and for an energy-based model the partition function drops out because it does not depend on
$\mathbf{x}$:

$$
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}) = -\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w}). \tag{14.60}
$$

**Langevin sampling**, or **stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics** (Parisi, 1981; Welling and Teh,
2011), starts from $\mathbf{x}^{(0)}$ drawn from some simple prior and iterates

$$
\mathbf{x}^{(\tau+1)} = \mathbf{x}^{(\tau)} + \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \ln p(\mathbf{x}^{(\tau)}, \mathbf{w}) + \sqrt{2\eta}\, \boldsymbol\epsilon^{(\tau)}, \qquad \tau \in 1, \dots, T, \tag{14.61}
$$

with $\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(\tau)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ independent and step size
$\eta$: a gradient-ascent step on $\ln p$ plus Gaussian noise. The gradient pulls toward high
density; the noise keeps the chain from collapsing onto the mode. The factor $\sqrt{2\eta}$ is
what balances the two so that, as $\eta \to 0$ and $T \to \infty$, $\mathbf{x}^{(T)}$ is a sample
from $p$.<Sidenote>The book's sentence after (14.61) says "the value of $\mathbf{z}^{(T)}$"; it means
$\mathbf{x}^{(T)}$.</Sidenote> Algorithm 14.4:

```python
def langevin(score, x0, eta, T, rng):
    x = np.array(x0, float)
    for _ in range(T):
        x = x + eta * score(x) + np.sqrt(2 * eta) * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
    return x
```

**There is no acceptance test, so a finite $\eta$ samples a slightly wrong distribution.** For a
Gaussian we can compute how wrong. Along a principal axis with variance $s^2$ the score is
$-x/s^2$, so (14.61) is $x' = (1 - \eta/s^2)x + \sqrt{2\eta}\,\epsilon$. Its stationary variance
$v$ solves $v = (1 - \eta/s^2)^2 v + 2\eta$, which gives

$$
v = \frac{s^2}{1 - \eta/(2s^2)},
$$

too wide by a factor that grows with $\eta/s^2$, and the chain diverges if $\eta \ge 2s^2$. On our
Gaussian the short axis has $s = 0.2$, so the limit is $\eta < 0.08$:

| step size $\eta$ | 0.001 | 0.01 | 0.04 | 0.07 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| short-axis sd, formula (true 0.200) | 0.201 | 0.214 | 0.283 | 0.566 |
| short-axis sd, 100,000 steps | 0.203 | 0.214 | 0.283 | 0.565 |
| $\tau_{\text{int}}$ along the long axis (steps) | 792 | 130 | 32 | 17 |

SciPy's discrete Lyapunov solver for the full 2D chain gives the same standard deviations. Small
$\eta$ is accurate but slow; large $\eta$ is fast but biased. Adding a Metropolis–Hastings test to
each Langevin step removes the bias (the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm), at the cost of
some rejections.

### Training an energy-based model

With (14.61) the model term of (14.57) becomes an average over $M$ Langevin samples
$\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_M$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x})}\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{w})\big] \simeq \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{x}_m, \mathbf{w}). \tag{14.62}
$$

Running a long chain from scratch for every gradient step is expensive. **Contrastive
divergence** (Hinton, 2002) starts each chain at a training point and runs only a few steps. The
samples stay close to the data, so the gradient shapes the energy only near the data manifold;
that can be enough for discrimination but tends to give a poor generative model. A common
alternative, not in the book, is **persistent** chains: keep the model samples from one training
step to the next and run a few more Langevin steps each time, so the chains track the slowly
changing model.

**Worked example.** A one-dimensional energy-based model with a quartic energy

$$
E(x, \mathbf{w}) = w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + w_3 x^3 + w_4 x^4, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E = (x, x^2, x^3, x^4),
$$

trained on 200 points from an equal mixture of $\mathcal{N}(-1.5, 0.5^2)$ and $\mathcal{N}(1.5,
0.5^2)$. Because $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E$ is the feature vector, (14.57) says the gradient is zero
exactly when **the model's average of $x, x^2, x^3, x^4$ equals the data's**: maximum likelihood
here is moment matching. The data averages are $(0.179, 2.525, 0.669, 8.742)$.

- Start at $\mathbf{w} = (0, 0.5, 0, 0.01)$, nearly a Gaussian with standard deviation 1. Its
  moments are $(0, 0.907, 0, 2.337)$ and its mean log-likelihood is $-2.243$.
- Each iteration moves 300 persistent particles by 20 Langevin steps with $\eta = 0.01$, then
  updates $w_k \leftarrow w_k + 0.3\,(\bar\phi_k^{\text{model}} - \bar\phi_k^{\text{data}}) /
  \overline{\phi_k^2}^{\text{data}}$, where $\phi = (x, x^2, x^3, x^4)$ and the denominator, the
  data's average of $\phi_k^2$, only rescales each coordinate's step.<Sidenote>The data averages of
  $\phi_k^2 = x^2, x^4, x^6, x^8$ are $2.5, 8.7, 36.8, 179.5$. Without the rescaling, the $x^4$
  coordinate would need a learning rate about 70 times smaller than the $x$ coordinate. The scaling changes the path, not the
  fixed point, which is still where the two averages of (14.57) agree.</Sidenote>
- The exact maximum, found by the same update with model moments computed on a grid, is
  $\mathbf{w} = (-0.035, -1.237, -0.011, 0.208)$ with mean log-likelihood $-1.4615$. SciPy's
  optimiser on the exact likelihood finds the same $\mathbf{w}$ and $-1.4614$. The negative $w_2$
  and positive $w_4$ make a double well, with minima near $\pm 1.7$.
- The Langevin-trained model reaches log-likelihood $-1.514$ after 100 iterations, $-1.474$ after
  200 and $-1.466$ at iteration 400, with $\mathbf{w} = (-0.052, -1.134, -0.008, 0.199)$. Afterwards it
  hovers near the optimum, jittering with the sampling noise in (14.62).
- Contrastive divergence (chains restarted at the data, 10 steps) reaches only $-1.585$ after
  600 iterations, with $w_1$ drifting to $0.33$: ten steps of size $0.01$ move a sample only about
  $\sqrt{2 \cdot 0.1} \approx 0.45$, so the model never "sees" the mass it puts away from the data.

The same machinery reappears in diffusion models, which learn the score (14.59) directly by
**score matching** instead of through an energy, and then generate with Langevin-like updates.

## Summary

- **Monte Carlo** replaces an expectation by an average of samples; the error falls as
  $1/\sqrt{L}$ in any dimension, scaled by the variance of $f$ and inflated when samples are
  correlated.
- **Exact samplers** (inverse CDF, Box–Muller, Cholesky, rejection) work for simple or
  low-dimensional targets. Rejection and importance sampling depend on a global proposal close to
  $p$, and their efficiency decays exponentially with dimension; importance sampling can fail
  silently.
- **MCMC** needs only local moves and the unnormalized $\tilde p$. Detailed balance makes $p$
  invariant, ergodicity makes the chain reach it; Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs both satisfy
  detailed balance, and Gibbs is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance 1.
- **Random-walk behaviour** is the main cost of MCMC: distance grows as $\sqrt\tau$, so mixing
  time grows with the squared ratio of the target's length scales. Tune proposals for
  effective samples per step, not acceptance rate.
- **Langevin sampling** uses the score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\ln p = -\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}E$ to move
  toward high probability, which makes it the sampler of choice for **energy-based models**,
  whose likelihood gradient (14.57) is "data average minus model average" of $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}E$.
