## Background

A language model learns to predict the next token. That objective teaches grammar, facts and
patterns, but it does not directly teach the model to be helpful, follow an instruction or solve a
math problem correctly. Those goals are easier to judge at the level of a complete answer than at
the level of each next token.

**Reinforcement learning** (RL) is a framework for learning actions from consequences. In language
model post-training, the model is the learner, generated tokens are its actions, and a reward says
how good the finished response was. This matters because a reward can express goals that are hard
to demonstrate token by token: correctness, safety, style or a person's preference.

This post builds the common core first: states, actions, rewards, policy gradients and value
baselines. It then follows two branches. The online branch covers reinforcement learning from human
feedback (RLHF), proximal policy optimization (PPO), group relative policy optimization (GRPO) and
related methods. The offline branch derives direct preference optimization (DPO), explains the
ambiguous name DRPO, and places newer preference losses on the same map.

## The reinforcement-learning problem

An **agent** observes a **state** $s_t$, chooses an **action** $a_t$, receives a scalar **reward**
$r_t$, and moves to the next state. The **environment** is everything that determines the reward
and transition. A **policy** $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is the agent's probability distribution over
actions, parameterized by $\theta$.

The agent wants a large **return**, the discounted sum of future rewards:

$$
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t} \gamma^k r_{t+k}, \qquad 0 \leq \gamma \leq 1
$$

Read the symbols from left to right:

- $t$ is the current time step, and $T$ is the final step.
- $r_{t+k}$ is the reward received $k$ steps into the future.
- $\gamma^k$ discounts that future reward. If $\gamma=1$, a reward counts fully no matter when it
  arrives. If $\gamma=0.9$, a reward two steps away is multiplied by $0.9^2=0.81$.
- The summation adds the discounted rewards from now until the episode ends.

For example, if the next three rewards are $[2,0,5]$ and $\gamma=0.9$, then
$G_t=2+0.9(0)+0.9^2(5)=6.05$. The agent does not merely chase the immediate reward of 2; it also
gets credit for the later reward of 5.

The discount $\gamma$ says how much later rewards count. In language-model alignment, an episode is
usually one response. State $s_t$ is the prompt plus tokens generated so far, action $a_t$ is the
next token, and the main reward often arrives only after the final token. With $\gamma=1$, every
token in that response shares the same terminal return.

### Values answer “what happens from here?”

The **state value** $V^\pi(s)$ is the expected return after reaching $s$ and following policy
$\pi$. The **action value** $Q^\pi(s,a)$ also fixes the next action. Their difference is the
**advantage**:

$$
A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)
$$

$Q^\pi(s,a)$ asks, “what return should I expect if I take action $a$ here?” $V^\pi(s)$ asks,
“what return do I normally expect from this state?” Therefore $A^\pi(s,a)>0$ means the action was
better than the policy's usual choice, while $A^\pi(s,a)<0$ means it was worse. If $Q=8$ and
$V=6$, the advantage is $+2$.

Advantage measures whether an action was better or worse than the policy normally does from that
state. Subtracting the baseline does not change the expected policy gradient, but it reduces its
variance: “reward 8” is hard to interpret, while “2 better than the expected 6” gives a useful
direction.

### Three classical families

| Family | What it learns | How it chooses | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Value-based | $Q(s,a)$ | choose the action with highest value | Q-learning, DQN |
| Policy-based | $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ | sample directly from the policy | REINFORCE, PPO |
| Actor–critic | policy and value baseline | actor acts; critic estimates advantage | A2C, A3C, PPO |

Q-learning works well when the action set is manageable. A language model may choose among tens of
thousands of tokens at every step, so alignment methods usually optimize its policy directly.

## Policy gradients: learning from sampled answers

The **policy-gradient theorem** gives a practical estimator of how parameters affect expected
return. REINFORCE, the simplest policy-gradient algorithm, minimizes

$$
L_{\text{PG}}(\theta) =
-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}
\left[\sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,A_t\right].
$$

Here $\theta$ denotes all trainable model parameters. A **trajectory** $\tau$ is one sampled
sequence of states, actions and rewards. The expectation says to average over many trajectories
sampled from the current policy. Inside it, $\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ is the log-probability of
the action the model actually took, and $A_t$ grades that action relative to a baseline.

The leading minus sign appears because training software normally *minimizes* a loss. For a positive
$A_t$, minimizing the loss raises the action's log-probability. For a negative $A_t$, it lowers the
log-probability. The logarithm is useful because a response probability is a product of token
probabilities, while its log-probability is their numerically manageable sum.

Gradient descent raises the log-probability of actions with positive advantage and lowers it for
negative advantage. The multiplication is the key: a likely action receives no push when
$A_t=0$, while an unexpectedly good action receives a large positive push.

Consider a two-action bandit with logits $z=[0,0]$, hence probabilities $[0.5,0.5]$. Action A is
sampled and earns reward 1; the baseline is 0.4, so $A=0.6$. For a softmax policy,
$\nabla_z\log\pi(A)=[1-0.5,-0.5]=[0.5,-0.5]$. The ascent direction is therefore
$0.6[0.5,-0.5]=[0.3,-0.3]$: increase A's logit and decrease B's.

One sample is noisy. REINFORCE can mistake luck for skill, which motivates value baselines,
multiple samples and conservative updates.

## RLHF turns preferences into a reward

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) replaces a hand-written reward with a model
learned from human comparisons. The influential InstructGPT pipeline has three stages:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning** (SFT) trains a pretrained model on good demonstrations.
2. A **reward model** learns from pairs or rankings of candidate answers. For a chosen answer
   $y_w$ and rejected answer $y_l$, a common Bradley–Terry loss is

   $$
   L_{\text{RM}}=-\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big).
   $$

   The reward model with parameters $\phi$ assigns the scores $r_\phi(x,y_w)$ and
   $r_\phi(x,y_l)$. The subscripts $w$ and $l$ mean *winner* and *loser*. Their difference is passed
   through the sigmoid $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$, turning any real-valued score gap into a probability
   between 0 and 1. If the chosen response scores much higher, $\sigma$ is near 1 and the negative
   log loss is near 0. If the ordering is wrong, the loss is large.

3. The policy samples new responses and PPO maximizes the learned reward while penalizing movement
   away from a frozen reference model.

The regularized objective is commonly written

$$
\max_\pi\;\mathbb{E}_{x, y\sim\pi}[r_\phi(x,y)]
-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
$$

The first term is the average learned reward for prompt $x$ and response $y$ sampled from policy
$\pi$. The second term measures the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the trainable policy
and frozen reference policy. KL divergence is zero when the distributions match and grows as they
separate. The coefficient $\beta$ sets the trade-off: a large $\beta$ keeps the model close to the
reference; a small $\beta$ gives reward maximization more freedom. The $\max_\pi$ means that the
whole expression, reward minus drift penalty, is what training tries to increase.

The KL term is a guardrail. Without it, the policy can exploit errors in the learned reward rather
than improve in the way humans intended. This is **reward hacking**: maximizing the score while
missing the underlying goal.

## PPO: make a useful update, but not a reckless one

**Proximal policy optimization** (PPO) reuses sampled trajectories for several optimization steps
without letting the new policy move too far from the policy that collected them. Define the
probability ratio

$$
r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
$$

The denominator is the probability assigned when the trajectory was collected. The numerator is
the probability under the policy being updated. Thus $r_t=1$ means no change, $r_t=1.2$ means the
action is now 20% more likely, and $r_t=0.8$ means it is 20% less likely. A ratio lets PPO reuse old
samples while correcting for the fact that the policy has already changed.

The clipped surrogate objective is

$$
L^{\text{clip}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_t\left[
\min\left(r_t A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\right)
\right].
$$

There are two candidate contributions for every sampled action. The first, $r_tA_t$, is the ordinary
importance-weighted policy-gradient term. The second replaces $r_t$ with a value clipped to
$[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$. PPO takes the smaller contribution. For a good action this caps the
reward for making it much more likely; for a bad action it caps the reward for making it much less
likely. The expectation averages these per-token terms across the batch, and training maximizes the
result.<Sidenote>Implementations usually minimize the negative of this objective and add value-loss
and entropy terms. The displayed expression isolates PPO's defining clipped policy term.</Sidenote>

With $\epsilon=0.2$, suppose a good token has $A_t=2$ and its ratio rises to $1.3$. The unclipped
term is $1.3\times2=2.6$, but the clipped term is $1.2\times2=2.4$, so the objective uses $2.4$.
There is no extra benefit for pushing its probability beyond the 20% range on this batch.

The sign matters. If a bad token has $A_t=-2$ and its ratio falls to $0.7$, the two terms are
$-1.4$ and $0.8\times(-2)=-1.6$. The minimum is $-1.6$, so PPO also removes the incentive to drive
the ratio farther below $0.8$. Clipping is not a hard constraint on every probability; it flattens
the training incentive beyond the interval.

PPO-based RLHF commonly keeps four logical models: the trainable policy, frozen reference policy,
trainable value model (the **critic**) and frozen reward model. Some can share parameters, but the
memory, inference and synchronization cost remains substantial.

## DPO: solve the regularized problem through comparisons

**Direct preference optimization** (DPO) starts from the same KL-regularized objective but avoids
training an explicit reward model and avoids an online RL loop. At the optimum, reward and policy
are related by

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+C(x),
$$

This equation says that an optimal policy makes high-reward responses more likely than the
reference. The ratio compares the optimal response probability $\pi^*(y\mid x)$ with the reference
probability. Its logarithm is positive when the optimal policy upweights the response and negative
when it downweights it. $\beta$ converts that log-ratio into reward units. $C(x)$ is a
prompt-dependent normalizing constant; crucially, it does not depend on which response $y$ is being
compared.

where $C(x)$ is the same for all answers to prompt $x$. Substitute this expression into the
Bradley–Terry preference model. The unknown $C(x)$ cancels between the chosen and rejected answers,
leaving the DPO loss:

$$
L_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left[
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\right]\right)\right].
$$

The inner bracket is a **relative margin**. Its first log-ratio measures how much the trainable
policy has upweighted the chosen answer relative to the reference. Its second measures the same for
the rejected answer, and is subtracted. A positive bracket therefore means the policy favors the
winner more strongly than the reference did. The sigmoid turns the scaled margin into a predicted
preference probability, and the negative log penalizes disagreement with the recorded label.

Both $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ refer to a whole response. For an
autoregressive model, the whole-response log-probability is the sum of token log-probabilities:

$$
\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$

Here $y_t$ is token $t$, $y_{<t}$ is the response prefix before it, and $|y|$ is the response
length. This sum also explains a practical complication: longer answers accumulate more log terms,
so sequence length can affect preference losses.

### A numerical DPO update

Suppose the reference assigns probabilities $0.20$ and $0.10$ to the complete chosen and rejected
answers. The current policy assigns $0.24$ and $0.06$. With $\beta=0.1$, the margin inside the
sigmoid is

$$
0.1\left[\log(0.24/0.20)-\log(0.06/0.10)\right]
=0.0693.
$$

The implied probability of the recorded preference is $\sigma(0.0693)=0.5173$, and the loss is
$-\log(0.5173)=0.6591$. Training pushes the chosen answer's policy-to-reference ratio up relative
to the rejected answer's ratio.

DPO's simplicity has a price. Its data came from an older **behavior policy**, but training changes
the current policy. The fixed dataset may stop representing mistakes the new model actually makes.
DPO also inherits assumptions from the Bradley–Terry preference model and the reference-policy
derivation. “No explicit RL loop” does not mean “no RL ideas”: DPO is a closed-form route to a
particular regularized RL objective.

## GRPO: compare several answers to the same prompt

**Group relative policy optimization** (GRPO), introduced in DeepSeekMath, removes PPO's learned
critic. For each prompt it samples a group of $G$ responses, scores them, and builds an advantage
from the group's mean and standard deviation:

$$
A_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)}
{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\ldots,r_G)}.
$$

The numerator centers response $i$'s reward around the average for the same prompt. The denominator
rescales by the group's reward spread. This makes the advantages dimensionless and comparable
across prompts whose reward scales differ. It also makes their group mean zero: above-average
responses are positive and below-average responses are negative.

Take four rewards $[1,0,1,0.5]$. Their mean is $0.625$ and population standard deviation is
$0.4146$, giving advantages $[0.9045,-1.5076,0.9045,-0.3015]$. Check the fourth response:
$(0.5-0.625)/0.4146=-0.3015$. It receives a negative update even though its raw reward is positive,
because it was worse than the alternatives for that prompt.

GRPO then uses a PPO-like clipped ratio and a KL penalty. It saves the critic's memory and training
cost, but it is not free: the group requires several completions per prompt. A group with identical
rewards also has zero standard deviation, so implementations need an epsilon or must skip that
uninformative group.<Sidenote>The precise GRPO loss and KL estimator vary across papers and
implementations. “Critic-free group baseline” is the stable defining idea; implementation details
must be compared explicitly.</Sidenote>

GRPO is especially natural for **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards** (RLVR). A math
checker, unit test or compiler can provide an objective terminal score. For open-ended helpfulness,
the reward still usually comes from a learned judge, and its biases remain part of the training
signal.

## What does DRPO mean?

Unlike PPO, DPO and GRPO, **DRPO is overloaded**. A paper or codebase must define it before the
acronym is useful. Recent papers use it for at least Direct Ranking Preference Optimization,
Dataset Reset Policy Optimization, Decoupled Reward Policy Optimization, and Doubly Robust
Preference Optimization.

This post uses **doubly robust preference optimization**, from *Doubly Robust Alignment for Large
Language Models*. “Doubly robust” has a specific statistical meaning: the estimator remains
consistent if either the preference model or the reference policy is correctly specified, without
requiring both to be correct. It targets a weakness shared by many neat derivations: real human
preferences may not follow the assumed comparison model, and a chosen reference may not represent
the required distribution.

DRPO is therefore not “DPO but newer” in a generic sense. It is a particular robustness proposal,
and its extra estimation machinery only pays off when model misspecification is a serious concern.
When another source says DRPO, check its expanded name first.

## Other algorithms on the same map

Most modern variants change one of four things: where data comes from, how a baseline is estimated,
how far the policy may move, or what preference model turns judgments into a loss.

| Method | Online? | Critic? | Reference? | Main idea |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: | --- |
| REINFORCE | yes | no | optional | multiply sampled log-probabilities by return |
| RLOO | yes | no | usually | use other responses' mean reward as a leave-one-out baseline |
| PPO | yes | yes | usually | clip probability-ratio incentives |
| GRPO | yes | no | usually | normalize rewards within a response group |
| DPO | usually no | no | yes | logistic loss on policy/reference preference margins |
| IPO | usually no | no | yes | avoid committing to Bradley–Terry reward fitting |
| KTO | no | no | yes | learn from individually desirable or undesirable examples |
| SimPO | no | no | no | use length-normalized policy log-probability and a target margin |
| ORPO | no | no | no | combine SFT with an odds-ratio penalty in one stage |

**REINFORCE leave-one-out** (RLOO) samples several answers and, for each answer, uses the average
reward of the *other* answers as its baseline. It is close in spirit to GRPO, but the exact
advantage, normalization and clipped objective differ.

**Kahneman–Tversky optimization** (KTO) accepts unpaired good/bad labels, useful when collecting a
clean chosen/rejected pair is difficult. **Simple preference optimization** (SimPO) removes the
frozen reference and uses average sequence log-probability to reduce length bias. **Odds ratio
preference optimization** (ORPO) folds preference learning into supervised fine-tuning.

These names do not form a ladder where each later method dominates the previous one. They encode
different assumptions about available labels, online sampling budget, memory and failure modes.

## Choosing a method

Start from the feedback you can trust, not from the fashionable optimizer.

- Use **PPO** when you need online exploration, have a meaningful learned reward, and can afford a
  critic and a careful distributed training loop.
- Use **GRPO or RLOO** when online sampling is useful but a critic is too expensive, especially when
  a verifier can score several answers to the same prompt.
- Use **DPO** when you already have high-quality preference pairs and want a stable, simple offline
  baseline.
- Consider **KTO** for unpaired binary feedback and **SimPO or ORPO** when reference-model memory is
  the binding constraint.
- Consider a specific **DRPO** only after identifying its full name and the misspecification or
  ranking problem it was designed to solve.

Whatever the optimizer, evaluate the reward and the policy separately. Hold out prompts, use human
judgments where possible, slice results by response length and task, and watch both KL divergence
and reward. A rising training reward can mean genuine improvement, reward hacking, a verbosity
bias, or distribution shift; the scalar alone cannot tell you which.

## Summary

- RL raises the probability of actions with positive advantage and lowers it for negative
  advantage; baselines make that estimate less noisy.
- RLHF is a feedback pipeline, not one optimizer. Its classic form learns a reward model and uses
  PPO with KL regularization.
- PPO clips the incentive for large policy-ratio changes; DPO turns the same regularized objective
  into an offline classification loss under specific assumptions.
- GRPO replaces PPO's learned critic with relative rewards from several responses to one prompt.
- DRPO is an ambiguous acronym. Always expand it; doubly robust preference optimization is one
  recent method designed for preference-model or reference-policy misspecification.
